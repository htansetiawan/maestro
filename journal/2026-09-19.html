From data to a labelled dataset: the David Foster corpus

The ACE experiment gets its corpus: 28 David Foster songs from two albums and a YouTube compilation, cut as whole songs, with captions drafted from the audio and confirmed by ear in a private web workbench.

This week the ACE-Step experiment got its data. The target is the sound of David Foster: sixteen Grammy awards, decades of chart records as composer, arranger and producer for Celine Dion, All-4-One, Babyface, James Ingram and Olivia Newton-John, and a smaller body of work released under his own name. The question the experiment asks is narrow and testable: which of his expressive qualities can a music model learn from audio, and which still need the musician's hand in the studio. To ask it, I first needed a corpus, a way to cut and describe it, and a place to do the describing.

What changed

A corpus exists. Two albums I own, Love Lights the World (1997) and Touch of David Foster (1998, with three tracks tagged River of Love, 1990), came in as 24 MP3 files, catalogued with tags, durations, hashes and duplicates. A fan-uploaded YouTube compilation of Foster duets added five more songs; the platform had muted it from the nineteenth minute onward, so the tool learned to report the audible span and to skip silence. Together that is 28 songs of 3.3 to 5.5 minutes.

Whole songs, not windows. My first cut was fixed 90-second windows, 89 clips with orphan tails of 25 seconds. That teaches a model the wrong thing about how pieces begin and end, and it triples the labeling. The training unit is now the complete song. The pipeline's duration cap moved to 360 seconds, and a compilation is split into songs at its quiet gaps so the held-out split separates songs, never halves of one.

Captions are drafted by analysis, confirmed by ear. Writing a description of a five-minute arrangement from scratch is slow and inconsistent. An autolabel stage now estimates tempo, key, a coarse section map with loudness, and instrument presence over time, then writes a caption from a fixed template and a fixed vocabulary. Every draft is marked as a draft until I have listened and saved it; the training gate refuses unconfirmed rows.

A workbench to do the listening. A small web app serves each song with a timeline of sections and instrument entrances under the player, the draft caption prefilled, tap tempo, key and instrument controls, and a train-or-reference switch. It runs on the GPU box behind a token and a Cloudflare tunnel at maestro.enrica.ai. The page is private; the link preview is not.

Current thinking

The model this pipeline trains is instrumental. Half of the corpus has lead vocals, and the preprocessing tags every clip as instrumental with no lyrics, so training on sung tracks would teach that "instrumental" means voices appear. Those 14 songs stay in the manifest as reference material for listening comparisons, not as training rows. That leaves 14 instrumental songs, most of them the Love Lights the World album, which is where Foster the pianist is most audible anyway.

Rights are handled by recording, not by pretending. Every clip carries its source, offsets and a rights: unverified flag, and training on such rows requires an explicit opt-in in the frozen config, which I have set. This is research on a personal machine; no adapter trained on this material will be published or shared.

Consistent vocabulary matters more than eloquent captions. The words in the drafts, piano, strings, drums, bass, synthesizer, brass and so on, are the words I will prompt with later. The template guarantees that the same evidence produces the same words, and the workbench nudges me to stay inside that list.

Findings and evidence

  • The muted compilation is a small lesson in provenance: both the Opus and AAC streams were digital silence after 1,143 seconds of a 3,042-second file. The intake now records the audible span and would have flagged it before any cutting.
  • Track splitting on level gaps found five songs in the compilation's audible part, at 221, 474, 681 and 884 seconds, with lengths of 3.3 to 4.3 minutes. This is measured on one source; crossfaded compilations will not split this cleanly.
  • The auto-labeler's tempo and dynamics arc were plausible on the two songs I checked, and it flagged vocals in exactly the songs I expected. Instrument identification is its weak spot: zero-shot CLAP hears string pads as organ, synthesizer or flute. Trimming the vocabulary and requiring a tag to persist through a quarter of the song removed most of that. This is an impression from a few drafts, not a measured accuracy.
  • Nothing about ACE itself was tested this week. No checkpoint has been loaded and no GPU run has happened. The pipeline's ACE-facing stages remain verified only by the September 12 synthetic smoke.

Artifacts completed

The experiment README documents the new stages: fetch, import, segment (with --tracks), caption, pending, autolabel and web. The dated results record describes the first source, the muted tail, the whole-song decision and the recorded rights opt-in. A user service file and a one-shot setup script for the workbench live under the experiment's deploy/ folder. The album catalogue and all audio stay on my machine.

Open questions

  • Which qualities survive the piano-led instrumental subset? The orchestral entrances and the vocal-arrangement moves live mostly in the songs I have set aside.
  • Will complete songs of up to 5.5 minutes fit in memory for LoRA training on the shared 48 GB card, or do the longest tracks need to be cut after all?
  • How much do the auto-drafted captions bias what I write? I am confirming a machine's description rather than writing my own, and the vocabulary is mine but the first guess is not.
  • Is 14 songs enough for the adapter to change the music rather than memorise it? The September 12 entry guessed 20 to 30 as a pilot size.

Next experiment

Confirm the 28 captions in the workbench, marking the sung tracks as reference, and freeze the split with prepare. Install the pinned ACE-Step 1.5 checkout and base checkpoint, run the real-ACE GPU smoke, then preprocess and train the first SFT adapter on the instrumental songs. Generate the frozen evaluation prompts with the same seeds from the base model and the adapter, and listen blind before drawing any conclusion about what was learned.
