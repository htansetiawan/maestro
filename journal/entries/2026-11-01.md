# Play the line: fitting words to a melody by playing it

I wrote words for my own piano recording of the Color Purple theme and wanted to hear them sung over it, on the melody I have in my head. The hard part was not the singer but the fitting: which syllable goes on which note, for how long, starting where. Four ways of doing it were tried on the same verse. The one that worked is to play the melody on the computer keyboard while the recording plays, tapping the beat with one key. The take comes back engraved, with my words under the notes, and is then sung over the piano.

## Highlights

Four recordings, each with a voice laid on top of the backing by the same chain of open-model steps.

**All the Things You Are.** Bars 13 to 18 of Yuval Cohen's live solo [All the Things You Are](../../artifacts/play-the-line/all-the-things/canvas.html), with a voice laid on top in separate steps, each an open model doing one task that can be swapped: Kong transcribed six minutes of piano in 29 seconds; I played a 37-note line over those bars on a MIDI keyboard; words were fitted one syllable per note (placeholders proposed by the agent, not the song's lyrics); SoulX sang it in my baritone. Running a second recording through the tools found three general faults that one piece had hidden: the bar grid assumed a half-time feel, the score always wrote F major at one tempo, and a take without tapped beats stopped the tool.

**Someday My Prince Will Come.** A jazz waltz, Sangah Noona's solo piano, as a short research excerpt: I played a 49-note line over it on a MIDI keyboard, words were written for that line (not the song's lyrics), and [the canvas](../../artifacts/play-the-line/someday/canvas.html) carries it in 3/4, sung by six voices. The tools now count bars in three as well as four. A melody drafted automatically from the piano transcription matched about two thirds of my notes by pitch class, but an octave above the line I played.

**Biarkanlah.** Kahitna's Indonesian ballad over a karaoke backing, 16 bars as a research excerpt, with Korean words: I played the line on a MIDI keyboard, words about missing someone and letting go were written for it one Hangul syllable per note (not the song's lyrics), and [the canvas](../../artifacts/play-the-line/biarkanlah/canvas.html) has it sung by four voices. The singing model knows English sounds only, so the Korean is sung through the nearest English sounds and carries an accent.

**Theme from The Color Purple.** My own recording and my own words, one verse of 15 bars: I played the line on keys over the recording while tapping the beat, the [score canvas](../../artifacts/play-the-line/canvas.html) showed it engraved with the words and the sounds sent for each syllable, and the verse was sung in my baritone, with "Footsteps" pronounced the way I chose. The sections below are that verse in detail.

## Four ways to put words on a melody

| Way | What happened |
|---|---|
| Drag each syllable onto a timeline | Possible, but slow, and the result was messy |
| Transcribe a reference singer automatically | Fast, but it began partway into the song instead of from bar 1, and its notes still needed correcting |
| Type the melody as short text, like `z2 Bb4/ Bb4 C5 D5/` | Exact, but counting beats while typing was tiring and easy to get wrong |
| Play it on keys over the recording, tapping the beat | Kept: pitch and rhythm come in one pass, the way a pianist thinks |

Typing is still useful for small fixes in words: "hold *earth* for four beats", "start *Footsteps* half a beat later". Anything bigger goes back to the keyboard.

## What the score has to show

The score turned out to be the place where the fitting is checked and discussed, so what is on it matters. These were added because they were missing when needed, and removed because they got in the way:

- **Bar numbers counted from the first bar of the recording**, from my own taps rather than a fixed tempo, so "bar 18" means the same thing to me and to the tool.
- **Words under the notes, with hyphens**, and under each word the note's place in its line, so a fix can be named as "line 2, note 5".
- **Sharps and flats written for the key.** In F major only B flat is marked; an earlier version put a natural sign on every note.
- **No clock times on the staff.** Start times in seconds moved to a list beside the score; on the staff they crowded the bar numbers.
- **No chord names, for now.** Chords read from the piano part were wrong in places and collided with the bar numbers; they come back when they can be checked.
- **The beat the music starts on.** Each line of this verse starts on the second beat of its bar, after a beat of rest, and the score shows it that way.
- **The sounds under each syllable**, as the singer receives them (*Mor*: M AO R, *ning*: N IH NG), so a mispronounced note can be traced to its cause.
- **Colour by where a note came from:** played by me, split or held by the tool, or still without words (grey).

## What the tool measures and fixes

On the verse shown (four lines, 38 notes, bars 10 to 24):

- **Beat.** 88 taps, two dropped as double taps, median 0.689 seconds between taps. The bars follow these taps.
- **Lead.** I play 33 to 46 milliseconds ahead of my own taps (median per take). The tool measures this and removes it before snapping notes to quarters of a beat.
- **Double presses.** A key registering twice within 120 ms counts once; in one take that was 18 notes. Two different keys within 80 ms are reported as a fumble rather than kept or dropped silently; one such fumble had swallowed the word *brick*.
- **Fitting.** One syllable too many for the notes: a long note is split into two of the same pitch (*Foot-steps*). Notes left over: a word is held across them. Words are never dropped; notes without words stay grey until they get some. Where the singer struggled, the line itself changed: *the red brick walls* became *to the la-yer of red brick wall*, on F G A A G, *red* carried from A down to G, *brick wall* on F F.
- **Entries.** After singing, each line's first word is measured against the score: *Morning* 89 ms late, *Footsteps* 48 ms late, *Windows* 24 ms early; *A hush* follows straight on from the note before.

Splitting words well matters for the singer, not only for the page. The first version split *across* as *acr-oss* and *footsteps* as *footst-eps*; the singer then came in 0.4 seconds early on *Windows*. With the words split the way they are sung (*a-cross*, *foot-steps*, *win-dows*), the same verse comes in within a tenth of a second on every line.

## The voice

The voice on the page is my own baritone. SoulX-Singer copies its sound from ten seconds of me singing another song of mine, *The Yard*, and sings this verse note by note an octave below the written line. It sings the right notes on the right words at the right time, which is what this week was about; it is not a finished vocal, and it does not phrase the way a singer would. The words also have to be split into sounds the way they are sung: an early version handed the singer *mo* + *r-ning*, and it sang *mo-re-ning*. Better voices are a separate problem from fitting, and the fitting does not change when the voice does.

The page also plays the verse in other voices, labelled as AI voice models made from short references to commercial recordings and not the singers: voice conversion with Seed-VC keeps the notes steadier than singing from the score, and neither method carries a famous singer's technique, only the colour of the voice.
