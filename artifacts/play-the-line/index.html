Play the line

I wrote words for my own piano recording of the Color Purple theme and wanted to hear them sung over it. The fastest way to fit words to the melody turned out to be playing the melody, not typing it. I play the vocal line on the computer keyboard while my recording plays, tap the beat as I go, and the line comes back engraved with my words under the notes and sung over the piano.

Henry Tan · Maestro · November 2026

The first verse

Sung in my own baritone over the piano, from 23 seconds into the recording (46 seconds)

The words are mine:

Morning comes soft across the earth
Footsteps on the paths beneath the tree
Windows waking in
to the layer of red brick wall
A hush that settles like a breeze

How it works

  1. Play the line. The recording plays. I tap the beat and play the melody I want sung, on a MIDI keyboard (keys for the notes, a drum pad for the beat) or on the computer keys. My taps become the beat grid, so the bar numbers follow my playing, not a fixed tempo. The recorder is on its own page; it needs Chrome or Edge for a MIDI keyboard, and it saves a take as a file.
  2. See it on the score. The take comes back engraved: bar numbers, notes, my words under them, sharps and flats written for the key of F. Notes are coloured by where they came from, so I can see at a glance which ones I played and which ones the tool split or held.
  3. Fix small things in words. “Hold earth for four beats.” “Start Footsteps half a beat later.” Bigger changes go back to the keyboard.
  4. Hear it sung. The score goes note by note to a singing-voice model, SoulX-Singer, and the voice is mixed over the piano at the same place.

Four ways to put words on a melody

The same verse was fitted four ways before this one stuck.

WayWhat happened
Drag each syllable onto a timelinePossible, but slow, and the result was messy
Transcribe a reference singer automaticallyFast, but it began partway into the song instead of from bar 1, and its notes still needed correcting
Type the melody as short text, like z2 Bb4/ Bb4 C5 D5/Exact, but counting beats in my head while typing was tiring and easy to get wrong
Play it on keys over the recording, tapping the beatThe one I kept: pitch and rhythm come in one pass, the way a pianist thinks

What the tool does for you

  • It removes your lead. I play about 33 to 46 milliseconds ahead of my own taps. The tool measures that and takes it out before snapping notes to the grid.
  • It cleans up the keyboard. A key pressed twice within 120 ms counts once; in one take that was 18 notes. Two different keys within 80 ms are reported as a fumble instead of being kept silently.
  • It fits the words. When a line has one more syllable than notes, a long note is split into two of the same pitch (Foot-steps). When it has more notes than syllables, a word is held across them. It never drops a word: notes without words stay grey on the score until I give them some. Where the singer struggled, I changed the line itself: the red brick walls became to the la-yer of red brick wall, on F G A A G, red carried from A down to G, then brick wall on F F.
  • It shows the sounds. Under each syllable the canvas lists the sounds the singer is told to pronounce on that note (Mor: M AO R, ning: N IH NG). An early version gave the singer mo + r-ning, and it sang mo-re-ning; now a consonant moves to the next note only if an English syllable can start with it.
  • It checks the entries. After singing, each line's first word is measured against the score. In this verse the voice comes in 89 ms late on Morning, 48 ms late on Footsteps and 24 ms early on Windows; A hush follows straight on from the note before.

What it is not yet

The voice is my own baritone: the model copies its sound from ten seconds of me singing another song of mine, The Yard, and sings this verse an octave below the written line. It follows the score, but it is not a finished vocal: the model does not phrase or breathe the way a person would, and a line with many short words still sounds stiff. The score, the timing and the words are the part this page is about. Only the first verse is fitted so far.
