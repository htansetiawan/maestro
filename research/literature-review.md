## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The paradigm problem

By September 2026 the public face of music AI is a text box. A user types a sentence, waits, and receives a finished stereo master with vocals. Suno's models advanced from v3 to v5.5 in roughly two years, Udio reached commercial scale before settling with two major labels, and Google's Lyria family moved from research demo to a consumer product line and a real-time API [@suno2026; @udio2025; @google2025lyria2]. Open weights followed the same shape: ACE-Step, YuE, DiffRhythm, SongGen and HeartMuLa all take a text prompt and lyrics and return a song [@gong2026acestep15; @yuan2025yue; @ning2025diffrhythm; @liu2025songgen; @heartmula2026].

For a composer this is the wrong shape. A prompt is a very low-bandwidth channel for musical intent, the output arrives as an opaque mixdown with no handle for correction, and the human contribution collapses to prompt authorship and curation. The evidence that professionals experience it this way is now direct. Seventeen producers given MusicGen and Demucs reported "creative misalignment" and pain aligning generated material to their own tempo, key and beat grids, and asked for control and editability above all [@ronchini2025ttmuserstudy]. Six professional composers interviewed for ISMIR 2023 asked for MusicXML and MIDI interoperability, open and explainable models, and preservation of control [@newman2023perceptions]. An eighteen-month study embedded with six professional acts at Sony CSL found that practitioners want to *pull* material rather than be *pushed* it, to prime models on their own audio, and to stay inside the tools they already use [@deruty2022sonycsl]. A four-week ecological study of a variation tool found that transformations of the user's own authored material preserved creative ownership while automatic completion was rejected outright [@krol2025ownership].

The market itself has been drifting toward the composer's shape without adopting its premise. Suno added Covers, Personas and Replace Section, then a DAW-like Studio in September 2025, then MIDI import and editing in August 2026; Udio added inpainting; Google's Music AI Sandbox added region editing and a multitrack view; Stable Audio 2.5 and ElevenLabs Music advertise inpainting; ACE-Step ships repainting, lyric editing and vocal-to-accompaniment [@suno2026; @udio2025; @google2025lyria2; @novack2025arc; @elevenlabs2025music; @gong2026acestep15]. Every one of these is an admission that one-shot generation is insufficient, retrofitted onto an architecture that was not designed for iteration.

This review surveys the space from the opposite starting point. It asks what the literature and the existing systems offer to a studio in which the composer authors the material, marks it up, and asks a machine to **compile** a fuller realization under those marks — then edits, re-annotates, and recompiles. The loop is

> compose → annotate → compile → edit → annotate → compile → …

with editing understood broadly: opening the notation and changing it by hand, scribbling on it, humming a line, attaching an example recording, a picture, a video or a sentence of prose. The word *compile* is deliberate. It implies a source that a human owns, intermediate representations that can be inspected, a deterministic and repeatable build, and a build log that explains what the machine did with each instruction.

### 1.2 Scope, method and corpus

The corpus is 464 annotated entries gathered in September 2026 across eight parallel research clusters, yielding 399 unique bibliographic records. Coverage runs from Hiller and Isaacson's *Illiac Suite* in 1957 to preprints of mid-2026, with emphasis on 2023–2026. The clusters were seeded by a practising composer-engineer's own reading list — Music Transformer and Anna Huang's lineage, the CMU cluster around Chris Donahue's Generative Creativity Lab, Amuse, ExpressEdit, music21 and MIT's computational music theory teaching, Suno and ACE-Step, and a January 2026 CMU result on human versus AI-assisted creativity — and then expanded systematically: symbolic generation and editing; audio generation and controllable audio; HCI and creativity-support research on co-creation; multimodal input, annotation and the music-as-code lineage; infrastructure, datasets, computational music theory and education; and the historical, commercial, evaluative and legal context.

Each entry was recorded with a verification level. Roughly two-thirds were verified against a primary source fetched during the survey — an arXiv abstract page, an ACM Digital Library record, a project page, a repository README, a court docket or an official release note. Entries marked *partial* were confirmed only from secondary pages or search snippets, and a small number marked *unverified* rest on recall alone. Claims drawn from *partial* and *unverified* entries are hedged in the prose that follows, and precise figures are omitted where the source could not be re-read. Three limitations follow from the method. Search budgets were exhausted in several clusters, so older canonical works (Koenig's *Project 1*, Vercoe's Synthetic Performer, GenJam) are recorded at lower confidence than 2025–2026 preprints. Non-English literature, the commercial Japanese and Chinese ecosystems beyond the systems named here, and the substantial NIME and ICMC hardware-instrument literature are under-represented. And the fast-moving product and legal material is a snapshot: it is accurate as of early September 2026 and will decay quickly.

### 1.3 The organizing taxonomy

Existing surveys of music AI are organized by technique, domain or task [@ji2023survey; @le2025nlpsurvey; @ma2024foundation; @kader2025survey]. Those axes are the right ones for a modeller and the wrong ones for a builder, because they do not say where a given contribution sits in a composer's working day. The companion taxonomy therefore uses the loop itself as its trunk — **Compose (S1)**, **Annotate (S2)**, **Compile (S3)**, **Edit (S4)**, with **Play (S5)** as the real-time branch — surrounded by **Foundations (F)** that precede it, a **Substrate (B)** of representations, engines, toolkits and data beneath it, **Context (C)** of products, law and economics around it, and **Evidence and Evaluation (E)** as the meta-layer that decides whether any of it works. A second, orthogonal view codes each work on ten dimensions: output domain, task framing, control granularity, input modalities, initiative and timing, communication direction, editability of the intermediate representation, integration surface, evidence level, and openness and provenance.

Three pieces of vocabulary are borrowed rather than invented. Rowe's 1993 distinctions — score-driven versus performance-driven input, transformative versus generative versus sequenced response, and the instrument versus player paradigm — describe the studio precisely as a score-driven, transformative system in the instrument paradigm [@rowe1993interactive]. Kantosalo and Toivonen's alternating versus task-divided co-creativity names the turn structure [@kantosalo2016modes]. Rezwana and Maher's COFI supplies the two communication directions and, in coding 92 co-creative systems, the finding that the AI-to-human direction is almost entirely undesigned [@rezwana2023cofi].

Sections 2 through 12 follow the taxonomy's order. Section 13 consolidates the gaps into a research agenda.

### 1.4 Five theses this review establishes

**First, infilling under hard constraints — not generation from scratch — is the primitive that implements the loop.** Coconet framed this in 2017 in almost the founder's words, observing that composers write "in a nonlinear fashion, scribbling motifs here and there, often revisiting choices previously made," and modelling it as blocked-Gibbs completion of a partially masked score [@huang2017coconet]. Nine years of work has made it mature: bar-by-track masking in MMM and MIDI-GPT, arbitrary track-measure selection with roughly ten fine-grained controls in Composer's Assistant, anticipation tokens that let any subset of notes be pinned as hard constraints in the Anticipatory Music Transformer, and arbitrary piano-roll masks in Polyffusion and GETMusic [@ens2020mmm; @pasquier2025midigpt; @malandro2024composersassistant2; @thickstun2024anticipatory; @min2023polyffusion; @lv2023getmusic]. The strongest deployment evidence in the entire corpus is Hookpad Aria's: about 74,000 of 318,000 span-level suggestions accepted by roughly 3,000 users inside a lead-sheet editor — about a 23% acceptance rate, reported in full under E3 [@donahue2024hookpadaria].

**Second, interface steerability moves the outcomes that matter to composers at least as much as model capability does.** The only two studies that varied model and interface independently found that steering tools produced the larger gains in control, ownership and self-efficacy, while the more expressive model produced better listener-judged musicality — the two are complementary, not substitutes [@louie2020cococo; @louie2022expressive]. The corollary from Cococo is a design rule: generative power should be "partitioned into smaller, semantically meaningful tools" rather than exposed as one button. MMM-C's single-slider design confirms the negative case, scoring acceptable usability while its users' desire for more control averaged about 9.5 out of 10 [@tchemeube2023mmmc].

**Third, every input modality that works routes through an editable symbolic representation.** Humming becomes MIDI or a lead sheet, drawing becomes notes, an image becomes chords, and in each case the symbolic layer is what gives the human a correction handle [@bittner2022basicpitch; @donahue2022sheetsage; @liang2024drawlody; @kim2025amuse]. Systems that map a loose gesture straight to audio are impressive and uncorrectable [@donahue2023singsong; @tian2025vidmuse]. Amuse is the cleanest demonstration of the alternative: a multimodal model proposes keywords the user can edit, and those keywords drive chord candidates filtered by rejection sampling against a chord prior trained on real music, with significant gains in inspiration support and controllability over baselines [@kim2025amuse].

**Fourth, the annotation layer is the studio's genuine white space.** The pieces exist separately. Score annotation already has standardised, machine-readable, musical-time-anchored formats in MEI, Dezrann's labels, Humdrum and RomanText [@pugin2014verovio; @giraud2018dezrann; @huron1995humdrum; @gotham2019wheninrome]. Analysis models can propose harmonic and structural labels well enough to be corrected rather than trusted, with full-label Roman-numeral accuracy of about 51% for AugmentedNet and 62% for RNBert, and key accuracy of 82–83% [@napoleslopez2021augmentednet; @sailor2024rnbert]. Generation systems have converged on a control vocabulary — instrument, note density, polyphony, pitch range, chord per beat, section label, style — that reads like a list of things a composer would write on a score [@vonrutte2023figaro; @guo2022musiac; @chen2024sympac]. And the interaction pattern for turning free-form marks into machine instructions has been demonstrated in video, where ExpressEdit parses natural language plus frame sketches into temporal, spatial, operation and parameter references, resolves each with a per-modality resolver, and returns inspectable, editable suggestions [@tilekbay2024expressedit]. No system found in this survey combines them for music. Treating a composer's margin notes as a compilable instruction set is the most defensible research contribution available here.

**Fifth, a symbolic-first, human-authored studio sits on materially better legal and ethical ground than the prompt-to-audio alternative.** The Munich Regional Court held in November 2025 that memorisation of lyrics in model weights constitutes reproduction, and extended the reasoning to Suno in a judgment of 31 July 2026 in which the text-and-data-mining exception was found inapplicable [@lgmunich2025gemaopenai; @lgmunich2026gemasuno]. In the United States, the Copyright Office's second report and the *Thaler* line hold that human authorship is required for protection, with certiorari denied in March 2026 — which means human-authored, human-edited notation is protectable where prompt-only output largely is not [@usco2025part2; @thaler2025perlmutter]. Meanwhile licence-clean training material for symbolic models genuinely exists: PDMX offers more than 250,000 public-domain MusicXML scores under CC-BY, and OpenScore's critical editions are CC0 [@long2024pdmx; @gotham2022openscore]. Creator surveys point the same way, with a majority accepting AI that assists the creative process while overwhelming majorities demand consent and disclosure [@apraamcos2024ai].

## 2. Historical lineages (F1–F3)

The studio's central metaphor — a composer authors material and annotations, and an AI *compiles* a fuller realization that the composer then edits — has three ancestries, each contributing something the others lack. Algorithmic composition (F1) supplied generation from rules and probabilistic style models, and later the idea that a neural model can be a primitive inside a tool rather than an autonomous author. Interactive music systems (F2) supplied real-time listening, score following and Rowe's three axes, which this review's taxonomy reuses directly. The commercial arranger and notation lineage (F3) supplied the only mass-market instantiation of "chords plus style yields an arrangement," a 35-year history whose strengths and limits define what a learned, annotation-steerable descendant must improve upon.

### 2.1 F1 Algorithmic composition lineage

Both paradigms a compiler needs were present at the origin. Hiller and Isaacson's *Illiac Suite* (composed 1956–57 on ILLIAC I) is generally credited as the first computer-composed score; its four experiments ran from cantus firmus generation, through four-voice writing under strict counterpoint rules by generate-and-test, to rhythm and dynamics, and finally to Markov-chain generation, with the symbolic output engraved by hand and performed by a string quartet [@hiller1959experimental]. Experiment 2 (hard constraints) and experiment 4 (soft stylistic priors) are exactly the two modes a compile step must combine. Koenig's Project 1 (1964) and Project 2 (1966), as commonly dated, moved toward the composer programming the generator: selection principles and constraints went in, and a structured table of pitch, duration, register, dynamics and entry delays came out for transcription into notation [@koenig1970project]. Xenakis's UPIC (1977) made drawing an executable score — arcs on a tablet with time on one axis and pitch on the other, rendered directly to sound and transformable by stretching, transposition and inversion — a lineage continued by IanniX and UPISketch [@xenakis1992formalized]. The paradigms are surveyed by @nierhaus2009algorithmic.

The recombinant strand culminated in Cope's Experiments in Musical Intelligence (from 1981): roughly 20,000 lines of Lisp segmented a single composer's corpus, matched "signatures," classified segments with the SPEAC scheme and recombined them under grammatical constraints into new works in the style of Bach, Mozart or Chopin [@cope2001virtual]. Hofstadter's touring "Chopin versus EMI" tests found audiences guessing at roughly chance, *Virtual Music* (2001) collected the ensuing debate on whether pastiche is creativity, and Cope deleted the database in 2005 [@cope2001virtual]. The 1994 CD *Bach by Design* was the first commercial recording of EMI output; the same year McIntyre's "Bach in a Box" harmonised a melody in four-part Baroque style with a genetic algorithm and rule-based fitness — recorded in the notes from recollection rather than a fetched source [@mcintyre1994bachinabox]. EMI's legacy is the canonical warning about style pastiche and authorship, and the origin of the "creativity versus perception of creativity" argument later formalised by Colton (section 3.1).

The neural turn began with Eck and Schmidhuber's 2002 LSTM, which learned 12-bar blues progressions and melodies and is generally credited with keeping global form where earlier recurrent networks drifted [@eck2002blues]. Boulanger-Lewandowski et al.'s RNN-RBM (2012) modelled polyphonic piano-rolls and reportedly popularised the JSB Chorales, MuseData, Nottingham and Piano-midi.de benchmarks that anchored evaluation for a decade [@boulangerlewandowski2012modeling]. folk-rnn (2016) trained an LSTM on roughly 23,000 ABC-notation folk tunes, let musicians steer by key, metre and seed, and was evaluated at population and individual levels and by composers *using* the output, which entered sessions, concerts and an album [@sturm2016folkrnn]; the follow-up case study argued that generated tunes are raw material for editing and that likelihood is the wrong yardstick [@sturm2019machinefolk]. Two Magenta models mark the pivot from generation-as-artifact to generation-as-primitive. Coconet (2017) trained an orderless model over four-voice chorales so that any subset of notes could be masked and re-sampled by blocked Gibbs sampling — "an analogue to rewriting," motivated by composers who "write music in a nonlinear fashion, scribbling motifs here and there" [@huang2017coconet]. Music Transformer (2018–19) made relative self-attention linear in memory, generated minute-long performance MIDI with repeated motifs and ABA-like structure, and demonstrated unconditioned generation, motif continuation and melody-to-accompaniment, with listening studies preferring it to PerformanceRNN [@huang2018musictransformer]. What made these the pivot is what was built on them: Coconet became the Bach Doodle, which answered more than 55 million harmonisation queries in three days and released 21.6 million rated melody–harmonisation pairs [@huang2019bachdoodle], and the Cococo steering interface [@louie2020cococo]; MusicVAE's latent space became an interpolation primitive [@roberts2018musicvae] packaged as clip-level DAW devices in Magenta Studio [@roberts2019magentastudio]. From here the model is a component of a composer's tool; descendants are treated under S3.1–S3.4.

### 2.2 F2 Interactive music systems and accompaniment lineage

Real-time accompaniment has a birth year. At ICMC 1984 Dannenberg matched a live monophonic performance against a stored score by dynamic programming and drove synchronised accompaniment from a tempo estimate [@dannenberg1984online]; at the same conference Vercoe's Synthetic Performer reportedly tracked a flautist through pitch and fingering sensors and learned tempo tendencies across rehearsals [@vercoe1984syntheticperformer]. The Dannenberg line continued through polyphonic following, vocal tracking and the CACM 2006 review, then broadened into Human-Computer Music Performance (2011–14), which cast the computer as a band member with beat- and section-level synchronisation, human cueing and "active scores" — machine-readable, structure-aware scores with repeats and cues that can be re-sequenced live and followed by humans and machines alike [@dannenberg2014hcmp]. Both founders also left a music-as-code inheritance: Vercoe's Csound (1985–86) compiles an orchestra file and a score file into sound [@vercoe1984syntheticperformer], and Dannenberg's Nyquist treats scores as programs of behaviours and temporal combinators, later becoming Audacity's scripting language [@dannenberg1997nyquist].

Rowe's *Interactive Music Systems* (1993) turned this practice into a taxonomy whose axes the present review reuses. Rowe defined an interactive system as a sensing → processing → response chain and classified it on three dimensions [@rowe1993interactive]. Input: *score-driven* systems match incoming events against a stored score and can therefore use beat and metre, whereas *performance-driven* systems have no stored score and rely on perceptual measures such as density and regularity. Response: *transformative* systems vary the material they receive, *generative* systems produce complete output from elementary seeds under rules, and *sequenced* systems play back prerecorded fragments. Relationship: in the *instrument* paradigm the system extends a performer's gesture (solo-like), while in the *player* paradigm it is an artificial musician with its own personality (duet-like). The vocabulary became standard at ICMC and NIME [@rowe1993interactive]. In these terms the studio is a score-driven, transformative system in the instrument paradigm, with optional player agents in Play (S5); the taxonomy's initiative dimension D5 inherits the axes directly.

The player pole is populated by a distinguished line. Lewis's *Voyager* (begun 1987) listens to an improviser through pitch-to-MIDI and generates multiple independent voices that may respond, ignore or initiate — explicitly *not* an instrument the human controls, rooted in Afrological improvisation aesthetics [@lewis2000toomanynotes]. Biles's GenJam (1994) evolved jazz licks over a chord progression with a human "mentor" supplying real-time good/bad judgements as fitness, exposing the fitness bottleneck and formalising *annotation as fitness*, the primitive form of the studio's annotate-and-regenerate loop [@biles1994genjam]. Pachet's Continuator (2003) learned a variable-order Markov model of a player's phrases in real time and continued them in the same style, biasable by external constraints [@pachet2003continuator]. Flow Machines (2012–17) generalised this into *Markov constraints* — style plus hard constraints on metre, harmony and position — and FlowComposer, where users chose a corpus from more than 12,000 lead sheets, entered partial melody or chords, regenerated any selected region under sliders, and in professional use rarely generated more than eight bars at a time [@papadopoulos2016flowcomposer]. IRCAM's OMax and Somax2 learned a Factor Oracle over the live stream and improvised by recombining the performer's own material, without any external corpus [@assayag2006omax]. A parallel MIT strand is institutional: Machover's hyperinstruments (from 1986) augmented instruments with sensors so gesture shaped sound beyond the acoustic instrument, then reached non-professionals and children under the goal of "making every human being into a musician" [@machover1992hyperinstruments]; the sketch-based Hyperscore from that group is treated under S1.3 [@farbood2004hyperscore]. The thread from Vercoe's 1984 accompanist to the RL-tuned ReaLchords of 2024 is forty years long at one institution [@vercoe1984syntheticperformer; @wu2024realchords].

### 2.3 F3 Commercial auto-accompaniment and notation lineage

Band-in-a-Box (PG Music, 1990) is the longest-lived commercial "compile from a lead sheet": chord symbols typed into a bar grid plus key, tempo and style yield a full band arrangement, originally as MIDI styles and since 2007 as RealTracks — recorded human phrases time-stretched, pitch-shifted and spliced to fit the chords [@pgmusic1990bandinabox]. Soloist (1997), RealDrums (2006) and the Audio Chord Wizard (2007) followed; the 2026 release advertises more than 4,400 hours of recordings with 202 new sets, a notation view, DAW export and an "AI-Notes" polyphonic transcriber [@pgmusic2026biab]. The Korg i3 (1993) established the hardware "pro arranger": 48 styles each with four variations, two fills and two intros/endings; left-hand chord recognition driving drums, bass and three to four accompaniment parts in real time; and a 16-track sequencer plus a "Backing Sequencer" that recorded the style performance into an editable song, at about $2,500 [@korg1993i3]. It is credited with inspiring the Roland G-800 and Yamaha PSR variants, and the lineage runs to the Korg Pa5X (2022) and Yamaha Genos2 (2023), which add hundreds of styles, style-creator editors, chord sequencers and style parts on fixed MIDI channels [@korg1993i3].

Notation editors and sequencers form two families that never merged: engraving-first Finale (1988), Encore (1990), Sibelius (1993; Avid since 2006), MuseScore (2002) and Dorico (2016), versus timeline-first Cakewalk (1987), Cubase (1989) and Logic (Notator 1988 → Apple 2002), bridged by MusicXML (2004); the sequencer dates are partially verified [@makemusic2024finale]. MakeMusic discontinued Finale on 26 August 2024, stating that its "massive codebase — millions of lines accumulated over decades — has exponentially increased the challenge of delivering customer value," and offered Finale owners a $149 crossgrade to Dorico Pro (list $579) [@makemusic2024finale]. Dorico 6 (2025) added a rule-based Proofreading panel and no learned features [@steinberg2025dorico6], Sibelius's February 2026 release shipped no AI [@avid2026sibelius], and MuseScore Studio 4.6–4.7 added a beta audio-to-score converter with a 5.0 roadmap toward DAW-like features [@musegroup2025musescore46; @musescore2026studio]. No notation incumbent ships a generative composition assistant in 2026.

The arranger was instead reborn inside the DAW. Logic Pro 11 (May 2024) extended the 2013 Drummer into Session Players: a Bass Player with eight players, complexity and intensity controls and articulations (slides, mutes, dead notes, pickup hits) and a Keyboard Player with four styles, both following the Chord Track, "trained in collaboration with" session musicians and emitting editable MIDI regions [@apple2024logicpro11]. Scaler 3's Arrange page and the Orb and Captain Chords families drive part generators from one shared progression using curated content and a "re-roll a block" convention [@scaler2025v3; @hexachords2022orb], while Ableton Live 12 is the deliberate foil, with rule-based MIDI Generators and machine learning reserved for sample retrieval [@ableton2024live12]. The first learned member on a *commercial* notation surface is Hookpad Aria (2024), DeepBach's 2017 MuseScore plugin having preceded it in research (see S3.2), which infills, continues and harmonises inside a lead-sheet editor and reported a roughly 23% acceptance rate across its deployment (see E3) [@donahue2024hookpadaria].

Together these products give "compile from chords + style" a 35-year history and a stable set of virtues: instant, deterministic, reproducible arrangements; few and legible parameters; editable MIDI output; and, in the best cases, curated, licensed performance data rather than generic synthesis [@pgmusic2026biab; @apple2024logicpro11]. Their limits are equally stable. The control vocabulary is coarse and closed — a style name and a few switches, with no way to say "like this recording" or "quieter here." Styles are fixed pattern libraries external to the composer's material, the source of the lineage's genericness; nothing learns from what the composer wrote. The only input is a chord sequence; audio examples, humming, sketches and text play no role. Output is MIDI or audio rather than notation, the round-trip to a score is weak, and editing one part does not reconcile the others. Structure stops at intro, variation, fill and ending, and no system explains what it did.

### 2.4 Implications for the studio

The lineages agree on the shape of the compile step without sharing a vocabulary. From F1 the studio inherits the two-mode structure of hard constraints and soft stylistic priors, and from Koenig, Xenakis, Csound and Nyquist the idea that a composition is a program whose constraints the composer authors and whose realisation is compiled [@hiller1959experimental; @koenig1970project; @xenakis1992formalized; @vercoe1984syntheticperformer; @dannenberg1997nyquist]. From the Magenta pivot it inherits the finding that the useful unit is a model that fills holes in a human's material in any order, deployable at web scale on a notation surface [@huang2017coconet; @huang2019bachdoodle]. From F2 it inherits Rowe's axes as design coordinates, Dannenberg's active scores as an early machine-followable structured score, Biles's annotation-as-fitness, Pachet's Markov constraints as the pre-deep-learning form of "in my style, subject to my annotations," and OMax's proof that an agent can work from the composer's own material alone [@rowe1993interactive; @dannenberg2014hcmp; @biles1994genjam; @pachet2003continuator; @assayag2006omax]. From F3 it inherits a validated interaction and a market in which the notation incumbents have left generative assistance untouched while the DAWs have rebuilt the arranger with learned players [@apple2024logicpro11; @steinberg2025dorico6; @avid2026sibelius].

The gaps are as instructive. No commercial system accepts the loose modalities the studio proposes or learns from the composer's own material; the research precedents that did — FlowComposer's corpus selection, OMax's reinjection, the Continuator's real-time style learning — never reached the arranger market [@papadopoulos2016flowcomposer; @assayag2006omax; @pachet2003continuator]. The engraving and sequencing families remain split, so a notation-first compiler that renders to audio has no incumbent architecture to copy, and the Finale exit shows that legacy monoliths are liabilities and an open engraving core the safer foundation [@makemusic2024finale; @musegroup2025musescore46]. And the lineage leaves the AI silent: from the Illiac Suite to Session Players, no system explains which constraint or style choice produced which note — a gap section 3 shows to be general. The history therefore argues that the studio should present its compile step as the learned, annotation-steered successor of the arranger — keeping instant, deterministic, editable output and legible parameters — while replacing the closed style vocabulary with the composer's material and annotations, treating notation as the source of truth, and adding reconciliation across parts, structure beyond the fill, and an account of what was done. Aria's telemetry suggests that even the lead-sheet version of this proposition finds users [@donahue2024hookpadaria]; the arranger keyboard shows what the full-arrangement version could feel like [@korg1993i3].

## 3. Theories of human–AI co-creation (F4–F5)

Where section 2 traced what has been built, this section reviews how human–computer creative partnership has been theorised and evaluated, and which research programmes share the studio's stance. The frameworks (F4) supply the vocabulary in which the studio's position is stated — initiative, task division, communication direction, the AI's role — and the instruments by which its claims can be tested. The programmes and design spaces (F5) show that "steerable, decomposable, interactive rather than imitative" is a shared agenda at Magenta and MIT, CMU, Sony CSL and the Metacreation Lab.

### 3.1 F4 Theories of co-creativity and creativity support

The creativity-support-tools agenda gave the field its first principles and its standard instrument. Shneiderman's 2007 essay argued that creativity support tools should support exploratory search and collaboration, provide rich history-keeping (undo, versioning, replay), offer low thresholds with high ceilings and wide walls, allow many paths and styles, and be evaluated by long-term case studies rather than short lab tasks [@shneiderman2007cst]. Cherry and Latulipe's Creativity Support Index (2014) is a validated questionnaire with six factors — Exploration, Expressiveness, Immersion, Enjoyment, Results Worth Effort and Collaboration — weighted by paired-factor comparisons to yield a 0–100 score; it has become the default instrument in music co-creation studies, used by Cococo, MMM-C and Amuse [@cherry2014csi; @louie2020cococo; @tchemeube2023mmmc; @kim2025amuse]. Shneiderman's human-centered AI framework replaced the one-dimensional trade-off between automation and control with a two-dimensional space and argued for the high-control, high-automation quadrant — "supertools" rather than autonomous agents — with audit trails and explainability [@shneiderman2020hcai]. The studio's thesis that a compiler can be highly automated and highly controlled at once is, in this framework, a design target rather than a paradox.

Mixed-initiative and role frameworks describe who does what, when. The Mixed-Initiative Creative Interfaces workshop (CHI 2017) placed such systems between human-driven tools and autonomous computational creativity, defined them by a tight loop of constraining, suggesting and evaluating, and named the open challenges: letting non-programmers express formal constraints, legibility of AI decisions, fatigue, evaluation of co-creativity, and de-skilling [@deterding2017mici]. Annotations are precisely a means for non-programmers to express constraints, so these are the studio's research questions. Lubart's roles — *nanny* (managing process), *pen-pal* (mediating human collaboration), *coach* (teaching techniques) and *colleague* (generating alternatives the human evaluates and refines) — remain the most-cited role vocabulary [@lubart2005partners]. Kantosalo and Toivonen, working from Wiggins's creativity-as-search formalism, distinguished *alternating* co-creativity (turn-taking on one artefact) from *task-divided* co-creativity (agents own different sub-tasks in parallel) [@kantosalo2016modes]. Role preference has proved individual: Guzdial et al.'s turn-based Morai Maker level editor, across studies with more than 100 participants (91 in the controlled study), found designers split between wanting a friend, collaborator, student or manager, with the same agent rated helpful or annoying accordingly [@guzdial2019friend]. Davis et al.'s Drawing Apprentice introduced participatory sense-making for turn-by-turn analysis and reported that users often could not distinguish the agent from a Wizard-of-Oz human [@davis2016drawingapprentice]. Wan et al.'s prewriting study with GPT-3 (N=15) proposed LLM-led ideation followed by human-led illumination and implementation, with humans holding initiative except when blocked [@wan2024secondmind].

Rezwana and Maher's COFI is the most complete interaction vocabulary and the source of the field's most consequential negative finding. It decomposes co-creation into *collaboration* dimensions — participation style (alternating or parallel), timing, task distribution, contribution type, creative process — and *communication* dimensions split into human-to-AI channels (direct manipulation, embodied, intentional) and AI-to-human channels [@rezwana2023cofi]. Coding 92 systems, the authors found the field concentrated on generative ability and almost entirely lacked AI-to-human communication — explanations, intentions, confidence — which they argued distinguishes a partner from a tool [@rezwana2023cofi]. Their Creative Penpal study compared a sketching partner that only drew with one that also communicated its intentions; the communicating version was reportedly perceived as more collaborative, engaging and reliable, though some found it intrusive [@rezwana2022perceptions]. The gap is visible in music: Cococo's users developed "debugging" strategies to infer what the model was doing [@louie2020cococo], and the systems that do communicate back — RefinPaint's critic marking *where* to modify, ReaLJam's committed-versus-tentative chord waterfall, the XAI-for-the-arts programme's visualised latent dimensions — are recent exceptions treated under S4.4 [@ramoneda2024refinpaint; @scarlatos2025realjam; @bryankinns2021xai].

Interactive machine learning supplies the theory of example-based specification. Fiebrink and Caramiaux argued that a learning algorithm should be understood as an interface, and that learning by demonstration lets a musician teach an idiosyncratic mapping in minutes and then "break the rules"; the Wekinator's record–train–play–correct loop showed over years of workshops that examples and fast iteration beat parameter tweaking for non-programmers [@fiebrink2018mlcreativetool]. Scurto et al.'s Co-Explorer extended this to feedback-as-steering, with a reinforcement-learning agent adapting to like/dislike judgements in a synthesiser's parameter space [@scurto2021coexplorer], and Piano Genie, which decodes eight buttons into piano music so the human supplies contour and rhythm while the model supplies pitches, is the archetype of *AI as instrument* [@donahue2019pianogenie].

Computational-creativity theory supplies the terms in which creativity can be evaluated at all. Boden's distinction between combinational, exploratory and transformational creativity lets the studio say that its compiler performs exploratory generation inside a composer-defined space while the human supplies the transformational moves [@boden2004creativemind]. Ritchie's eighteen criteria over an "inspiring set," using typicality and quality ratings, ask whether output is both typical of the source material and good without copying it [@ritchie2007criteria]. Colton's tripod — skill, appreciation, imagination — stressed perception and framing, and *appreciation*, the ability to judge one's own output against the brief, is the leg most generators lack [@colton2008tripod]. Jordanous's SPECS defines creativity for the domain from fourteen components, chooses standards and tests against them, with a case study on improvisation systems [@jordanous2012standardised]. Karimi et al.'s four questions — *who* evaluates, *what* (product, process, interaction, experience), *when*, *how* — revealed that co-creative systems overwhelmingly evaluate user experience rather than the creativity of the collaboration [@karimi2018evaluating]. These are taken up as instruments in E5; the table records the vocabulary the taxonomy adopts and where the studio sits.

### 3.2 F5 Human-centered music-AI programmes and design spaces

Anna Huang's work is the most sustained articulation of a compatible philosophy, stated in her own words: creativity elicited "not through imitation but through interaction," Coconet's block-Gibbs procedure as support for "a nonlinear compositional process," and the aim "to build systems that musicians can shape, negotiate, and jam with," with visualisations that help them "understand and steer system behavior" [@huang2025creativityinteraction]. The trajectory runs from ChordRipple's chord recommendations at Harvard [@huang2016chordripple] through Magenta — Coconet, Music Transformer, the Bach Doodle and Cococo, whose study (N=21) raised controllability, comprehensibility, ownership and self-efficacy over a plain infilling baseline [@huang2017coconet; @huang2018musictransformer; @huang2019bachdoodle; @louie2020cococo] — to two field-defining studies. The AI Song Contest analysis of 13 teams (61 people) found that practitioners decomposed songwriting into building blocks, generated massively and curated because models were not steerable, and asked for interfaces that are "decomposable, steerable, interpretable, and adaptive" [@huang2020aisongcontest]. Expressive Communication crossed two models with two interfaces (26 composers, 1,020 listener comparisons) and found that better steering interfaces and more expressive models each independently improved composer control, ownership and self-efficacy as well as listener-judged musicality — the two are complementary rather than substitutes [@louie2022expressive]. Since 2024 her group at MIT — where she is Robert N. Noyce Career Development Professor with a shared appointment in EECS and Music and Theater Arts — has turned toward RL-tuned jamming agents — ReaLchords, ReaLJam's intention-displaying waterfall, the jam_bot built with Jordan Rudess, the concert case study Agents in Concert — and weekly co-design with working musicians [@wu2024realchords; @scarlatos2025realjam; @blanchard2025jambot; @brade2026agentsinconcert]. It sits inside MIT's Music Technology and Computation programme, launched Fall 2024 with Egozy as director, which held its first showcase on 13 May 2026 with a founding cohort of five and admitted ten students from more than a hundred applicants for 2026–27 [@mit2026mtcshowcase]; its ancestors are Vercoe's studio and Machover's hyperinstruments, both framing technology as expansion of human expression [@vercoe1984syntheticperformer; @machover1992hyperinstruments].

Chris Donahue's Generative Creativity Lab (G-CLef) at CMU, founded 2023 to "empower and enrich human creativity and productivity with generative AI," is the closest academic analogue of the studio's loop — CMU's music-technology activity otherwise splitting between the School of Music and the Dannenberg line [@cmu2025musictech] [@gclef2026lab]. Its line runs from Piano Genie [@donahue2019pianogenie] through the Anticipatory Music Transformer, whose asynchronous conditioning lets a human pin arbitrary notes while the model fills the rest [@thickstun2024anticipatory], to the deployed Hookpad Aria [@donahue2024hookpadaria], to Amuse (CHI 2025 Best Paper), which turned image, text and audio inspirations into chords via an editable keyword intermediate and rejection sampling against a harmonic prior, improving inspiration support, controllability, collaborative feel, expressiveness and exploration for ten songwriters with no time penalty [@kim2025amuse], and to Decomposer (2026), which decompiles MIDI into executable Strudel programs so that repeats, voices and harmony become editable code — the founder's "composition as program" run in the decompile direction [@kim2026decomposer]. Donahue holds the Dannenberg professorship, and the two CMU generations connect through Dai and Dannenberg's finding that deep generators differ strikingly from human pop in repetition and structure, answered with user-editable structural skeletons [@dai2022missing; @dai2021controllable].

The CHI 2026 *Design Space for Live Music Agents*, a Donahue–Huang collaboration, is the first systematic map of real-time human–AI music collaboration, coding 184 systems into 31 dimensions; it is treated in detail under S5.4, and its dimensions overlap D4–D6 and D8 for the real-time corner [@kim2026designspace].

Sony CSL Paris represents a practitioner-embedded stance with a longer pedigree, from the Continuator and Flow Machines [@pachet2003continuator; @papadopoulos2016flowcomposer] to Deruty et al.'s six- to eighteen-month collaborations with six professional acts using DrumNet, BassNet, DrumGAN and related prototypes. Thematic analysis yielded "pull" over "push," *priming* on the artist's own audio, generate-and-curate and the exploitation of artefacts, alongside the friction of leaving the DAW and the lack of recognisable control; the authors proposed validating tools by workflow integration, task simplification, stimulation, identifiable contribution and commercial viability, and argued that studio pop practice is audio-first — a counterpoint the symbolic-first studio must answer by rendering stems [@deruty2022sonycsl]. RefinPaint, a UPF–Sony CSL Tokyo collaboration, extends the stance to critique [@ramoneda2024refinpaint].

The Metacreation Lab at Simon Fraser has pursued the multi-track symbolic compiler with unusual continuity and measured it. The Multi-Track Music Machine became Calliope, in which users select bars and tracks for infilling, batch-generate up to 1,000 candidates and rank them under per-track controls for instrument, density, polyphony and duration [@tchemeube2022calliope]. Integrated into Cubase as the one-parameter MMM-C and evaluated with 18 expert composers, it scored SUS 71.4–75.7 across three tasks and highest on CSI enjoyment, but ease of control was 5.2/10 while *desire for more control* averaged 9.5/10 — the cost of under-parameterised control even inside the DAW [@tchemeube2023mmmc]. The lineage continued into MMM4Live [@metacreation2023mmm4live] and MIDI-GPT (AAAI 2025), with bar-level multi-track infilling and attribute controls under an Open RAIL-M licence [@pasquier2025midigpt].

A final programme is pedagogical and functions as a design stance. Egozy's 21M.385 Interactive Music Systems at MIT has students build real-time musical software in Python with Kivy and the `imslib` library — synthesis and looping, MIDI sequencing, generative composition, gesture sensing, game design and visualisation — culminating in a public demo concert, with Guitar Hero as the case study of interactive systems' cultural reach [@egozy21m385]. The stance is Harmonix's: Guitar Hero (2005) and Rock Band (2007), co-founded by Egozy and Rigopulos out of Machover's group, let non-musicians perform through simplified controllers and a scrolling note highway, reportedly selling more than 35 million units [@egozy2005harmonix]; Egozy's account of musical expression in those games, and later work on in-browser score following and AI-augmented instruments, articulates how agency survives inside constrained interfaces [@egozy2016harmonix]. Miller's ethnography argued the games are genuine performance and informal learning [@miller2009schizophonic], and Fuser and DropMix showed casual users building coherent arrangements from constrained stems [@harmonix2020fuser]. ConcertCue, streaming time-synchronised programme notes cued to musical events, is a deployed time-aligned annotation system aimed at listeners [@egozy2018concertcue], and Cuthbert's 21M.383 teaches composers to treat notation as data over the music21 corpus — the mindset behind composition as a program [@cuthbert2023ocw21m383; @cuthbert2010music21]. The note highway is the ancestor of ReaLJam's waterfall and Piano Genie's contour mapping [@scarlatos2025realjam; @donahue2019pianogenie], and the pedagogy's insistence on low latency, visual clarity and playfulness is a design prior for Play mode.

### 3.3 Implications for the studio

The frameworks let the studio's position be stated exactly, and the statement exposes where it departs from the literature. In Rowe's terms the compiler is score-driven, transformative and in the instrument paradigm; in Lubart's it is a colleague at compile time and a coach when it annotates back; in Kantosalo and Toivonen's it alternates on the score and divides tasks by role; in Shneiderman's it must occupy the high-control, high-automation quadrant [@rowe1993interactive; @lubart2005partners; @kantosalo2016modes; @shneiderman2020hcai]. In COFI's terms it is human-initiated and bidirectional — and this is the departure, because AI-to-human communication was almost entirely absent across 92 coded systems [@rezwana2023cofi]. Evidence that such communication is wanted comes from Creative Penpal, from Cococo's users reverse-engineering the model, and from ReaLJam's pianists asking to see the agent's plan, while RefinPaint shows what a critic that marks *where* to change looks like [@rezwana2022perceptions; @louie2020cococo; @scarlatos2025realjam; @ramoneda2024refinpaint]. A compiler that returns annotations — what it did with each mark, where it is unsure, what alternatives it considered — is therefore both a theoretical requirement and an empirical gap (S4.4, D6).

The programmes converge on requirements the studio should treat as settled. Practitioners decompose, steer and curate and want interpretability [@huang2020aisongcontest]; steering interfaces move ownership more than stronger models, and the two are complementary [@louie2022expressive]; one control parameter inside the DAW is not enough [@tchemeube2023mmmc]; professionals pull rather than accept pushes, prime on their own material and refuse to leave their tools [@deruty2022sonycsl]; examples beat parameters for musicians [@fiebrink2018mlcreativetool]; initiative must be adjustable because role preference is individual [@guzdial2019friend]; and ownership tracks the strength of the human's authored input [@krol2025ownership]. The MICI challenges remain open and are the questions an annotation layer must answer [@deterding2017mici], while the live-agents design space shows the real-time corner well mapped and ecosystem questions addressed by one system in twenty [@kim2026designspace].

Two gaps remain. First, evaluation: co-creative systems overwhelmingly measure user experience, long-term case studies were called for in 2007, and longitudinal music studies remain rare [@karimi2018evaluating; @shneiderman2007cst; @deruty2022sonycsl]. The studio should adopt the CSI with ownership, control and comprehension items, pair it with listener judgements in the Expressive Communication protocol, apply Ritchie's criteria and SPECS's components to compiled output, and log interventions as provenance from the first prototype (E1, E5) [@cherry2014csi; @louie2022expressive; @ritchie2007criteria; @jordanous2012standardised]. Second, the surface: every programme reviewed here operates on a piano-roll, a lead sheet, a DAW clip or a live instrument; none has articulated a human-centered agenda for the engraved score, the surface on which composers in the classical and arranging traditions actually annotate. The frameworks are ready; the notation-native instance of them is the studio's contribution to make.

## 4. Compose: getting musical ideas in (S1)

The composer's loop begins with raw material that rarely arrives as clean notation: it is played on a keyboard, hummed into a phone, scribbled on paper, photographed, or triggered by an image, a film cue or a sentence. The Compose node (S1) collects the systems that turn such inputs into something a studio can hold — MIDI, a lead sheet, a chord chart, a score — across five channels: performance capture and transcription (S1.1), voice (S1.2), sketch and pen (S1.3), image, video and text (S1.4), and example audio or personal style (S1.5). The recurring result, stated first because it organises what follows, is that every *loose* modality that has succeeded with musicians lands in a symbolic, editable representation, while systems that map directly to audio offer no handle for correction; and that even the best transcribers err often enough that a correction interface is a design requirement rather than a courtesy [@donahue2022sheetsage; @liu2023humtrans; @kim2025amuse; @cavez2025euterpen].

### 4.1 S1.1 Performance capture and transcription

Audio-to-MIDI transcription is the most mature loose channel, and its architecture has been stable since @hawthorne2018onsets introduced Onsets and Frames: a CNN–BiLSTM predicts onsets and frame-wise pitch activity jointly, onsets gate the frames so no note begins without a detected attack, and velocity is predicted alongside. It more than doubled note-with-offset F1 on MAPS, and its failure modes — missed offsets, merged repeated notes — are inherited by every descendant. @gardner2022mt3 generalised the recipe to mixtures with MT3, a T5-style encoder–decoder emitting MIDI-like tokens with instrument programs, trained jointly on MAESTRO, Slakh, Cerberus4, GuitarSet, MusicNet and URMP; joint training substantially improved low-resource instruments such as guitar. @bittner2022basicpitch went the other way with Basic Pitch, a very small instrument-agnostic CNN that outputs MIDI with pitch bends, ships in Python and TypeScript under Apache-2.0, and exposes onset and frame thresholds and a minimum note length as the user's only correction knobs. For a symbolic-first studio the more consequential system is Sheet Sage: @donahue2022sheetsage fed Jukebox representations — following the JukeMIR finding that codified-audio language models learn transferable MIR features [@castellon2021codified] — into a melody transcriber, gaining 20% over spectrogram features and 77% over the best prior baseline, then composed it with beat, key and chord estimation to emit a *lead sheet* from arbitrary audio. Its 50-hour Hooktheory melody-and-harmony dataset later trained Amuse's prior and Hookpad Aria [@kim2025amuse; @donahue2024hookpadaria].

The large expressive-piano corpora that anchor symbolic modelling are themselves transcription products, which matters for provenance (B4): MAESTRO paired roughly 200 hours of Disklavier performances with audio under CC-BY-NC-SA [@hawthorne2019maestro]; GiantMIDI-Piano and ATEPP transcribed 10,855 and 11,674 recordings under CC-BY 4.0 [@kong2020giantmidi; @zhang2022atepp]; Aria-MIDI scaled to 1,186,253 files (~100,629 hours) transcribed from YouTube, again non-commercial [@bradshaw2025ariamidi]. Optical music recognition is the parallel path for material that starts on paper. The Sheet Music Transformer of @riosvila2024smt transcribed polyphonic pianoform and quartet images end-to-end into Humdrum **kern, cutting character error rate by 91.8% and line error rate by 89.1% on GrandStaff (53,882 images) and a new Quartets set (38,051); oemer (MIT) targets MusicXML from phone photographs, and Audiveris (AGPL-3.0) wraps its pipeline in an interactive OMR editor for correction before MusicXML 4.0 export. @jung2025unified then unified score images, MusicXML, MIDI and audio under one tokenisation, trained on the >1,300-hour YouTube Score Video dataset, reducing OMR symbol error rate from 24.58% to 13.67% and demonstrating the first score-image-conditioned audio generation. In all of these the human controls nothing at inference; correction happens downstream in a DAW or notation editor, which is why the studio's transcription step must terminate in an editable surface [@bittner2022basicpitch; @riosvila2024smt].

### 4.2 S1.2 Voice: humming and singing as input

The voice lineage is thirty years old and has taught one durable lesson: hummed pitch is unreliable, so systems either reduce it to a coarse representation or accept that a human must fix it. @ghias1995qbh converted a hum to a three-symbol up/down/same contour string matched approximately against a 183-song MIDI database, finding that 10–12 pitch transitions discriminated 90% of songs. Google's Hum to Search replaced the symbolic intermediate with an embedding trained to land hums near studio recordings across more than 500k songs, using synthetic hums derived via SPICE pitch extraction [@frank2020humtosearch] — production-viable retrieval with no editable intermediate whatsoever. The transcription side has been quantified by HumTrans, a 56.22-hour dataset of 1,000 segments hummed twice each by ten music-trained students against reference melodies, on which the best off-the-shelf vocal transcriber reached only 6.8% note F1 on validation and 5.7% on test [@liu2023humtrans]. @gupta2024dynhumtrans corrected HumTrans's onset and offset labels and paired a CNN frame classifier with dynamic-programming decoding to reach an octave-invariant note-plus-onset F1 of 0.673 against 0.604 for the next-best method (MIR-ST500) — a real but modest gain that still leaves roughly one note in three wrong, and whose DP stage is the natural place to inject a composer's declared key and meter as priors.

Products reached the same conclusion by other means. ScoreCloud (2014–) and Sing2Notes (2021–) transcribe singing straight to sheet music with quantised and unquantised MIDI and MusicXML export, and Sing2Notes ships an in-app Edit Mode; neither publishes accuracy, though Klangio claims more than four million transcriptions [@doremir2014scorecloud]. Vochlea's Dubler 2 converts singing, humming and beatboxing to MIDI in real time with a key/scale lock, a visual note wheel, a "stickiness" slider that smooths spurious transitions, vowel-to-CC mapping and per-user training of up to twelve examples per beatbox sound [@vochlea2021dubler] — constrain, smooth, visualise and personalise rather than trust the tracker. At the audio end, SingSong adapted AudioLM to generate a full instrumental accompaniment from a sung vocal, mining (vocal, instrumental) pairs by source separation; listeners significantly preferred it to a retrieval baseline (the reported margin is given under S3.9), and the only control is to sing again [@donahue2023singsong]. It is the strongest demonstration that a hummed line implies harmony, groove and style, and the clearest case of an inspiring system with no correction handle. Two strands widen what the voice can specify. VocalSketch collected 4,429 vocal imitations from 248 contributors and found forced-choice identification of 0.80 for everyday sounds but only 0.42–0.54 for synthesiser sounds and 0.45 for instruments against 0.1 chance [@cartwright2015vocalsketch]; Sketch2Sound made a vocal imitation a *control signal*, extracting loudness, spectral-centroid and pitch curves into a text-to-audio DiT through one linear layer per control, with training-time median filtering so the user chooses how tightly the output follows the gesture [@floresgarcia2025sketch2sound]. Tone Transfer had put the simplest version in a browser in 2020 — SPICE pitch plus a DDSP model re-render a hum as flute, saxophone, violin or trumpet [@magenta2020tonetransfer; @engel2020ddsp] — and Piano Genie's eight buttons decoded to full piano performances remain the archetype of low-dimensional performed intent realised by a model [@donahue2019pianogenie].

### 4.3 S1.3 Sketch, pen and scribble as input

Drawing as a score predates deep learning by decades. Xenakis's UPIC (1977) let a composer draw arcs on a tablet with time on one axis and pitch on the other, and draw waveforms and envelopes too; IanniX later generalised it into an open-source graphical sequencer emitting OSC and MIDI [@xenakis1977upic; @xenakis1992formalized]. UPIC also carries a warning: its drawings map to sound, not notation, so a system must decide what a scribble *means*. Hyperscore made that decision explicit. Motives entered on a pitch–time grid are assigned colours; coloured strokes in a sketch window instantiate them, with stroke length as repetitions and shape as transposition contour; and a separate harmony line — flat for stability, rising for tension, spiked for modulation — drives re-harmonisation of the whole texture into orchestral notation [@farbood2004hyperscore]. In Toy Symphony workshops in five cities, with seven to twelve children each over five sessions, nearly every child completed a string-orchestra piece. @coughlan2006interaction supplied the theory, proposing ideation–evaluation cycles as the atomic unit of creative interaction and arguing that tools should support incomplete, ambiguous representations; @garcia2012paper reportedly extended this to interactive paper on which composers lay out their own pen-sensitive staff, curve and timeline substrates linked to OpenMusic and Max.

The pen has since become a commercial notation surface and an object of HCI study. StaffPad (Surface 2015, iPad 2020) recognises handwriting one measure at a time, replaces ink with engraving, keeps a correction palette, and pushes parts and shared annotations to players' tablets through ScoreSync [@hearn2015staffpad]. @cavez2024challenges interviewed nine professional composers and, through the Cognitive Dimensions of Notations, found that structured notation editors impose premature commitment and viscosity that block ideation and drive composers back to paper; their EuterPen prototype selectively relaxes syntactic and structural constraints so composers can move symbols freely and "make space on, between and around staves to insert additional content such as digital ink, pictures and audio samples" [@cavez2025euterpen]. Participating composers called it compelling; there is no AI in it, which is exactly what makes it the closest existing model of the studio's scribble-as-annotation step.

Sketch-conditioned *generation* is a smaller but well-evidenced line. Music SketchNet treated a sketch as a partial specification of a missing measure — pitch contour, rhythm pattern, or both — and factorised a VAE so each could be supplied independently; a 106-subject listening test found improved musicality over baselines [@chen2020sketchnet]. Draw and Listen! inpainted missing measures from a drawn pitch-contour curve and a note-density curve, beating rule-based and genetic baselines on 24,065 Irish tunes, and users without notation knowledge found it intuitive [@benetatos2022drawlisten]. MIDI-Draw found that non-musicians drew over-oscillating curves and wanted rhythm variation, arguing for smoothing affordances and a separate rhythm lane [@namgyal2022mididraw]. Drawlody is the best-evidenced system: a CNN–Transformer maps a simplified contour to a melody and displays the alignment between sketch and result; eighteen participants rated its user-friendliness 4.38/5 against 2.94 for a chord-based and 2.38 for a DAW interface, and interpretability 4.50/5, while musicality stayed below expert compositions [@liang2024drawlody]. The shared interaction contract — coarse gesture, plausible inference, instant display, gestural refinement — was set by Teddy's inflation of 2D strokes into 3D models [@igarashi1999teddy]; the claim that pen input's value lies in relaxing structure rather than recognising handwriting rests on the Paris-Saclay studies [@cavez2024challenges; @cavez2025euterpen].

### 4.4 S1.4 Image, video and text as inspiration or condition

Amuse is the reference design for turning a non-musical inspiration into editable material [@kim2025amuse]. An image, text or audio clip goes to a multimodal LLM (GPT-4o), which extracts *music keywords* the user can inspect, add or delete; the LLM proposes N diverse four-bar chord progressions; and a rejection sampler accepts a candidate only if u < P(x)/(M·Q(x)), where P is an LSTM chord prior trained on the 50-hour Hooktheory data, Q an LSTM fitted to 25,000 GPT-4o progressions, and M = 7.64. Audio inspirations go through a chord transcriber instead, and accepted chords paste into Hookpad for audition and editing. In a within-subjects study with ten songwriters (eight hobbyists, two professionals) writing an eight-bar chorus in 25 minutes, Amuse plus Aria beat Aria alone on inspiration support (6.20 vs 4.60, p < .01), controllability (5.80 vs 4.40), collaboration, exploration and expressiveness, with no difference in completion time or externally judged quality; 45 listeners found the rejection-sampled chords matched the LSTM prior on coherence, exceeded raw GPT-4o (p = .009) and were most preferred for keyword relevance (58%). The transferable moves are the human-legible intermediate and the small domain prior that disciplines a broad LLM; the cautionary finding is that participants expected the downstream model to know the inspiration too, so annotations must propagate through the whole compile chain [@kim2025amuse; @donahue2024hookpadaria].

The wider image-to-music literature splits by output domain, and the split predicts usefulness to a composer. Vis2Mus, a rare symbolic system, found visual-to-music mappings approximately equivariant and let users steer generated accompaniment through an image's brightness and contrast, with participants matching transformed music to transformed images at 62.5–68.2% against 50% chance [@zhang2022vis2mus]. MeLFusion injected text-to-image attention features into a music diffusion model, improving FAD by up to 67.98% over text-only on the 11,250-triplet MeLBench, but emits audio only [@chowdhury2024melfusion]. Art2Mus is the honest negative result: an ImageBind encoder on frozen AudioLDM 2 lowered KL divergence, yet raters preferred plain AudioLDM 2, and the 2026 extension with 105,884 pairs found only marginal differences [@rinaldi2024art2mus]. Video follows suit: V2Meow generated ten-second audio from frame features and an optional text prompt with 83.8% visual-relevance preference [@su2024v2meow]; VidMuse scaled to ~360k video–music pairs with a long–short-term visual module [@tian2025vidmuse]; MuVi separated semantic alignment from rhythmic synchronisation, raising beat-hit score from 25.14% to 49.23% [@li2024muvi]; MuMu-LLaMA wrapped MERT, ViT and ViViT encoders around LLaMA with MusicGen and AudioLDM 2 decoders to accept any modality plus prompt-based edits, with 81.3% and 80.7% preference for image- and video-to-music [@liu2024mumullama]. None yields anything a composer can edit. Video2Music is the exception: an Affective Multimodal Transformer decodes scene cuts, motion, emotion and semantics into a *chord sequence*, the user supplies key and an optional seed progression, and 21 raters scored it 4.2/7 against 3.5 for a Music Transformer baseline [@kang2024video2music]. MuVi's semantic/rhythmic and VidMuse's global/local splits are the two things a film composer actually marks — mood and hit points — and suggest a symbolic pipeline of detect, annotate, compile [@li2024muvi; @tian2025vidmuse].

Text-to-symbolic generation, the commonest inspiration channel, points the same way, and is treated as a compile-time control under S3.3: the systems that work best interpose an explicit, user-editable layer of attributes or tags between the sentence and the notes rather than mapping prose straight to music [@lu2023musecoco; @bhandari2025text2midi; @wu2025midillm; @xu2025metascore]. Across S1.4, inspiration is best routed through a visible intermediate — keywords, attributes, a chord chart — that the composer can override before anything is compiled.

### 4.5 S1.5 Example audio and personal style as reference

Musicians who think in references rather than theory terms want to steer by instance. Music Creation by Example studied 104 video creators and found that supplying an example song, then regenerating and mixing at stem level, was more natural than tag or text search [@frid2020example]. Cococo's example-based similarity slider, one of four steering tools that lifted controllability from 3.5 to 5.9 and ownership from 3.8 to 5.2 among 21 novices, implemented the same idea as a soft prior on Coconet's sampling [@louie2020cococo]. MusicGen-Style conditioned MusicGen on an excerpt through a discrete RVQ bottleneck with heavy dropout so that style is copied without content, balanced text against audio with double classifier-free guidance, and released weights under CC-BY-NC [@rouard2024musicgenstyle]; Neutone's plugins reportedly run neural timbre models in real time inside a DAW with an SDK for artists to train on their own recordings [@neutone2024morpho].

Personal style is the symbolic counterpart. MidiMe trained a four-dimensional VAE over MusicVAE's 256-dimensional latent from a single user MIDI file, in the browser in seconds, yielding a personal four-knob space of variations [@dinculescu2019midime]. MIDI-RWKV introduced *state tuning* — fine-tuning only the initial recurrent state of a linear-time RWKV-7 model — so a composer's handful of files personalise an edge-deployable infilling model, released under CC-BY 4.0 [@zhouzheng2025midirwkv]. @dai2023personalised analysed a reference song's structure, melody, chords and bass style and generated a new song constrained to that template, with the user choosing what to imitate. In the terms of programming by demonstration, each generalises from a demonstrated example and lets the user correct the generalisation [@cypher1993wwid].

### 4.6 Implications for the studio

The central finding is architectural. Every loose modality that musicians have adopted terminates in an editable symbolic object: humming in MIDI or a lead sheet [@bittner2022basicpitch; @donahue2022sheetsage; @doremir2014scorecloud], drawing in notes or a contour lane [@farbood2004hyperscore; @liang2024drawlody; @hearn2015staffpad], images and video in keywords and chords [@kim2025amuse; @kang2024video2music], text in an attribute layer [@lu2023musecoco]. Systems that skip the symbolic layer — SingSong, V2Meow, VidMuse, MeLFusion, Hum to Search — are impressive and offer the composer nothing to correct but the input [@donahue2023singsong; @su2024v2meow; @frank2020humtosearch], and where a direct comparison exists, end-to-end conditioning was not even preferred [@rinaldi2024art2mus]. The studio should route all eight modalities (D4) into notation or MIDI first and treat audio as a rendering target (S3.8); a symbolic SingSong — sung melody to chords and arrangement in notation — is an open and well-motivated target.

Second, transcription needs a correction interface, not a better model alone. Purpose-built humming transcription reaches ~0.67 note F1 and off-the-shelf models 0.43–0.60 on the same relabelled data — the single-digit F1 scores reported against HumTrans's original labels reflect defective onset annotation rather than model incapability [@liu2023humtrans; @gupta2024dynhumtrans]; the best OMR still misreads more than one symbol in eight [@jung2025unified]; Roman-numeral labelling sits at 51–62% on full labels (S2.2). The working patterns exist in products — scale lock and stickiness smoothing [@vochlea2021dubler], click-to-fix edit modes [@doremir2014scorecloud], threshold sliders [@bittner2022basicpitch], measure-granular recognition with a correction palette [@hearn2015staffpad], an OMR editor before export [@riosvila2024smt] — and composer-declared key and meter should enter decoders as priors, with every transcription shown for confirmation before it is compiled.

The gaps follow. Pen input should relax structure, not merely recognise handwriting: EuterPen's ink, pictures and audio clips between staves is the model, and it awaits an AI that reads them [@cavez2025euterpen]. Contour sketches deserve a first-class annotation type with separate pitch and rhythm lanes, smoothing affordances and an alignment view showing how the sketch was interpreted [@chen2020sketchnet; @namgyal2022mididraw; @liang2024drawlody]. Amuse's keyword intermediate must propagate through the whole compile chain [@kim2025amuse]. And no system yet accepts humming, sketch, image and example audio *on the same score*; the annotation layer that would make this possible is the subject of the next section.

## 5. Annotate: expressing intent on the material (S2)

Annotation is the studio's central concept: a typed, musically anchored mark — on a bar, a staff, a region or a note — that the compiler must honour. Three literatures bear on it, and they have barely met. Musicology and notation engineering supply mature *encodings* for marks anchored to musical time (S2.1); music information retrieval supplies *analysis models* that can propose such marks for a human to accept or fix (S2.2); and controllable generation has converged on a *control vocabulary* — density, polyphony, register, chord per bar, section label, tension curve — that is exactly what a composer would annotate (S2.3). What is missing is the fourth piece: treating a composer's multimodal marks on notation as instructions to a generator, for which the templates come from video and vision rather than music (S2.4). A short final subsection records the evidence that one well-placed human label can outperform automation (S2.5).

### 5.1 S2.1 Annotation encodings, standards and tools

Machine-readable score annotation is a solved problem in digital musicology, and the solutions share a shape: a typed label, an anchor in *symbolic* time, an optional span, free text. MEI encodes Western notation with editorial markup for readings, corrections, supplied text and genetic layers, and Verovio engraves it to SVG in which every glyph is an addressable element carrying its MEI id, alongside MIDI and a timemap for audio synchronisation [@pugin2014verovio]; MEI thus already has vocabulary for "this passage is a variant or an editorial suggestion" — for distinguishing an AI proposal from the composer's text. mei-friend places an MEI code view beside a live Verovio rendering, commits encodings to GitHub and attaches Web Annotations to score elements [@goebl2023meifriend], a precedent for text–notation dual editing, git-based history (S4.5) and annotation anchored to notation ids. MusicXML 4.0 (W3C Final Community Group Specification, June 2021) remains the interchange format read by 250+ applications, but its annotation vocabulary stops at `<direction>` text; its JSON successor MNX had settled on `.mnx.json` in March 2026 yet was still deciding core encoding questions in August 2026 and cannot be treated as finished [@w3c2021musicxml40]. ABC is compact, text-native and re-rendered on every keystroke by abcjs, at the cost of weaker polyphony and layout [@walshaw2011abc; @abcjs2026]. Humdrum makes annotation a parallel column: **kern holds the notes and users add **harm, **text or **dynam spines beside them, so labels and notes travel in one plain-text file consumable by seventy-odd tools and by an LLM [@huron1995humdrum].

Analysts' tools show what a good annotation *interface* looks like. Dezrann places labels by dragging left-to-right for a span or top-to-bottom for an instant, on a staff or in zones above and below it; each label carries a type, an onset and optional duration in measure-and-beat time, a tag and a comment, so the same `.dez` JSON aligns to score, waveform and video; by 2025 it held 35,000+ annotations over 1,500+ pieces in ten corpora with collaborative editing, under GPLv3+ code and ODbL data [@giraud2018dezrann]. Sonic Visualiser is the audio-side counterpart: layers of instants, curves, notes and regions over a waveform, entered by clicking, tapping during playback or MIDI, with Vamp plugins that auto-generate beat, chord, key and segmentation layers the user then corrects [@cannam2010sonicvisualiser]. Theorists have standardised the label grammars. RomanText is an off-score, human-writable text format — measure, beat, key, Roman numeral, comment — parsed by music21, in which When in Rome holds 2,000+ analyses of 1,500 works with conversion to on-score labels [@gotham2019wheninrome; @cuthbert2010music21]. The DCML standard is the on-score alternative: ~36,000 harmony, phrase and cadence labels for the sixteen Beethoven quartets were typed as text attached to notes in MuseScore, then extracted by the `ms3` parser into tables, with the syntax defined by a regular expression that admits recursive applied chords [@neuwirth2018abc; @hentschel2021mozart]. This "annotate in the editor, parse to a table" workflow is the one the studio needs, and the regex grammar is directly reusable for validating harmonic instructions.

Performers annotate too, and their practices supply a taxonomy. Newzik's LiveScores turn PDFs into interactive MusicXML and synchronise a conductor's marks to an ensemble's tablets, treating annotations as first-class objects separate from the score [@newzik2026readers], as does StaffPad's ScoreSync [@hearn2015staffpad]. Tactus analysed seventeen practice videos and ten interviews and distinguished *performance-oriented* annotations that persist (dynamics, bowings, breaths) from *practice-oriented* ones that are temporary (rhythm visualisations, accidentals, progress), then proposed system-authored feedback and feedforward marks alongside user-created ones [@fu2026tactus]. ConcertCue streamed program notes to audience phones during Boston Symphony and other performances, cued to specific musical events [@egozy2018concertcue]. Together these fix the fields a studio annotation record needs: type, anchor, span, author (human or system), lifetime (persistent or ephemeral) and free text.

### 5.2 S2.2 Machine-proposed annotations (analysis models)

Analysis models can propose labels for the composer to accept or fix, and their accuracy curve tells the studio how much to trust them. AugmentedNet read pitch-spelled MusicXML and predicted eleven tonal tasks jointly before assembling Roman numerals, reaching 82.9% on key, 67.0% on scale degree and 46.4% on the full Roman numeral; it was adopted for harmonic analysis in Sibelius [@napoleslopez2021augmentednet]. ChordGNN modelled the score as a note graph built with partitura and pooled note-wise features to onset-wise predictions, reporting higher accuracy than AugmentedNet [@karystinaios2023chordgnn]; RNBert fine-tuned the MusicBERT symbolic language model for key-conditioned Roman-numeral prediction and reported state of the art by transfer from unlabelled pretraining [@sailor2024rnbert] — evidence that one symbolic foundation model can serve generation and annotation heads alike. The segmentation-plus-labelling framing came from Chen and Su's Harmony Transformer line and the BPS-FH dataset [@chen2021attend]. Full-label accuracy near one in two is good enough to *propose* and not good enough to trust silently — the mixed-initiative posture the annotate step requires.

Structure analysis is more mature. MSAF bundled the classical boundary and labelling algorithms with mir_eval evaluation [@nieto2016msaf]; All-In-One predicted beats, downbeats, section boundaries and functional labels (intro, verse, chorus, bridge, outro) jointly from source-separated spectrograms and set the state of the art on the Harmonix Set with fewer parameters [@kim2023allinone]. For symbolic pop, Dai and Dannenberg extracted section/phrase repetition structure from 909 Chinese pop transcriptions with 93% phrase-boundary accuracy, finding that repeated phrases cover 50–90% of most songs and that V–I cadences close 94% of sections against 47% elsewhere [@dai2024interconnections], and showed that deep generators differ strikingly from human pop on these metrics [@dai2022missing] — the empirical case for structure as an explicit, editable layer [@dai2021controllable]. Deeper theory has thinner tooling: the Duke Schenkerian dataset grew the machine-readable corpus from 41 to more than 140 excerpts with custom notation software for reductions and a heterogeneous-graph formulation [@nihahn2024schenker], while galant schema detection via skipgram pattern discovery reportedly remains open [@finkensiep2018skipgrams]. Older baselines still power products: madmom-era chord and key estimators reportedly plateaued near 80–85% major/minor accuracy, and symbolic phrase segmentation lacks a shared benchmark [@bock2016madmom]. Two further families act as critics rather than labellers. Voice-leading checkers — music21's `voiceLeading` module and, reportedly, Palestrina Pal — are deterministic linters that flag parallel fifths and voice crossings, the natural "compiler warnings" for compiled output [@huang2005palestrinapal; @cuthbert2010music21]. SynTheory probed Jukebox and MusicGen layer by layer for tempo, meter, intervals, scales, chords and progressions and found them linearly detectable, varying by size and layer [@wei2024musictheory] — a method for checking whether a backbone understands the vocabulary the composer annotates with. Whether *LLMs* understand it is doubtful: MusicTheoryBench and MuChoMusic both report weak reasoning and over-reliance on the language modality [@yuan2024chatmusician; @weck2024muchomusic], so the LLM should orchestrate symbolic analysers rather than replace them (E6).

### 5.3 S2.3 Control vocabularies as annotation schemas

Controllable generation has, without coordinating, converged on the attributes a composer would write in the margin. FIGARO conditions a seq2seq Transformer on a per-bar description of expert features — instruments present, chords, time signature, note density, mean pitch, mean velocity, mean duration — plus learned VQ-VAE codes, so a composer can edit the bar-by-bar description and regenerate [@vonrutte2023figaro]: a formal annotation language in all but name. MusIAC added track-level note density, polyphony and occupation rate and *bar-level tonal tension* (tensile strain and cloud diameter) as infilling controls, finding the richer tokens improved perceived coherence over density alone [@guo2022musiac]. Composer's Assistant 2 lets a composer create empty MIDI items on named REAPER tracks to mark what to generate, then set rhythmic-conditioning modes, horizontal and vertical onset density, pitch range and "rhythmic interest"; its listening study rated AI-completed excerpts comparably to fully human ones [@malandro2024composersassistant2; @malandro2023composersassistant]. MIDI-GPT, a ~20M-parameter bar-within-track model shipped inside Cubase and Ableton Live, conditions on instrument, ten instrument-relative density levels, polyphony range, a five-bin duration range and a style ontology [@pasquier2025midigpt]. MuseCoco's twelve-attribute layer [@lu2023musecoco], MuseMorphose's eight-level bar-wise rhythmic-intensity and polyphony faders over an existing piece [@wu2023musemorphose], and Polyffusion's separation of *internal* masks (what to keep) from *external* chord and texture conditions (what to steer) [@min2023polyffusion] complete the symbolic inventory.

Two distinctions in this literature matter for an annotation compiler. The first is hard versus soft. SymPAC placed genre, section labels, tempo, chords and track list in *prompt bars* the user may partially specify and enforced them by finite-state constrained decoding rather than soft conditioning; twelve MIR researchers and producers rated it significantly above FIGARO and MMT on structure and arrangement [@chen2024sympac]. A composer who writes "Cmaj7 here" expects a constraint, not a suggestion, and the Anticipatory Music Transformer offers the same guarantee for notes: whatever is pinned becomes a control event the model must condition on, wherever in time it lies [@thickstun2024anticipatory]. The second is discrete versus continuous. Music ControlNet accepted melody, dynamics and rhythm *curves* specified for only part of the timeline — a melody for bars 1–4, free elsewhere — and was 49% more faithful to input melodies than MusicGen with 35× fewer parameters [@wu2024musiccontrolnet]; Sketch2Sound's loudness, brightness and pitch curves from a vocal gesture are the same idea (S1.2) [@floresgarcia2025sketch2sound]. Music FaderNets grounded the high-level attribute *arousal*, learned from 1% labels, in low-level rhythm and note-density faders [@tan2020fadernets], showing how "more intense here" can be compiled to controllable features. Structure enters too: melody infilling with user-provided structural context let the user say which earlier phrase a gap should echo, and structure-aware fills beat structure-agnostic baselines [@tan2022melodyinfilling]. Finally, constraints must combine: Compositional Steering reportedly composed lightweight attribute controls (pitch range, density, key) into one product-of-experts distribution over a frozen Music Transformer [@young2021compositionalsteering], and Cococo's voice lanes, semantic sliders and example slider were soft priors on Coconet's sampling that raised controllability from 3.5 to 5.9 among 21 novices [@louie2020cococo]. Read together, S2.3 already defines the schema — instrument, density, polyphony, register, chord, section, tension, dynamics curve, structural reference — and the two semantics, hard constraint or soft prior, that an annotation type must declare.

### 5.4 S2.4 Multimodal annotation as instruction

No fetched music system treats a composer's natural-language, drawn, hummed or attached marks on notation as instructions to a generator; the templates come from neighbouring media. ExpressEdit is the most complete [@tilekbay2024expressedit]. Its design was grounded in 176 multimodal edit requests from ten video editors, all using natural language and 78 of 97 visual ones sketched on frames. GPT-4 parses each request into four reference types — *temporal* ("whenever he discusses tips"), *spatial* ("top-left", or the sketch), *edit operation* and *parameters*; temporal references are resolved positionally, against the transcript or against dense visual captions by embedding similarity, spatial references by Segment-Anything crops matched to text or sketch in CLIP space. On 50 ground-truth commands the pipeline reached temporal recall 0.68, spatial mIoU 0.56 and operation F1 0.82. Crucially, output is a set of *edit suggestions* the user accepts, rejects or adjusts, with an "Examine" panel showing how each reference was interpreted. Among ten novices, 45.98% of suggestions were accepted and 58.09% of final edits were modified suggestions; time split roughly a third each into describing, examining and manual editing; SUS was 75.7, the Examine feature was rated 6.1/7, sketches appeared in 26% of commands, and vague commands failed. Substitute bar, beat and section for timecodes, staff, voice and register for screen regions, reharmonise, thin and transpose for cut, zoom and crop, and audio similarity for CLIP, and the grammar transfers almost verbatim.

Two vision-side results fix the interaction and data models. DirectGPT wrapped an LLM in direct manipulation — a continuously visible object, toolbar commands that reuse prompt syntax, drag-and-select to compose prompts, undo — and users edited text, code and vector images 50% faster with 50% fewer and 72% shorter prompts than with chat alone [@masson2024directgpt]; selection first, then instruction, is what makes a short annotation sufficient, and it is the modern form of Shneiderman's continuous, reversible, immediately visible manipulation [@shneiderman1983direct]. InstructPix2Pix trained an (image, instruction) to edited-image model on triples *synthesised* by GPT-3 and Stable Diffusion with no human labelling [@brooks2023instructpix2pix] — the recipe for bootstrapping the (score, annotation, edited score) dataset that annotation-driven symbolic editing lacks. The music-side pieces are partial. Libretto defined a text grammar with explicit onset slots, voice declarations and bar blocks, a 29-axis structural fingerprint calibrated to corpus percentiles, and an LLM agent that loops generate → measure → *musician-readable feedback* ("reduce harmonic instability") → revise, lifting gap-filling pass rate from 12% single-shot to 39% and reaching 94% on full pieces with retrieval and refinement [@xu2026libretto]; the feedback is generated by metrics, but it has exactly the form a composer's margin note would take. Not that Groove showed that general LLMs can edit a drum groove in a compact text notation from specific, descriptive and stylistic instructions, verified by expert-authored unit tests that agreed with professionals at 89% TPR and 94% TNR, with the best model passing 68% [@zhang2025groove] — test-based verification is the right tool for edit checking. DAWZY converted typed, spoken or hummed requests into reversible REAPER actions through three MCP tools; its 21-participant study rated enjoyment 4.48/5 but control lowest at 3.81, flagging the need for direct-manipulation fallbacks [@elkins2025dawzy]. MuMu-LLaMA accepted prompt-based edits alongside any input modality, at the audio level [@liu2024mumullama], and Decomposer's decompilation of MIDI into editable Strudel programs supplies a programmatic target on which such instructions could operate (S3.6) [@kim2026decomposer]. Two non-AI precedents complete the picture: EuterPen already hosts ink, pictures and audio clips between staves with no reader behind them [@cavez2025euterpen], and REAL, a small agentic video pipeline, contributed the anchoring rule that "the word is the identity, the timestamp is derived" — placements pinned to semantic events, timing re-derived at compile time, every quantity badged estimated or measured [@thisgoober2026real]. For music the event is the bar, beat or note, and the timestamp follows from the tempo map at compile time.

### 5.5 S2.5 Economics of human labelling

One targeted human annotation can outperform full automation by a wide margin. Just Label the Repeats aligned real performance audio to scanned sheet music with improved measure detection and onset-probability features, and replaced automatic detection of repeats and jumps with a clickable interface in which the human labels the repeat signs; measure-level alignment accuracy rose from 33% to 82% [@bukey2024justlabel]. The same economics recur throughout the sections above: Amuse's editable keywords [@kim2025amuse], Dubler's per-user trained sounds [@vochlea2021dubler], Sonic Visualiser's auto-annotate-then-correct layers [@cannam2010sonicvisualiser], and AugmentedNet's 46% full-label accuracy that makes correction essential [@napoleslopez2021augmentednet]. The design rule is to ask the human for the few labels that are cheap for a musician and hard for a model — repeats, section boundaries, key, the phrase a gap should echo — and automate the rest.

### 5.6 Implications for the studio

The annotation layer should reuse rather than invent. The record shape is already fixed by Dezrann, MEI and Humdrum: a typed label anchored in symbolic time (measure and beat, or a note id), with optional span, staff scope, author, lifetime and free text [@giraud2018dezrann; @pugin2014verovio; @huron1995humdrum; @fu2026tactus]. Harmonic instructions can borrow the DCML regex grammar or RomanText syntax directly [@neuwirth2018abc; @gotham2019wheninrome]; Verovio's id-addressable SVG makes click-to-annotate on an engraved score feasible, and MEI's editorial vocabulary can mark AI proposals as variants distinct from the composer's text [@pugin2014verovio; @goebl2023meifriend]. Because MNX is unfinished and MusicXML's annotation vocabulary is minimal, a studio-internal annotation model with MusicXML 4.0 interchange is justified [@w3c2021musicxml40]. The control-vocabulary literature then supplies the *types*: instrument, density, polyphony, register, chord, section label, tension, dynamics or rhythm curve, structural reference, example — each declared as a hard constraint enforced at decode time or a soft prior on sampling [@vonrutte2023figaro; @chen2024sympac; @thickstun2024anticipatory; @louie2020cococo]. Analysis models should populate this layer proactively but tentatively, at the ~46% harmonic and ~93% phrase-boundary accuracies now achievable, so that the composer corrects rather than authors from nothing [@napoleslopez2021augmentednet; @dai2024interconnections; @kim2023allinone].

The open gap is *annotations as prompts on notation*. No system combines ExpressEdit's reference grammar — when, where, what, how — with resolvers for musical modalities and inspectable, editable suggestions [@tilekbay2024expressedit]; DirectGPT's selection-then-instruction pattern has not been applied to a score [@masson2024directgpt]; Libretto's structural feedback loop is driven by metrics, not by a composer's margin notes [@xu2026libretto]; EuterPen's multimodal marks have no reader [@cavez2025euterpen]; and the (score, annotation, edited score) training triples that InstructPix2Pix's synthetic recipe could produce do not exist [@brooks2023instructpix2pix]. A music annotation compiler that unifies these pieces — anchored to events with derived timing [@thisgoober2026real], verified by unit-test-style checks [@zhang2025groove] and voice-leading linters [@huang2005palestrinapal], and explaining how each mark was interpreted [@tilekbay2024expressedit; @liang2024drawlody] — is the studio's most defensible research contribution, and the primitive on which the compile (S3) and edit (S4) stages depend.

## 6. Compile I: symbolic generation, infilling and arrangement (S3.1–S3.7)

The compile stage is where the studio's thesis is tested. Given human-authored material — a melody, a chord chart, a few bars of one part — and annotations on it, the system must produce a fuller realisation that the composer can read, audition and change. The symbolic sub-tree of S3 is the natural home for this operation because its outputs are notation or MIDI, which are directly editable, rather than audio, which is not (D7). This section surveys seven nodes: symbolic encodings (S3.1); infilling and constrained regeneration, the primitive that implements "edit → compile" (S3.2); steering by attributes, text and rules (S3.3); structure-aware pipelines that behave like compilers with intermediate representations (S3.4); harmonisation, accompaniment and arrangement, including the commercial arranger engines that are the historical form of "compile" (S3.5); music LLMs and the music-as-code lineage that makes the metaphor literal (S3.6); and expressive performance rendering as the separable stage between score and sound (S3.7). Audio rendering (S3.8), text-to-song (S3.9) and understanding models as critics (S3.10) follow in the next section. Throughout, works are read against task framing (D2), control granularity (D3), editability of the intermediate (D7) and integration surface (D8).

### 6.1 S3.1 Symbolic representations and tokenizations

Four representational families have emerged since 2016, and the choice among them determines what a composer can later edit. The first is the MIDI-like performance event stream — NOTE_ON, NOTE_OFF, TIME_SHIFT, VELOCITY — over which Music Transformer generated minute-long piano continuations using a relative self-attention whose memory grows linearly rather than quadratically with length; it has no bars or beats, models what a pianist played rather than what a score says, and produced pieces roughly four times longer than the preceding recurrent models at state-of-the-art likelihood on Piano-e-Competition data [@huang2018musictransformer]. The second family imposes a metrical grid. REMI added explicit Bar, Position (sixteen per bar), Tempo, Chord (60 types), Note-On, Velocity (32 bins) and Duration tokens so that a Transformer-XL trained on transcribed pop piano "knows" where beats fall, and objective beat-salience metrics and a listening study found better rhythmic and harmonic coherence than MIDI-like tokens [@huang2020popmusictransformer]. Compound Word Transformer grouped the tokens of one time step into a typed "compound word" with a note family (pitch, duration, velocity) and a metric family (bar/position, tempo, chord), each predicted by its own head, converging five to ten times faster than REMI and fitting a full pop song of roughly ten thousand REMI tokens into a linear Transformer [@hsiao2021compoundword]. OctupleMIDI packed eight fields (bar, position, instrument, pitch, duration, velocity, time signature, tempo) into one token per note for MusicBERT's bar-masked pre-training on more than a million songs [@zeng2021musicbert; @chou2021midibert]; as understanding models these belong to the annotation stage (S2.2). The Multitrack Music Transformer's six-tuple per note yielded 2.6–3.5 times more music per token than MMM or REMI+ across 64 instruments [@dong2023mmt], while MMM serialised each track's bars as their own sequence and concatenated tracks so that one bar of one track could be masked and regenerated [@ens2020mmm]. Aria, trained on the 1.19-million-file Aria-MIDI corpus, used absolute onsets within five-second segments instead of cumulative time-shifts to avoid drift in long performances [@bradshaw2025ariamidi; @bradshaw2025aria].

The third family is text. folk-rnn trained a character-level LSTM on roughly 23,000 Irish session tunes in ABC and generated complete tunes as text that rendered directly to notation [@sturm2016folkrnn], founding the lineage every "music LLM" now follows. ABC is a compact plain-text format with header fields for metre, unit length and key and a body of note letters with pitch and duration modifiers; abc2midi compiles it to MIDI, abcm2ps to engraving, and abcjs re-renders it in the browser on every keystroke [@walshaw2011abc]. ChatMusician treated ABC as "a second language" for a 7B LLaMA model [@yuan2024chatmusician], and MuPT's Synchronized Multi-Track ABC interleaves bars of the same index across voices with `<|>` delimiters so multi-part alignment survives tokenisation; in MuPT's controlled comparison ABC outperformed MIDI-style tokens as an LLM target [@qu2024mupt]. The fourth family is image-like: the piano roll used by Coconet's convolutional chorale model [@huang2017coconet], Polyffusion's eight-bar diffusion segments [@min2023polyffusion], Whole-Song's cascaded diffusion [@wang2024wholesong] and GETMusic's GETScore, which stacks tracks vertically with pitch and duration rows per track [@lv2023getmusic]. Piano rolls make "select a region and regenerate it" a literal spatial mask, which is why they dominate diffusion-based infilling.

Le et al. mapped these trade-offs along time-slice versus event-based and elementary versus composite tokens, and discussed the data-scale disparity between text and music and the limits of the music-as-language analogy [@le2025nlpsurvey]; Ji et al. classified by representation, algorithm and task [@ji2023survey]. MidiTok implements REMI, REMI+, MIDI-Like, TSD, Structured, CPWord, Octuple, MuMIDI, MMM and PerTok behind one MIT-licensed API with sub-word training, and reported that byte-pair encoding speeds up and improves symbolic models [@fradet2021miditok]; symusic, its C++20 I/O backend, parses MIDI and ABC "hundreds of times faster" than the legacy Python stack [@liao2024symusic] (see B2).

One finding deserves emphasis because it is an absence: no verified system generates MusicXML or MEI natively. Generation lives in MIDI tokens, ABC or piano rolls, and even MetaScore, the largest score-origin dataset (963,000 MuseScore files with genre, composer, complexity and instrumentation metadata and LLM-generated captions), converts scores *out* to REMI+ and MMT-style tokens before its generators see them [@xu2025metascore]. MusicXML 4.0 remains the interchange standard for more than 250 applications while its JSON successor MNX was still unsettled in mid-2026 [@w3c2021musicxml40]. A lossless bridge between an LLM-friendly textual intermediate and engraving-grade notation is therefore both a prerequisite for a notation-first studio and an open research problem (Part D, gap 2).

### 6.2 S3.2 Infilling and constrained regeneration

Infilling — mask a region at note, bar, track or span granularity, hold everything else fixed and regenerate the mask — turns a generative model into an editing tool and is the primitive the studio's loop depends on (D2). Its modern formulation is Coconet, an orderless NADE over a four-voice piano roll trained to predict arbitrary masked notes and sampled with blocked Gibbs "rewriting", so that any subset of a score can be held fixed and the rest inpainted. Huang et al. motivated the design by observing that "human composers write music in a nonlinear fashion, scribbling motifs here and there, often revisiting choices previously made," and found that Gibbs rewriting beat ancestral sampling on likelihood and human evaluation because iterative revision corrects poorly modelled conditionals [@huang2017coconet]. Re-implemented in TensorFlow.js at about 400 KB, the same model powered the Bach Doodle, which harmonised user-drawn melodies for more than 55 million queries in three days and yielded a CC BY 4.0 dataset of 21.6 million harmonisations [@huang2019bachdoodle]. DeepBach applied pseudo-Gibbs resampling to per-voice bidirectional LSTMs and — uniquely for its era — shipped as a MuseScore plugin in which a composer selected a region and requested re-harmonisation; in a discrimination test with roughly 1,270 participants its outputs were judged "Bach" nearly as often as real chorales [@hadjeres2017deepbach]. Infilling Piano Performances reportedly extended hole-filling to the expressive performance-event domain [@ippolito2018infilling]; InpaintNet reframed a monophonic gap as a path through VAE latent space [@pati2019inpaintnet]; and Music SketchNet factorised pitch and rhythm so a user could sketch a partial contour or rhythm for the missing measures and have both honoured [@chen2020sketchnet] (see S1.3).

Multi-track, bar-scoped infilling begins with MMM's BarFill variant, which let a user choose which tracks and bars to regenerate, each track's instrument and a density level [@ens2020mmm]. Chang et al. adapted XLNet's permutation language model to fill gaps of variable length (up to 128 notes) in polyphonic piano, with a relative-bar positional encoding so the fill lands on the right beat [@chang2021xlnet]. MusIAC combined track-level tokens (note density, polyphony rate, occupation rate) with bar-level tokens for tonal tension — tensile strain and cloud diameter from the spiral array — and found that these improved perceived coherence over density alone; tension is the first composer-meaningful bar-level handle beyond density [@guo2022musiac].

The Anticipatory Music Transformer supplies the most general mechanism. Anticipation interleaves *control* events — a fixed melody, or any subset of notes the user wants preserved — into the event stream slightly *before* the moment they occur, so an ordinary autoregressive model learns to condition asynchronously on them. Because the controls are a subset of the notes themselves, one model trained on Lakh MIDI performs prompted continuation, span infilling and accompaniment given a fixed part; Thickstun et al. reported likelihoods comparable to plain autoregressive baselines and human ratings of twenty-second accompaniments comparable in musicality to human-composed music, and released 128M- to 780M-parameter checkpoints under Apache 2.0 [@thickstun2024anticipatory]. MIDInfinite ported the model to the browser through MLC and WebGPU at 51 notes per second on an M3 MacBook Pro, running faster than playback 72.9 percent of the time (86.3 percent with two seconds of buffering), which shows that an infilling model of this class can run client-side [@zhou2024midinfinite].

Two lines carry infilling into the DAW (D8). Composer's Assistant is a T5-style encoder–decoder (d = 384, ten plus ten layers) wired into REAPER: the composer creates empty MIDI items on named tracks to indicate which track-measures to fill. Version 1 offered per-track mono/poly tokens and implicit chord or rhythm conditioning through guide tracks, evaluated with 25 participants [@malandro2023composersassistant]; version 2 added two rhythmic-conditioning modes, horizontal and vertical note-onset density, pitch controls and a "rhythmic interest" control, and found no significant listener-rated difference between fully human excerpts and excerpts co-completed with the tool. It was trained only on permissively licensed MIDI and released under CC BY 4.0 [@malandro2024composersassistant2]. The Metacreation Lab's lineage ran from MMM through Calliope, a web app that batch-generates up to a thousand candidates for selected bars and tracks [@tchemeube2022calliope], to MMM-C, a one-parameter Cubase plugin whose 18 expert composers rated ease-of-control at 5.2/10 and their desire for more control at 9.5/10 [@tchemeube2023mmmc], and to MMM4Live for Ableton [@metacreation2023mmm4live]. MIDI-GPT consolidated this into a roughly 20-million-parameter, six-layer Transformer with a 2,048-token context that infills at bar and track level for all 128 GM instruments, conditioned on instrument, ten instrument-relative density levels, polyphony range, a five-bin duration range and a MusicMap style ontology; trained on GigaMIDI, it ships inside Steinberg Cubase, an Ableton Live plugin, the Teenage Engineering OP-Z, Elias game-audio middleware and Calliope [@pasquier2025midigpt]. MIDI-RWKV addressed offline inference and personalisation together: a compact linear-time RWKV-7 model for long-context multi-track infilling on edge devices, with *state tuning* (fine-tuning only the initial recurrent state) for style adaptation from a handful of a composer's own files, released under CC BY 4.0 and framed around refining existing work [@zhouzheng2025midirwkv].

Diffusion models make masks spatial. Polyffusion separated *internal* control (an inpainting mask over an eight-bar piano roll — keep the melody and fill the accompaniment, or fill arbitrary regions) from *external* control (cross-attention on chord or texture embeddings); 36 listeners rated it significantly above Transformer baselines and it produced zero invalid notes in 160 samples [@min2023polyffusion]. GETMusic's discrete diffusion denoises masked target tracks given intact source tracks, so any of the 665 source→target combinations over six tracks, and zero-shot infilling of arbitrary cells, work from one model [@lv2023getmusic]. RefinPaint closed the loop from the other side: a feedback model identifies which notes are weakest in context ("where to modify") and an inpainting model resamples them ("how"), iterated as proofreading; 15 annotators preferred it at 50, 30 and 10 percent fragment sizes and four amateur composers reported improved drafts [@ramoneda2024refinpaint] (see S4.2, S4.4).

Two systems bracket the history of deployed infilling. FlowComposer (2016) was a web lead-sheet editor in which the user chose a style corpus from more than 12,000 lead sheets, entered a partial melody or chords, and had Markov chains with metrical constraints fill the rest; any region could be regenerated alone, with sliders for harmonic conformance, "inspiration" from the current piece versus the corpus, note duration and chord-change rate. Used on Benoît Carré's "Daddy's Car" and the musical *Beyond the Fence*, it produced the observation that composers rarely generated more than eight bars at a time, using the tool for fragments inside longer human compositions [@papadopoulos2016flowcomposer]. Hookpad Aria (2024) is its modern counterpart: a copilot inside Hooktheory's web lead-sheet editor, built on the anticipatory approach and trained on the crowd-annotated Hooktheory corpus, offering continuation, infilling of spans in the middle of existing material, and harmony-from-melody and melody-from-harmony. From its March 2024 beta it generated an acceptance rate of about 23 percent (the figures are given under E3) — the only acceptance telemetry for a notation-surface generative tool found in this review [@donahue2024hookpadaria] (see E3).

### 6.3 S3.3 Attribute-, text- and rule-conditioned symbolic generation

Where infilling controls what stays, conditioning controls what changes. The earliest handle was the latent space: MusicVAE's hierarchical "conductor" decoder emitted one embedding per bar before decoding each bar, avoiding posterior collapse for sixteen-bar melodies, drums and trios, and exposed sampling, interpolation between clips and attribute vectors as user operations [@roberts2018musicvae]. Compositional Steering reportedly combined several lightweight attribute controls into a product-of-experts distribution over a frozen Music Transformer at sampling time [@young2021compositionalsteering]. Music FaderNets disentangled low-level faders (rhythm and note density) in a VAE and learned the high-level attribute *arousal* semi-supervised from one percent of labels, enabling arousal transfer on video-game MIDI [@tan2020fadernets]. MuseMorphose encoded each bar of an existing pop-piano piece and decoded it under bar-level rhythmic-intensity and polyphony settings (eight levels each), rewriting a whole song's texture while preserving its content and beating recurrent baselines on fidelity, control accuracy and diversity [@wu2023musemorphose].

A second line makes the condition legible. FIGARO's "description-to-sequence" model conditions a Transformer emitting REMI+ on a per-bar description of expert features (instruments, chords, time signature, note density, mean pitch, velocity and duration) plus learned VQ-VAE codes; a composer can edit the description — change bar 9's chords, add an instrument, raise density — and regenerate, and swapping learned codes yields style transfer [@vonrutte2023figaro]. MuseCoco split text-to-music into text-to-attribute understanding over about twelve attributes (instrument, rhythm, bar count, time signature, key, tempo, pitch range, emotion, genre) and attribute-to-music generation with a 1.2-billion-parameter decoder, so users can bypass text and set attributes directly; it reported roughly 20 percent higher control accuracy than baselines [@lu2023musecoco]. Text2midi paired a frozen FLAN-T5 encoder with an 18-layer decoder (272M parameters, 159M trainable) fine-tuned on the 168,000-pair MidiCaps; it beat MuseCoco on tempo (65.8 vs 54.6 percent) and key accuracy (35.6 vs 14.6 percent) but eleven listeners rated it below ground truth and it struggled with instrumentation within a 2,048-token context [@bhandari2025text2midi]. MetaScore's tag-conditioned generator offered categorical control as an alternative to captions, rated comparable to Text2midi by 22 participants [@xu2025metascore]. Diff-Symbo, an autoregressive latent-diffusion model for long text-conditioned multi-track music trained with 19,345 LLM-generated templates, reported gains over GPT-4, MuseCoco and MMT but appeared in August 2026 without peer review [@lin2026diffsymbo]. MIDI-LLM expanded Llama-3.2-1B's vocabulary with about 55,000 MIDI tokens in the anticipatory encoding, trained in two stages (continued pre-training on music text and MIDI, then supervised fine-tuning on pairs) while preserving language ability and vLLM-compatible inference, reported a FAD of 0.173 against 0.818 for Text2midi with four- to fourteen-fold faster inference, and in an in-the-wild study with 58 musicians and roughly 4,000 generations found higher acceptance for lead-sheet generation [@wu2025midillm]. The recurring lesson is that text should be compiled to an explicit, inspectable attribute layer the composer can override — an argument against opaque text-to-song that the vocabulary in S2.3 makes concrete.

A third line adds constraints to a frozen generator without retraining — the right mechanism when the set of annotations will grow. Stochastic Control Guidance steers a pre-trained latent diffusion model toward *non-differentiable* rules — pitch-class histogram, horizontal and vertical density, chord progression — by forward-evaluating the rule on candidates at each step; it improved adherence and quality over classifier guidance and supports editing an existing piece within a chosen time window [@huang2024scg]. LC-Diff kept a frozen unconditional VAE and trained small conditional diffusion priors on its latent space as plug-in constraints over note density, pitch range, melodic contour and rhythmic complexity for monophonic melodies (10 million sequences from 176,000 files), with Pearson correlations above 0.8 between requested and realised values for every attribute [@petteno2025lcdiff]. Across three generations — product-of-experts, latent priors, rule-guided diffusion — a new annotation type becomes a function rather than a retraining run.

### 6.4 S3.4 Structure-aware and hierarchical pipelines

Long-range form is where flat sequence models fail and where a compiler with intermediate representations is most needed. Music Transformer's relative attention was motivated by structure — relative position lets the model recognise a motif transposed or displaced — and its samples showed repeated motifs and ABA-like form over a minute [@huang2018musictransformer]. Museformer built a musical prior into attention: each bar attends finely to "structure-related" bars one, two, four, eight, twelve, sixteen, twenty-four and thirty-two bars back, chosen from bar-similarity statistics, and coarsely to summaries of the rest, modelling sequences more than three times longer than full attention with repetition curves closer to human music and higher ratings for long-term structure, though with no infilling or controls [@yu2022museformer].

Dai and Dannenberg's line makes structure explicit and editable. MusicFrameworks decomposed melody composition into section and phrase structure, a "basic melody" of one note per beat, a rhythm structure and a realised melody, with a network per stage, so that a user could alter chords, basic melody or rhythm and regenerate downstream; listeners preferred the results to POP909's human melodies about half the time [@dai2021controllable]. "What is missing in deep music generation?" established four principles from Chinese and American pop corpora — hierarchical structure, song-specific repetition with a limited vocabulary, interaction between structure and rhythm, melody and harmony, and non-random repetition trends measurable by cross-entropy — and found that recent deep generators "often reveal striking differences from a structural perspective" [@dai2022missing]. Their analyser reached 93 percent phrase-boundary accuracy on 909 pop transcriptions and quantified priors a compile-time checker could enforce: repeated phrases covered 50–90 percent of most songs and V–I cadences closed 94 percent of sections against 47 percent elsewhere [@dai2024interconnections]. Personalised imitation analysed a user-chosen reference first, then generated a new song constrained to its structural template [@dai2023personalised].

Whole-Song Hierarchical Generation is the most literal compiler in the corpus: four cascaded piano-roll diffusion models generate a pop song top-down — form and key, a reduced lead sheet, a lead sheet, and accompaniment — each conditioned on the level above and each an interpretable object the human can edit before the next level is compiled, with external encoders steering chords, rhythm and texture; a human study rated its full-song structure above flat baselines [@wang2024wholesong]. Melody Infilling with User-Provided Structural Context showed the idea at the scale of a gap: an attention-selecting module lets the composer say which earlier phrase the missing segment should echo, and structure-conditioned fills beat structure-agnostic ones [@tan2022melodyinfilling]. SymPAC trained a multi-track model solely on about one million audio tracks auto-transcribed for beats, chords, sections and instruments, controlled through *prompt bars* — a leading block of genre, section labels, tempo, chords and track list the user may fill fully or partially — and finite-state-machine *constrained decoding* that guarantees the output obeys both the grammar and the user's constraints; twelve MIR researchers and producers rated it significantly above FIGARO and MMT on coherence, arrangement and structure [@chen2024sympac]. Hard constraints at decode time are what a composer expects when writing "Cmaj7 here". Libretto made structure the medium of an agent loop: a text grammar with explicit onset slots, voice declarations and bar blocks, plus a 29-axis "structural fingerprint" calibrated to corpus percentiles, lets an LLM generate, measure, receive musician-readable feedback ("reduce harmonic instability") and revise; gap-filling pass rates rose from 12 percent single-shot to 39 percent with the loop, and full-piece generation reached 94 percent with retrieval and refinement [@xu2026libretto].

### 6.5 S3.5 Harmonization, accompaniment and arrangement

The lead sheet is the natural "source code" of popular music, and the operations that realise it — harmonise a melody, write a melody over chords, produce a piano texture, orchestrate for a band — are the compile steps a songwriter most wants automated and least wants to lose control of. Chordripple, Huang's earliest system, recommended next or replacement chords ranked by a learned chord embedding so suggestions were plausible yet "beyond the ordinary", leaving authorship with the novice; the notes record an IUI 2016 study without re-verified details [@huang2016chordripple]. Music Transformer's melody-to-accompaniment mode was the earliest instance in this corpus of a neural "compile a lead sheet into a piano part" [@huang2018musictransformer].

Gus Xia's Music X Lab built the most complete pipeline. AccoMontage turned a lead sheet into a full-length piano accompaniment by retrieving accompaniment *phrases* with dynamic programming over fitness and transition smoothness, then re-harmonising them to the target chords through a chord–texture disentangled VAE [@zhao2021accomontage]; AccoMontage2 added melody harmonisation and a GUI for chord style (Pop, R&B, Dark), texture density and rhythmic complexity, and 72 participants rated coherence and structure significantly above learning-only baselines (p < 0.05), musicality marginally (p = 0.053) [@yi2022accomontage2]. Retrieving human-made material and adapting it to the composer's chords is compositionally transparent, and the phrase database could be the composer's own past work. Q&A learned content from the mixture and style from individual tracks and re-rendered a piece for a user-chosen set of tracks — re-instrumentation, piano cover, piano-to-band orchestration, voice separation [@zhao2023qa]. Structured Multi-Track Accompaniment Arrangement combined the two: piano arrangement by texture retrieval, then orchestration by infusing per-track *function* styles as vector-quantised codes whose long-term flow a multi-stream Transformer prior generates, yielding whole-song coherence with genre choice and hierarchical control — the closest existing system to an arranger workstation with an editable intermediate piano stage [@zhao2024structured].

Several works treat harmony as constraint satisfaction. Kaliakatsos-Papakostas et al. harmonised melodies from 17,476 Hooktheory lead sheets with BART and GPT-2 models accepting structure tokens and user-specified chords at given positions, and proposed B* decoding (beam search with A* and backtracking) to guarantee the requested chords appear; soft constraints were satisfied 50–63 percent of the time, B* 90–98 percent at roughly 400–1,900 model calls [@kaliakatsospapakostas2025harmonization]. MelodyT5 unified seven melody-centric score-to-score tasks — generation, harmonisation, melodisation, segmentation, variation and others — in one ABC encoder–decoder pre-trained on MelodyHub's 261,000 melodies, with multi-task transfer helping low-data tasks [@wu2024melodyt5]. Function Alignment joined two pretrained symbolic language models (a RoFormer trained on 357,000 Los Angeles MIDI files), one reading a reference and one writing a target, through a lightweight adapter, so chord recognition, metrical analysis, chord-conditioned melody, melody-conditioned chords and drums-to-song became one parameter-efficient "music-for-music" operation [@jiang2025functionalignment]. GETMusic's 665 track combinations belong here too [@lv2023getmusic]. On the audio side, Stemphonic generated a variable set of synchronised stems in one pass, conditioned on existing sub-mixes and per-stem *activity patterns* specifying when each instrument plays — arrangement-as-compile over audio [@wu2026stemphonic] (see S3.8).

The commercial arranger engines are the baselines and define what "compile from chords" has meant for three decades (see F3). The Korg i3 (1993) established the pro-arranger category with 48 styles, each with variations, fills, intros and endings, driven by left-hand chord recognition into drums, bass and accompaniment parts, plus a backing sequencer that captured the result as an editable song [@korg1993i3]. Band-in-a-Box has since 1990 turned chord symbols typed into a bar grid plus a style into a complete arrangement, from MIDI styles or from RealTracks — recordings of session musicians exceeding 4,400 hours in the 2026 release — with a notation view and DAW export [@pgmusic2026biab]. Logic Pro 11 (May 2024) brought the idea into a mainstream DAW: Session Players — Drummer plus a new Bass Player (eight players with complexity and intensity controls) and Keyboard Player (four styles) — follow the Chord Track and are editable as ordinary MIDI regions, with no published evaluation [@apple2024logicpro11]. All three render parts from chords, style, key and tempo deterministically and editably; none accepts a sketch, a hummed line or an example recording, and none round-trips cleanly to notation. That gap is the studio's opening, and the arranger's virtues — instant, deterministic, parameterised, editable — are the bar a learned successor must clear.

### 6.6 S3.6 Music LLMs and music-as-code

The compile metaphor has a literal history in computer music into which the music LLMs fit. LilyPond compiles a text source into engraved PDF, SVG and MIDI under classical engraving rules, extensible in Scheme and GPL-licensed; its pain points — edit–compile latency and error messages phrased in terms of source rather than score — are exactly what a live, projectional studio should fix [@nienhuys2003lilypond]. The ABC toolchain is the lightweight equivalent [@walshaw2011abc]. Nyquist made scores into programs with temporal combinators such as `sim` and `seq`, and became Audacity's scripting layer [@dannenberg1997nyquist]. The audio languages offer two compile philosophies — Csound's separation of an orchestra (instruments) from a score (events), and the live re-evaluation of SuperCollider and ChucK, whose "strongly timed" design lets code be replaced while sound continues — while Faust's compilation of one DSL to C++, LLVM, WebAssembly and plugins shows that a single musical IR can target many outputs [@wang2015chuck]. Live coding is the most developed practice of music as continuously recompiled code: TidalCycles' mini-notation, Strudel's browser port with inline visualisations of what each pattern produced, Sonic Pi's `live_loop`, and Gibber's annotation of running code with live values [@mclean2014tidal; @aaron2016sonicpi]. The computer-aided-composition environments are composers' own compile tools: OpenMusic's visual Lisp patches compute structures displayed in editable notation and piano-roll editors on a timeline "maquette" (OM 8.0 appeared in March 2026), and bach brings the same into Max in real time with `bach.roll` and `bach.score` objects editable by mouse or by patching [@assayag1999openmusic]. Their dual editing — edit the program or edit the output — is the central unanswered UX question for the studio: which edits round-trip? Programming-languages research adds three pillars: Hazel's semantics for *incomplete* programs, in which evaluation proceeds around typed holes so every editor state yields feedback; MPS's projectional editing of one syntax tree in several notations; and Tanimoto's liveness levels [@omar2019hazel]. A score with a `[to be composed]` region should likewise still compile and play. music21 (BSD-3) is the conversion and analysis backbone between MusicXML, MEI, kern, ABC, MIDI and RomanText [@cuthbert2010music21], and Nierhaus' catalogue of Markov, grammar, evolutionary and cellular paradigms records what a rule-based backend can do without machine learning [@nierhaus2009algorithmic] (see F1).

The music LLMs write these textual scores directly. ChatMusician continually pre-trained LLaMA-2-7B on the 4-billion-token MusicPile to read and write ABC, conditioning on chords, motifs, form, style and text; it beat GPT-4 on the authors' composition tasks, yet on its own MusicTheoryBench all LLMs including GPT-4 were reported near chance on *reasoning* (a figure recalled rather than re-verified; see E6) [@yuan2024chatmusician]. MuPT scaled ABC pre-training to 190M–4.23B-parameter decoders over 33.6 billion tokens with an 8,192-token context and proposed a symbolic-music scaling law under data repetition [@qu2024mupt]. NotaGen applied the full LLM recipe to classical scores — pre-training on 1.6 million pieces, fine-tuning on about 9,000 high-quality works, then reinforcement with CLaMP-DPO, which uses the CLaMP 2 music–text model as an automatic judge without human labels — under a period–composer–instrumentation prompt; A/B tests favoured it over baselines and in some comparisons over human pieces [@wang2025notagen]. MelodyT5 is the score-to-score counterpart [@wu2024melodyt5]. Kumar et al. compared off-the-shelf LLMs with SFT- and DPO-adapted variants and a music-specialised baseline on ABC generation and understanding, finding that adaptation choices materially change outcomes and that standard metrics behave idiosyncratically for music [@kumar2026howfar]. The benchmarks are sobering: ZIQI-Eval's 14,000-plus questions found all sixteen tested LLMs "poor" [@li2024ziqieval], and ABC-Eval's 1,086 items found frontier models above 90 percent on ABC syntax but near random on sequence-level understanding [@zhao2025abceval] (see E6). Zero-shot natural-language *editing* was reliable only in a narrow domain: Not that Groove had eight LLMs edit drum grooves in a compact text notation from specific, descriptive and stylistic instructions, verified by expert-authored unit tests that agreed with professional musicians (89 percent true-positive, 94 percent true-negative), and the best model passed 68 percent [@zhang2025groove]. Test-based verification of edits is the transferable lesson.

Agentic architectures route around these limits. COSMIC (2021) reportedly parsed natural-language requests into intents routed to melody and lyric generators [@zhang2021cosmic]; MusicAgent had ChatGPT decompose requests into sub-tasks executed by tools from Hugging Face, GitHub and web APIs, without a human-editable intermediate [@yu2023musicagent]. ComposerX assigned six GPT-4 agents the roles of leader, melody, harmony, instrument, reviewer and arrangement to write polyphonic ABC from a brief with a review loop, reporting 98.2 percent generation success, a 0.77 preference for multi-agent over single-agent, and 32.2 percent of outputs judged human-composed against 55.4 percent for real pieces, with rater counts unreported [@deng2024composerx]. Libretto's measure-and-revise loop is the structure-aware member of the family [@xu2026libretto].

Decomposer runs the metaphor in reverse and is, for this project, the most consequential recent result. Kim et al. post-trained Qwen3-8B to convert MIDI into executable Strudel programs that re-render the input while exposing repeated patterns, voices, harmony and timing as editable code. Stage one was supervised fine-tuning on STRUDEL-SYNTH, 21,174 (Strudel, MIDI) pairs distilled from Claude Opus 4.6 — about thirty times the 688 public Strudel programs. Stage two was reinforcement learning with execution-based rewards computed from the input MIDI and the program alone, so unpaired MIDI could be used: a *faithfulness* reward (render and compare to the input) and a *readability* reward (penalise note-by-note transcription). The model compiled 99 percent of the time against 75–82 percent for frontier LLMs, reached onset F1 of 0.60 on Lakh against 0.27–0.28 for GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.6 and Gemini 3.5 Flash (0.58 against 0.21–0.23 on multi-instrument inputs), scored 0.61–0.74 on a readability rubric against 0.09 for a heuristic converter and 0.29–0.36 for frontier models, and generalised to GigaMIDI, Nottingham and NES-MDB; ablations exposed a faithfulness–readability trade-off and showed that RL without SFT plateaued. There was no user study [@kim2026decomposer]. Decomposer supplies a candidate intermediate representation in which human edits are semantic — change a loop, a transposition, a voice — and compilation is deterministic rendering; a forward model from program plus annotations to new program would close the loop.

### 6.7 S3.7 Expressive performance rendering

Between a finished score and sound lies a stage the studio can keep separate and editable: rendering notation into expressive timing, dynamics and articulation. VirtuosoNet took a MusicXML score with its dynamics, articulation and tempo markings and produced performance MIDI (tempo, velocity, onset deviation, articulation, pedal) through a hierarchical attention score encoder, a conditional-VAE style latent and a measure-to-note decoder, trained on 226 pieces and 1,052 Yamaha e-Competition performances aligned to scores; five piano students rated three pieces on seven criteria with significant gains over prior systems [@jeong2019virtuosonet]. It established score markings as inputs to a learnable rendering stage. ScorePerformer introduced SPMuple tokens (eight score and four performance fields), an MMD-VAE Transformer with style embeddings at global, bar, beat and onset level, and *direction-marking classifiers* mapping words such as crescendo, staccato and ritardando to latent deltas, so a user can steer rendering with score-like directions at any granularity, trained on ASAP's 212 pieces and 937 performances [@borovik2023scoreperformer]. Its successor PianoFlow applied flow matching to 2,968 hours of aligned score–performance MIDI with emotion and free-text controls at 355 notes per second; 26 listeners gave 708 pairwise ratings with a 67.1 percent win rate, preferring it over human recordings in 54 percent of comparisons [@borovik2025symupe]. DExter modelled performance parameters with diffusion conditioned on the score and on perceptual mid-level features, enabling steering and style transfer between performers [@zhang2024dexter]; RenderBox rendered score plus text ("play it tenderly, slightly rushed") directly to multi-instrument audio with curriculum learning from synthesis to expressive performance [@zhang2025renderbox] (see S3.8). Infilling Piano Performances showed that hole-filling applies to expressive data as well as notes [@ippolito2018infilling].

MIDI-DDSP is the clearest published instance of a compiler with intermediate representations. Its hierarchy — notes to per-note expression parameters (volume, vibrato, brightness, attack) to DDSP synthesis parameters to audio — lets a user intervene at any level, writing a note, dragging an expression curve or editing a synthesis parameter while learned priors fill the rest; listening tests on URMP instruments rated its realism close to real recordings, and the expression controls were verified independently [@wu2022mididdsp]. The expression layer is an annotation surface between notation and sound. Underneath sits partitura, an Apache-2.0 library that parses MusicXML, MEI, kern and MIDI into a structured score, aligns scores to performances through Match files, extracts performance features and underlies the Basis Mixer renderer, the ACCompanion score follower and ChordGNN, still released in 2026 [@cancinochacon2022partitura]. For a studio in which the composer plays a passage on a stage keyboard to show how a phrase should feel, score–performance alignment is what turns that playing into an annotation.

### 6.8 Implications for the studio

The work in S3.1–S3.7 shows that the compile step need not be invented; it must be assembled from primitives that exist in mature form and connected to a representation none of them yet shares. Infilling at bar-by-track granularity with everything else as a hard constraint is available in small, deployable models — MIDI-GPT at 20 million parameters inside Cubase, MIDI-RWKV on edge devices with state tuning to a composer's style, the Anticipatory Music Transformer running client-side — and anticipation in particular offers a single formulation of "everything I wrote is a constraint; fill the rest" that Hookpad Aria validated with a 23 percent acceptance rate on a notation surface [@thickstun2024anticipatory; @pasquier2025midigpt; @zhouzheng2025midirwkv; @donahue2024hookpadaria]. The control vocabulary these systems accept — instrument, horizontal and vertical density, polyphony, pitch and duration range, chord per beat, section label, tension, style — has converged across FIGARO, MusIAC, Composer's Assistant 2, MIDI-GPT and SymPAC and is exactly what a composer would annotate; it should be adopted as the studio's annotation schema rather than reinvented (see S2.3). Two further lessons are architectural. Hierarchy works: Whole-Song's four editable levels, MusicFrameworks' skeleton-to-surface cascade and Music X Lab's lead sheet → piano texture → orchestral function pipeline show that a compiler whose intermediates are themselves musical objects gives the composer a place to intervene at every stage and yields the long-range structure Dai and Dannenberg showed flat generators lack [@wang2024wholesong; @dai2021controllable; @zhao2024structured; @dai2022missing]. And constraints should be hard where the composer means them to be — SymPAC's constrained decoding and the B* harmoniser satisfy chord constraints 90–98 percent of the time where soft conditioning manages 50–63 percent — while steering should be retrofittable, as Stochastic Control Guidance and LC-Diff show, so a new annotation type becomes a function rather than a retraining run [@chen2024sympac; @kaliakatsospapakostas2025harmonization; @huang2024scg; @petteno2025lcdiff].

The music-as-code lineage settles what "compile" means. LilyPond, Nyquist, Csound, OpenMusic and the live-coding languages establish that a composition can be a program whose rendering is deterministic and whose edits are semantic, and Hazel's typed holes and MPS's projectional editing describe how an incomplete score should still play and how one musical model should be viewable as notation, piano roll, chord chart and text at once [@nienhuys2003lilypond; @assayag1999openmusic; @mclean2014tidal; @omar2019hazel]. Decomposer shows that a learned model can produce such a program from MIDI with 99 percent compile success and far higher faithfulness than frontier LLMs, which makes a programmatic intermediate (D7) a realistic design choice [@kim2026decomposer]. The LLM evidence cuts both ways: specialised ABC models compose well, while ZIQI-Eval, ABC-Eval and MusicTheoryBench show that general LLMs cannot yet be trusted to reason about notation and Not that Groove shows natural-language editing is reliable only in narrow domains under test-based verification [@wang2025notagen; @li2024ziqieval; @zhao2025abceval; @zhang2025groove]. The studio should therefore use an LLM as an orchestrator that compiles annotations into constraints for specialised symbolic models and verifies the result, in the manner of ComposerX's reviewer and Libretto's measure-and-revise loop, rather than as the composer's direct interlocutor over a score [@deng2024composerx; @xu2026libretto]. Expressive rendering should remain a separate, annotatable stage in the MIDI-DDSP and ScorePerformer mould, with partitura-style alignment turning the composer's own playing into an expression annotation [@wu2022mididdsp; @borovik2023scoreperformer; @cancinochacon2022partitura].

The gaps are as instructive as the achievements. No verified system generates MusicXML or MEI natively, and MetaScore converts scores out rather than in, so a lossless bridge between an LLM-friendly textual IR and engraving-grade notation is the first engineering task and an open research problem [@xu2025metascore]. Notation-editor integration is nearly absent — DeepBach's 2017 MuseScore plugin and Hookpad Aria are the only examples found, against a rich DAW side — and MMM-C's composers rated their desire for more control at 9.5 out of 10, which suggests that in-DAW integration is necessary but not sufficient and that the annotation layer is the missing channel [@hadjeres2017deepbach; @tchemeube2023mmmc]. The commercial arrangers show what a compile engine must match — instant, deterministic, parameterised, editable — and what it must add: sketches, hummed lines and example audio as conditions, and clean round-tripping to notation [@pgmusic2026biab; @apple2024logicpro11; @korg1993i3]. Almost every system here stops at offline metrics or a small listening study; only FlowComposer, the Bach Doodle and Hookpad Aria report deployment behaviour, and FlowComposer's finding that professionals regenerate no more than eight bars at a time remains the strongest design prior for how the loop will be used [@papadopoulos2016flowcomposer; @huang2019bachdoodle; @donahue2024hookpadaria]. Finally, the data behind the strongest models — Aria-MIDI under CC BY-NC-SA, MuseScore-derived corpora, Hooktheory — is often non-commercial or legally fraught, whereas Composer's Assistant shows that a useful infilling model can be trained on permissively licensed MIDI alone; an open-source compile engine must be built on that footing from the start [@bradshaw2025ariamidi; @malandro2024composersassistant2] (see B4, D10).

## 7. Compile II: audio rendering and audio-domain generation (S3.8–S3.10)

The symbolic compile stages surveyed in S3.1–S3.7 end with an editable score or an expressive MIDI performance; a composer still needs to *hear* it, increasingly as a produced arrangement rather than a General MIDI mock-up. This section covers the audio-domain literature the studio must both borrow from and define itself against, and it is organised deliberately out of taxonomic order. S3.9, text-to-music and full-song generation, comes first because it is the paradigm the studio inverts and because the 2023–2026 trajectory of its commercial and open-weights systems is itself evidence about what musicians want. S3.8 isolates the thinner line in which an audio model accepts *symbolic* conditions (chords, melody, MIDI, time-varying curves) or renders notation through interpretable synthesis, which is the studio's actual rendering requirement. S3.10 asks whether music-understanding models can serve as compile-time critics. Works are coded throughout on domain (D1), control granularity (D3), editability of the intermediate representation (D7) and openness of weights and training data (D10).

### 7.1 S3.9 Text-to-music and full-song audio generation (the paradigm to invert)

**Foundations (2020–2024).** Jukebox compressed 44.1 kHz audio through a three-level VQ-VAE and modelled the top level with autoregressive Transformers of up to 5B parameters, producing minutes-long songs with singing conditioned on artist, genre and unaligned lyrics; it offered no symbolic conditioning, no editing beyond primed continuation, and needed hours of compute per minute of audio [@dhariwal2020jukebox; @castellon2021codified]. The 2023 wave replaced raw-audio autoregression with neural codecs and latent diffusion. MusicLM cascaded a MuLan music–text embedding, semantic and acoustic tokens to generate 24 kHz music from text, released MusicCaps (5.5k expert-captioned 10-s clips) as the de-facto benchmark and, notably, could transform a *hummed or whistled melody* into the described style [@agostinelli2023musiclm]; Noise2Music introduced LLM pseudo-labelling of training captions [@huang2023noise2music]. Meta's MusicGen collapsed the cascade into a single-stage Transformer over interleaved EnCodec streams (300M–3.3B, 32 kHz, ~30 s) conditioned on text and a chromagram of a reference melody; its MIT code and CC-BY-NC 4.0 weights made it the most-extended open backbone, underlying Coco-Mulla, Instruct-MusicGen, MusicGen-Style and MusicGen-Stem [@copet2023musicgen; @lin2023cocomulla; @zhang2025instructmusicgen; @rouard2024musicgenstyle; @rouard2025musicgenstem]. MAGNeT showed that non-autoregressive masked generation over the same tokens ran roughly 7× faster, and its re-mask-and-redecode procedure is inherently local-editable [@ziv2024magnet].

The diffusion branch supplied the editing primitives. AudioLDM ran latent diffusion in a VAE-compressed mel space conditioned on CLAP embeddings and demonstrated zero-shot inpainting, style transfer and super-resolution without task-specific training [@liu2023audioldm; @wu2023clap]; AudioLDM 2 added a "language of audio" intermediate representation into which any conditioning modality is translated before rendering, a pattern for one renderer serving many condition types [@liu2024audioldm2]. Stable Audio 2.0 generated 4 min 45 s stereo tracks with explicit *timing conditioning* (start time and total length) and audio-to-audio initialisation from a user's clip, but its weights, trained on licensed AudioSparx data, were never released [@evans2024longform]. Its open sibling Stable Audio Open (≈1.2B parameters, up to 47 s) was trained only on Creative Commons audio from Freesound and the Free Music Archive (≈486k recordings) under the Stability AI Community License, making it the cleanest-provenance base for later controllable adapters [@evans2024stableaudioopen; @tsai2025musecontrollite; @hou2024melodycontrolnet; @floresgarcia2025sketch2sound]. JEN-1 and MusicFlow each showed that generation, inpainting and continuation can be one model, the former by mixing bidirectional and causal training, the latter by masked-prediction training of cascaded flow-matching networks; neither released weights [@li2023jen1; @prajwal2024musicflow]. Riffusion, a 2022 project that fine-tuned Stable Diffusion on spectrogram images, matters as a case study: it became the conversational, multi-turn ProducerAI in 2025, was acquired by Google in February 2026 and re-emerged in April 2026 as Google Flow Music on Lyria 3 with section-level "replace and extend" [@forsgren2022riffusion].

**Commercial full-song platforms and their drift toward editing.** These systems are the archetype the studio rejects: one prompt yields a finished mix, the human is prompt author and curator, and the intermediate representation is opaque audio (D7). Their feature histories nevertheless record sustained user pressure toward region- and part-level control. Suno's model line ran from v3 (February 2024, 2-min clips) through v4 and v4.5 to v5 (September 2025) and v5.5 (March 2026, with user-trainable "Custom Models") [@suno2026]. Around the generator it accreted Audio Input (June 2024), stem separation (July 2024, later 12-stem export), Covers (keep melody, change style), Replace Section (regenerate a time range, October 2024), Personas, Remaster and Add Vocals/Add Instrumental; in September 2025 it launched Suno Studio, a browser "generative audio workstation" with a multitrack timeline and per-section regeneration, and in August 2026 Studio 2.0 added MIDI import, recording and editing with a wavetable synthesizer [@suno2026]. The generator itself remained prompt- and audio-driven; MIDI lives inside the DAW, not as a generation condition. The Warner Music Group settlement of 25 November 2025 committed Suno to licensed models in 2026, deprecation of existing models, artist opt-in, a non-downloadable free tier and monthly download caps, reported to have taken effect on 3 September 2026 at 7 lifetime downloads on Free, 20 a month on Pro and 60 on Premier — figures documented in Suno's subscription help pages rather than its release notes, and so carried here as reported rather than verified [@wmg2025suno; @suno2026]. Udio, launched in April 2024, generated ~30-s segments extended forwards and backwards into songs and carried the earliest mainstream instance recorded in this corpus of *audio inpainting* of a user-selected region [@udio2025]. After settling with Universal on 29 October 2025 it disabled downloads and announced a 2026 subscription "walled garden" in which creations remain inside the platform, fingerprinted and filtered [@umg2025udio; @udio2025]. Both trajectories show the same two facts: users demanded region editing, and licensed audio generation arrived bundled with constrained ownership of the output.

Google's line is the most studio-like of the closed systems. Lyria powered the Music AI Sandbox, co-designed with musicians in the YouTube Music AI Incubator, whose verbs are *Create* (text plus optional lyrics on a timeline with key and tempo), *Extend* and *Edit* (transform a whole clip or a selected region, fill gaps, blend transitions); multitrack views and inpainting arrived in October 2024 and Lyria 2 with 48 kHz stereo output in April 2025, all outputs SynthID-watermarked and trained, Google states, on music it and YouTube "have the right to use" [@google2025lyria2; @googledeepmind2025sandbox]. Lyria RealTime, exposed through the Gemini API in mid-2025, is a block-autoregressive streaming model steered by weighted text prompts plus BPM, key, density and brightness with a reported control latency of at most 2 s; its PromptDJ-MIDI demo maps physical MIDI controllers to those *parameters*, not to notes [@google2025lyriarealtime]. Lyria 3 (February 2026) and Lyria 3.5 (mid-2026, reportedly 3-minute songs) power the Gemini app and Flow Music [@google2025lyriarealtime; @forsgren2022riffusion]. ElevenLabs Music (August 2025) shipped lyric editing throughout a track, regeneration of a verse, chorus or bridge, 2–6-stem export and API inpainting, positioned as trained on licensed data [@elevenlabs2025music]. Mureka reportedly marketed its O1 model in March 2025 as a "chain-of-thought" music model that plans structure before generating; these claims rest on vendor material [@kunlun2025mureka; @mureka2025]. Adobe's Project Music GenAI Control (February 2024, with UC San Diego and CMU) framed the problem as "generate, then edit with musical parameters" (tempo, structure, intensity, reference melody, section remix) but never shipped; it productised Music ControlNet and DITTO (S3.8) [@adobe2024musicgenaicontrol; @wu2024musiccontrolnet; @novack2024ditto]. Stable Audio 2.5 (September 2025) generated 3-minute multi-part tracks in under 2 s with API inpainting and audio-to-audio; its adversarial relativistic-contrastive (ARC) post-training also yielded the open-weights Stable Audio Open Small at roughly 75 ms for 12 s of stereo on an H100 [@novack2025arc]. None of these platforms publishes model details, benchmarks or user studies; the evidence level (D9) is product claims.

**Open-weights song models.** Between January 2025 and early 2026 permissively licensed lyrics-to-song models made the commercial paradigm reproducible on consumer hardware. YuE (released January 2025) is a LLaMA-2-based two-stage LM (7B semantic, 1B acoustic) generating up to 5 min of multilingual song with *track-decoupled* prediction that interleaves vocal and accompaniment tokens and in-context style transfer from a 30-s audio prompt; it is Apache-2.0 but heavy (24 GB VRAM minimum, ~360 s per 30 s on an RTX 4090) [@yuan2025yue]. DiffRhythm (ACL 2025) is a non-autoregressive latent DiT producing 4 min 45 s songs in about 10 s from *timestamped lyrics* and a text or audio style prompt, Apache-2.0 on 8 GB of VRAM, with extension and editing added in v1.2 [@ning2025diffrhythm]. SongGen (ICML 2025) is a 1.3B single-stage autoregressive Transformer with a *dual-track* mode emitting separate vocal and accompaniment streams, trained on a fully open ~2k h pipeline, Apache-2.0, limited to 30-s English songs [@liu2025songgen]. HeartMuLa (December 2025–February 2026) bundles a 3B open music LM with a 12.5 Hz codec, a lyric transcriber and a CLAP model under Apache-2.0, generating songs of up to 240 s at a real-time factor near 1; its claimed parity with Suno is self-reported [@heartmula2026].

ACE-Step deserves the closest reading because it is the strongest open candidate for the studio's arranger and renderer. ACE-Step 1.0 (ACE Studio and StepFun, May 2025) is a flow model over Sana's deep-compression autoencoder latents with a linear Transformer, aligned through REPA to MERT features; it generated up to 4 min of music with vocals in about 20 s on an A100, reportedly some 15× faster than LM-based systems, and shipped a menu of editing operations rather than one-shot generation: variations, *repainting* (local regeneration of a time range), lyric editing that keeps melody and accompaniment, lyric-to-vocal, singing-to-accompaniment, voice cloning and remixing through LoRA controllers [@gong2025acestep; @li2024mert]. ACE-Step 1.5 (technical report January–February 2026; 4B XL release April 2026) restructured the system as a hybrid *LM planner plus diffusion Transformer*: a 0.6B–4B language model converts the request into a "song blueprint" (metadata, lyrics, captions, via chain-of-thought) that conditions a DiT decoder; intrinsic RL alignment removes the external reward model; an 8-step turbo variant renders a full song in under 2 s on an A100 and under 10 s on an RTX 3090; memory ranges from under 4–6 GB to 24 GB or more; backends include CUDA, ROCm, Apple MLX and CPU; and it ships a REST and Python API and a VST3 plugin [@gong2026acestep15]. New capabilities include cover generation, repainting, vocal-to-accompaniment, track separation, multi-track layering, lyric-structure control, BPM/key/time-signature control, over 50 languages and LoRA fine-tuning from a few songs. The licence is MIT. Two gaps matter: there is no MIDI or chord input to the generator, and no training-data statement, so provenance is unknown (D10). Its planner is in effect a compiler front-end from intent to blueprint; a notation-first studio would replace that planner with the composer's score and annotations and retain the decoder.

**Stem-level "listen and respond" generation.** A distinct strand frames generation not as producing a song but as adding a part that fits what is already there, the unit of co-creation closest to arranging. SingSong source-separated a large corpus into (vocal, instrumental) pairs and trained AudioLM-style conditional generation to accompany a new a-cappella input; listeners preferred its accompaniments to retrieval baselines in a reported ~66% of pairwise comparisons [@donahue2023singsong]. StemGen (ICASSP 2024) is a non-autoregressive masked Transformer that takes an existing multi-stem context and generates one new stem of a requested category, evaluated by key, tempo and chroma coherence with the context; no weights were released [@parker2024stemgen]. Multi-Source Diffusion Models (ICLR 2024 oral) learned the *joint* score of bass, drums, guitar and piano on Slakh2100 so that a single model performs generation, separation and *source imputation*, filling in missing stems given the others [@mariani2024msdm; @manilow2019slakh]. Diff-A-Riff (Sony CSL, ISMIR 2024) generated a single 48 kHz accompaniment track to fit a given mix, steerable by a CLAP audio reference and/or text and designed for DAW workflows [@nistal2024diffariff]. MusicGen-Stem trained one codec per stem (bass, drums, other) so that one stem can be regenerated while the others are held fixed [@rouard2025musicgenstem], and Instruct-MusicGen added fusion modules to frozen MusicGen (about 8% extra parameters, 5k steps) so that it follows "add, remove or separate a stem" instructions, matching task-specific models on Slakh and MoisesDB [@zhang2025instructmusicgen]. Stemphonic, which generates a variable set of synchronised stems in one pass under per-stem activity patterns, is the arrangement-oriented endpoint of this line (S3.5) [@wu2026stemphonic]. In D2 terms these systems occupy the *response/accompaniment* framing, in which the human's material is a hard constraint, which is why they rather than the full-song generators are the natural audio analogue of the studio's compile step.

### 7.2 S3.8 Audio rendering and symbolic-conditioned audio generation

The studio's rendering requirement is narrower and harder than text-to-music: given notation, chords or MIDI, produce audio that honours them, with enough control to re-render one region after an edit. Only a handful of models accept structured musical input. JASCO (Hebrew University and Meta, ISMIR 2024) is a flow-matching text-to-music model that takes time-aligned *chord progressions* and a *melody salience matrix* as symbolic controls, plus a separated drum track and a full-mix embedding as audio controls, through information-bottleneck layers with temporal blurring; a human study found quality comparable to baselines with "significantly better and more versatile controls" [@tal2024jasco]. It is the clearest open example of lead-sheet-style conditioning of an audio model, but it produces roughly 10-s clips at 32 kHz under CC-BY-NC weights. Coco-Mulla fine-tuned under 4% of MusicGen's parameters on fewer than 300 songs to accept chord progressions, drum MIDI and pitch content alongside text, a cheap recipe for teaching an existing audio LM to read a composer's chords [@lin2023cocomulla]. Mustango (NAACL 2024) injected chords, beats, key and tempo parsed from the prompt through a music-domain-informed UNet module and released MusicBench (52k music–text pairs with MIR-extracted theory captions), improving chord, beat, key and tempo accuracy over MusicGen and AudioLDM 2 [@melechovsky2024mustango].

A second family conditions on time-varying *curves*, which maps directly onto what a composer might draw over a passage. Music ControlNet (CMU and Adobe, TASLP 2024) attached ControlNet-style adapters to a spectrogram diffusion model for melody (chroma), dynamics (loudness) and rhythm (beat and downbeat) curves; crucially, controls may be *partially specified in time*, pinning melody for bars 1–4 and leaving the rest free, and the model was 49% more faithful to input melodies than MusicGen with 35× fewer parameters and 11× less training data, though no code was released [@wu2024musiccontrolnet]. MuseControlLite (ICML 2025) reproduced these controls as 85M-parameter decoupled cross-attention adapters on Stable Audio Open, using rotary positional embeddings for time-varying conditions (melody accuracy 56.6% to 61.1%), and added native audio inpainting and outpainting with 6.75× fewer trainable parameters than a Stable Audio Open ControlNet; open and CC-trained, it is arguably the best starting point for a studio prototype's audio stage [@tsai2025musecontrollite; @evans2024stableaudioopen]. The Melody+Text ControlNet for Stable Audio's DiT reportedly used a top-k constant-Q melody representation with curriculum learning to balance melody against text [@hou2024melodycontrolnet]. Sketch2Sound, which extracts loudness, brightness and pitch curves from a vocal imitation with a tunable looseness, belongs to the same family and is treated under S1.2 [@floresgarcia2025sketch2sound].

DITTO reframes all of these as one mechanism. Rather than training adapters, it optimises the initial noise latents of a frozen text-to-music diffusion model through any differentiable feature-matching loss, obtaining inpainting, outpainting, looping, intensity-curve, melody and musical-structure (self-similarity) control from one model and outperforming training-based baselines including Music ControlNet; DITTO-2 distilled the model and optimised through a one-step surrogate for a 10–20× speed-up, faster than real time, and added targets such as maximising CLAP text adherence [@novack2024ditto; @wu2023clap]. For the studio this is the general "annotation → constraint" mechanism: any composer annotation with a computable target (a hummed contour, a dynamics sketch, a form diagram) becomes a loss on a frozen renderer without retraining per annotation type. Text2FX applies the same logic to interpretable production parameters, optimising differentiable EQ and reverb so that the processed audio's CLAP embedding matches a prompt (S4.3) [@chu2025text2fx].

The most composer-aligned rendering lineage remains DDSP. DDSP made synthesizer and effect modules differentiable so that networks output interpretable parameters (fundamental frequency, loudness, harmonic distribution) instead of waveforms, enabling independent pitch and loudness control and timbre transfer with small models [@engel2020ddsp]. MIDI-DDSP (ICLR 2022) built a three-level hierarchy on it, notes → per-note performance parameters (vibrato, dynamics, articulation, brightness) → synthesis parameters → audio, for monophonic URMP instruments under Apache-2.0; a user may write notes, edit an expression curve or adjust synthesis directly, or let learned priors fill in, the clearest published instance of a compiler with editable intermediate representations (D7 multi-level editable) [@wu2022mididdsp]. DDSP-VST packaged the approach as MIDI-playable neural synthesizer and re-timbring effect plugins with a trainer that builds a personal timbre from "a couple minutes of audio," and Tone Transfer put humming-to-instrument in the browser (S1.2) [@magenta2022ddspvst; @magenta2020tonetransfer]. Its limitation is monophony. MusicHiFi, reportedly a cascade of three GANs for vocoding, bandwidth extension and mono-to-stereo upmixing, addresses a back-end concern: many controllable models output 16–32 kHz mono, and a modular finishing stage can lift them to 44.1 kHz stereo [@zhu2024musichifi].

Finally, real-time MIDI-steerable audio arrived in 2026. Magenta RealTime 2 (June 2026) moved the open "live music model" from 2-s chunks to 40-ms frames with roughly 200 ms control latency on Apple Silicon (230M and 2.4B models) and accepts *MIDI notes* (Auto-Strum and precise-onset modes) alongside text and audio style prompts; it was trained on about 71k hours of mostly instrumental stock music paired with MT3-inferred MIDI labels, the only open audio model in this corpus whose training pairs audio with transcribed symbolic data [@lyria2025livemusic; @gardner2022mt3]. Its short context precludes long-form structure, but it is the first credible open building block for rendering or jamming on the studio's symbolic material inside a plugin (S5.3).

### 7.3 S3.10 Music-understanding models as compile-time critics

A compiler that emits audio needs a way to check its output: did the rendering keep the key and tempo, follow the chords, land the annotated dynamics? The music-understanding literature offers encoders, text–audio embeddings and audio-language models, but none is yet reliable enough to be trusted silently. The encoder layer is the most mature. JukeMIR showed in 2021 that Jukebox's intermediate representations transferred to tagging, genre, key and emotion tasks with linear probes and seeded the Sheet Sage melody transcriber (S1.1) [@castellon2021codified; @donahue2022sheetsage]. MERT (ICLR 2024) trained HuBERT-style masked prediction against an RVQ-VAE acoustic teacher and a constant-Q *musical* teacher for pitch and harmony, at 95M and 330M parameters, reaching state-of-the-art or competitive results across 14 MIR tasks; its CC-BY-SA 4.0 weights became the default backbone for audio-side annotation and are used inside MU-LLaMA, MusiLingo and ACE-Step [@li2024mert; @liu2023mullama; @deng2024musilingo; @gong2025acestep]. MuQ replaced MERT's teachers with a light Mel residual vector quantiser and beat MERT and MusicFM with only 0.9k hours of pretraining; its companion MuQ-MuLan is an open contrastive music–text model, the missing open analogue of MusicLM's MuLan [@zhu2025muq; @agostinelli2023musiclm]. LAION-CLAP, contrastive text–audio encoders trained on LAION-Audio-630K, is the de-facto text conditioner (AudioLDM, Stable Audio Open, Diff-A-Riff) and evaluation metric, and because it defines a shared text–audio space it lets text annotations act as loss functions in DITTO-2 and Text2FX [@wu2023clap; @liu2023audioldm; @nistal2024diffariff; @novack2024ditto; @chu2025text2fx].

The audio-LLM layer, where critique would happen, is weaker. MU-LLaMA (ICASSP 2024) joined MERT to LLaMA through an adapter for music question answering and captioning and introduced MusicQA [@liu2023mullama]. LLark (Spotify, ICML 2024) paired a Jukebox-5B encoder with Llama-2 on instruction data auto-generated from open datasets' annotations, producing key, tempo and instrumentation facts conversationally with high human agreement on captions; training code was released, weights were not [@gardner2024llark]. MusiLingo reportedly used a single projection layer between MERT and Vicuna [@deng2024musilingo]. SALMONN (ICLR 2024) fused Whisper and BEATs encoders through a Q-Former into Vicuna, handling speech, audio events and music in one model, a candidate front-end for parsing spoken and hummed annotations [@tang2024salmonn]. General models such as Qwen2-Audio (7B, Apache-2.0), Qwen2.5-Omni and Gemini 2.5 describe genre, instruments and mood and roughly estimate tempo, and Gemini is what Google pairs with Lyria, but they are not music-specialised; only Qwen2-Audio's report was verified directly [@chu2024qwen2audio]. OpenMU (Sony) is the one benchmark mixing the modalities the studio cares about: OpenMU-Bench (≈1M examples) covers captioning and reasoning but also lyrics, *ABC-notation* understanding and tool use, and the OpenMU model outperformed MU-LLaMA on it [@zhao2024openmu].

Two results temper the picture. MuChoMusic (ISMIR 2024) built 1,187 human-validated multiple-choice questions on 644 tracks from MusicCaps and Song Describer, spanning theory, instrumentation, structure and cultural reasoning, and evaluated five open audio LLMs including MU-LLaMA, MusiLingo, SALMONN and Qwen-Audio; the central finding was *over-reliance on the language modality*, models often answering without using the audio [@weck2024muchomusic; @manco2023songdescriber]. The second is the field's one user-centred data point. Ronchini et al. (CMMR 2025) had 17 music producers from seven countries use a tool combining MusicGen with HT-Demucs six-stem separation, followed by interviews and thematic analysis; the themes were "creative misalignment" between intent and output, integration pain concentrated on *tempo, key and beat alignment*, strong demand for control and editability, and a split between ideation (94% would use it) and production (few would) [@ronchini2025ttmuserstudy; @copet2023musicgen]. The alignment problems named by working producers are precisely the ones symbolic conditioning solves by construction, and the field's evaluation practice, FAD, CLAP score and small MOS panels, does not measure them (E4) [@kilgour2019fad; @chung2025kad].

### 7.4 Implications for the studio

The first lesson is that the paradigm being inverted has been inverting itself. Every commercial platform surveyed began as prompt-to-song and added, under user pressure, stems, section replacement, covers, personas, timelines and finally MIDI editing; Google moved from a Sandbox with region edit and extend to real-time steering to conversational section-level refinement; the open ACE-Step line shipped repaint, lyric-edit, cover and vocal-to-accompaniment operations and a planner that turns intent into a structured blueprint before decoding [@suno2026; @google2025lyria2; @forsgren2022riffusion; @gong2026acestep15]. What none of them did is make notation the source of truth or accept symbolic material as a generation condition; Suno's MIDI lives inside its DAW, and Udio's licensed platform keeps the output inside a walled garden [@suno2026; @udio2025]. The studio's differentiators are therefore concrete: compile from the composer's score and annotations, expose editable intermediate representations in the MIDI-DDSP sense, emit portable notation, MIDI and stems, and state the provenance of any audio model it ships [@wu2022mididdsp; @evans2024stableaudioopen].

The second lesson is that the rendering stage the studio needs exists only as research-grade pieces, and the pieces can be assembled. Symbolic conditioning of audio is demonstrated by JASCO, Coco-Mulla, Mustango and Music ControlNet; native region inpainting by MuseControlLite, JEN-1, MusicFlow and ACE-Step; part-at-a-time generation by SingSong, MSDM, Diff-A-Riff, MusicGen-Stem and Stemphonic; training-free imposition of arbitrary computable constraints by DITTO; per-note editable synthesis by MIDI-DDSP; and MIDI-steerable real-time rendering by Magenta RealTime 2 [@tal2024jasco; @lin2023cocomulla; @tsai2025musecontrollite; @mariani2024msdm; @novack2024ditto; @wu2022mididdsp; @lyria2025livemusic]. A plausible open stack is a CC-trained base (Stable Audio Open) with MuseControlLite-style adapters for curves, a Coco-Mulla-style adapter for chords and drum MIDI, DITTO for ad-hoc annotation constraints, per-stem regeneration, and an ACE-Step decoder where vocals or full-band production are required, with weight licences checked separately from code licences because MusicGen-family weights are non-commercial. The gaps are equally concrete: no open model renders polyphonic notation with MIDI-DDSP's level of control; symbolic-conditioned models produce 10–47-s clips; Magenta RealTime 2 has no long-form structure; and no verified model accepts a score, as opposed to chords or a melody curve, as its condition.

The third lesson concerns the critic. MERT, MuQ and CLAP are dependable feature extractors for key, tempo, chord and text-adherence probes, but MuChoMusic's finding that audio LLMs answer from text rather than sound means their judgments cannot close the loop unsupervised [@li2024mert; @zhu2025muq; @weck2024muchomusic]. A symbolic-first studio has an advantage that audio-first products lack: it can verify a rendering against the score it compiled from, using transcription and MIR probes on the output and reserving the LLM for phrasing the discrepancy back to the composer (S4.4). Ronchini et al.'s producers asked for exactly this alignment guarantee, and their study remains the only user evidence in an otherwise metric-driven field, a gap the studio's own evaluation programme (E1, E4) should fill [@ronchini2025ttmuserstudy].

## 8. Edit: revising and iterating (S4)

Editing is the stage at which the studio's thesis is either honoured or lost. If the composer can only accept or reject what the machine produced, the loop degenerates into curation whatever the interface promises; if the composer can select a region, say or draw or sing what should change, inspect the machine's proposal, alter it, and keep a record of what happened, then generation is genuinely subordinate to authorship. This section surveys the general HCI paradigms for machine-mediated editing (S4.1), symbolic transformation, variation and proofreading of music the composer already wrote (S4.2), audio editing and inpainting (S4.3), the neglected reverse channel in which the system explains or previews what it intends (S4.4), and the history, alternatives and branching machinery that makes iteration recoverable (S4.5). Two asymmetries structure it: editing is far better developed in audio than in symbolic music — eleven primary works in S4.3 against two in S4.2, though the symbolic surface is where edits are semantically meaningful — and the human→AI direction far better developed than the AI→human direction, which has two primary entries in total, a finding independently established by @rezwana2023cofi.

### 8.1 S4.1 Editing paradigms from HCI at large

The normative baseline is @shneiderman1983direct, whose three properties of direct manipulation — continuous visual representation of the object of interest, physical actions in place of complex syntax, and rapid, incremental, reversible operations whose effect is immediately visible — were formulated explicitly against command languages. Prompt-based music generation is a command language with a probabilistic interpreter: the object of interest is not continuously represented (it exists only as the last rendered output), the actions are syntax rather than gestures, and operations are neither incremental nor reversible. Judged by those criteria the dominant paradigm of 2023–2026 music AI is a regression, and the question is what it takes to recover direct manipulation without losing the expressive reach of language.

DirectGPT is the most precise answer available. @masson2024directgpt built a direct-manipulation layer over a large language model in which generated objects remain continuously represented and selectable, toolbar operators reuse prompt syntax, outputs can be dragged and selected to compose the next prompt, and every operation is undoable; the system translates manipulation into an engineered prompt rather than asking the user to write one. Across text, code and vector-graphics editing tasks, participants were reported 50% faster, issuing 50% fewer prompts and prompts 72% shorter than with a conversational baseline. The mechanism behind the shortening is the load-bearing insight: when the selection carries the scope and the referent, the utterance need only carry the intent — "make bars 17–24 of the second violin part sparser" collapses to a selection plus "sparser."

Instruction-conditioned editing supplies the model-side counterpart. @brooks2023instructpix2pix trained a conditional diffusion model mapping an (image, edit instruction) pair to an edited image in one forward pass, and solved the absent-dataset problem by *synthesising* training triples — GPT-3 generating instructions and edited captions, Stable Diffusion with prompt-to-prompt generating the before/after image pairs — so no human editing labels were required, establishing instruction editing as a trainable task rather than an inference-time trick. Its transfer to music is a data-generation recipe as much as an architecture: the missing corpus for annotation-driven symbolic editing is a set of (score, annotation, edited score) triples, bootstrappable by applying known transformations in reverse and having a language model describe them.

ExpressEdit shows what the interface around such a model should look like. Its pipeline and study figures are given under S2.4; what matters here is the contract it establishes. @tilekbay2024expressedit let users describe an edit in language *and* sketch on the artifact, parsed the request into typed references, resolved each with a modality-appropriate resolver, and returned suggestions the user could accept, reject or adjust, with a panel exposing how each reference had been interpreted. The distribution of outcomes is the lesson: fewer than half of suggestions were accepted as offered, and a clear majority of the edits that survived into the final result were *modified* suggestions. Most accepted work was therefore neither the machine's proposal nor the human's unaided edit but the human's revision of the proposal — which means the unit of delivery must be an editable draft, not a committed change.

Three older lineages fill out the paradigm. @igarashi1999teddy established the sketch-interface contract in 3D modelling: accept an ambiguous gesture, infer a plausible complete object, display it immediately, and let further gestures refine it, with no dialog boxes in the loop. @cypher1993wwid collected the programming-by-demonstration systems built on the premise that knowing how to perform a task should suffice to create a program performing it, the user's role being to correct over- and under-generalisation of the demonstrated example; read musically, an attached reference recording is a demonstration and "voice the chords like this" a generalisation request whose intended aspect the composer must be able to name. The live- and projectional-programming line contributes three further properties: Tanimoto's liveness levels, describing how immediately edits are reflected in running output; Hazel's typed holes, which give a dynamic semantics for *incomplete* programs so that evaluation proceeds around the holes and every editor state yields feedback; and projectional editors such as JetBrains MPS, which edit an abstract syntax tree directly and project it into several synchronised notations at once [@omar2019hazel]. A score with explicit "to be composed" regions that still compiles and plays, re-renders on every annotation, and is editable as notation, piano roll, chord chart or text is the musical reading of those properties, and what makes "compile" more than a metaphor (see S3.6 and @kim2026decomposer).

The transferable pattern across these lineages is one contract: *select a region of the artefact, express intent in whatever modality is handiest, and receive inspectable, editable suggestions rather than a committed change.* Each clause carries weight independently — selection makes utterances short and referents unambiguous, modality freedom lets a composer hum a counter-line rather than describe it, and suggestion-rather-than-commitment preserves the reversibility Shneiderman required while matching ExpressEdit's evidence that revision is the normal case. Conversational music systems violate all three clauses, and their evaluations register it: Loop Copilot's eight users rated it usable (SUS 75.3 ± 15.3) and valued it for inspiration but complained of limited musical-attribute control and poor prompt responsiveness, and asked for DAW integration [@zhang2023loopcopilot].

### 8.2 S4.2 Symbolic editing, variation and proofreading

The strongest single piece of evidence for the studio's stance comes from a study of a deliberately modest tool. @krol2025ownership built Rhapsody Refiner, which takes a musician's own MIDI and produces *variations* by selectively masking notes and re-predicting them with MusicBERT under user-set parameters; it cannot compose from scratch, and that dependence on strong human input was a design commitment, not a limitation. It was evaluated in a four-week ecological study with eight practising musicians — songwriters, producers, educators, instrumentalists — combining tutorials, independent in-the-wild use, journals, logs, interviews and thematic analysis. The findings' asymmetry is the point. Participants valued the tool for generating "moments, not whole ideas"; because output depended on material they had authored, they reported ownership of both artefact and *process*; and they explicitly rejected automatic completion, declining to have the system finish their work even when it could. Its imperfections and randomness were reported as productive, prompting creative problem-solving rather than read as errors. The generalisation is narrow but exactly the one the studio needs: transforming authored material preserves creative ownership, whereas supplying unauthored material displaces it — a distinction invisible to any evaluation measuring only output quality, and consistent with @suh2021socialglue's warning that a generous generative model can shift collaborators from co-composing to co-*producing*.

Attribute-conditioned rewriting is the best-developed technical form of "transform what I wrote." MuseMorphose encodes each bar of an existing pop-piano piece to a latent with a Transformer VAE and decodes it under user-set *bar-level* attributes — rhythmic intensity and polyphony, eight levels each — rewriting a song's texture bar by bar while preserving its content; @wu2023musemorphose reported gains over MusicVAE-style RNN baselines in fidelity, attribute-control accuracy and diversity across full songs. Music FaderNets learned disentangled "faders" for rhythm and note density in a VAE whose Gaussian-mixture latent captured *arousal* semi-supervised from only 1% labelled data, grounding a high-level intention ("more intense here") in inspectable low-level features [@tan2020fadernets]. FIGARO and MuseCoco apply the same manoeuvre at compile rather than edit time (see S3.3) [@vonrutte2023figaro; @lu2023musecoco], and the Latent Fourier Transform generalises it to timescale, masking latent frequency bands as an "equalizer for musical patterns" that keeps phrase-level shape while varying surface detail, on qualitative evidence [@wang2026latentft].

Natural-language editing of symbolic music has exactly one careful study. @zhang2025groove asked whether general-purpose LLMs can edit an existing drum groove, with no training, from instructions of three kinds — specific ("add a cymbal on beat 1"), descriptive ("reduce hi-hat activity") and stylistic ("make it funkier") — representing the groove in a compact textual "drumroll" notation of one line per instrument and one character per sixteenth. Its methodological contribution is the evaluation: in place of ground-truth edited grooves, which do not exist and would be non-unique anyway, it used expert-authored *unit tests*, which agreed with professional musicians at 89% true-positive and 94% true-negative rates; the best of eight models (gpt-4.1-mini) passed 68%, and reasoning-tuned models helped. Two lessons follow: zero-shot instruction editing is feasible in a narrow, densely quantised domain and should not be assumed to transfer to voiced polyphony, and verification of an edit is a test-satisfaction problem, which fits the compiler framing exactly — an edit request is a specification, and specifications can be checked.

Three further works reach the same primitive from the compile side and are treated at length under S3.2, each contributing one lesson to the editing side. RefinPaint separates *where* to change from *how*, pairing a critic that identifies the weakest notes in context with an inpainting model that resamples them, so proofreading becomes iterative rather than wholesale [@ramoneda2024refinpaint]. Composer's Assistant 2 marks the fine-grained-control endpoint, letting the composer mark partial track-measures and constrain the refill along roughly ten named musical axes [@malandro2024composersassistant2]. FlowComposer supplies the interaction datum that matters most here: shipped in 2016 with region-level regeneration and conformance sliders, it was observed to serve *fragments*, composers rarely generating more than eight bars at a time [@papadopoulos2016flowcomposer]. Cococo's steering tools produced the same behaviour with measured effects on ownership and control [@louie2020cococo].

The node is nonetheless thin. Only two works surveyed here have symbolic editing as their *primary* contribution — MuseMorphose and "Not that Groove" — and one covers drum grooves only; everything else is an infilling system repurposed for editing, an attribute-conditioned generator, or a study of a research prototype. There is no symbolic analogue of InstructPix2Pix: no model trained on (score, instruction, edited score) triples, no public corpus of such triples, no benchmark for polyphonic edit fidelity. Given that a composer's edits are the operations for which the symbolic surface exists, this is the largest gap on the *editing* side.

### 8.3 S4.3 Audio editing and inpainting

Audio went the other way, and quickly. The zero-shot branch manipulates a pretrained generator's conditioning or noise trajectory. MusicMagus edits *generated* music by translating the text-embedding difference between a source and target word — "piano" to "guitar" — into a latent-space move, with cross-attention constraints preserving unedited attributes; it beat zero-shot and some supervised baselines on style and timbre transfer but was limited on real recordings [@zhang2024musicmagus]. That limitation motivated inversion: @manor2024zeta applied edit-friendly DDPM inversion to AudioLDM2, giving ZETA for text-based editing of *real* recordings with structure preserved and ZEUS for *unsupervised* discovery of semantic edit directions by PCA over posterior-mean directions, which yielded musically meaningful axes — adding or removing an instrument's participation, melodic improvisation — with no text at all. MelodyFlow adapted ReNoise latent inversion to flow matching with regularisation for fast zero-shot editing of existing stereo clips that changes genre and instrumentation while preserving melody and structure, outperforming ReNoise and DDIM inversion on editing consistency [@lelan2024melodyflow]. Text2FX is the outlier: rather than regenerate audio, it optimises *differentiable audio-effect* parameters (EQ, reverb) so the processed signal's CLAP embedding matches a prompt such as "warmer," leaving interpretable, further-editable parameters behind [@chu2025text2fx].

The trained branch is stronger. AUDIT was the first instruction-following audio editor, trained on (instruction, input audio, output audio) triplets to add, drop, replace, inpaint and super-resolve, establishing the natural-language-edit-over-a-clip pattern for general audio [@wang2023audit]. InstructME specialised it to music — add, remove, extract, replace and remix instruments, multi-round — and, importantly for a composer-centred tool, injected a chord-progression matrix as a condition so edits stay harmonically faithful [@han2023instructme]. Instruct-MusicGen reached comparable capability far more cheaply, adding audio- and text-fusion modules to a frozen MusicGen at roughly 8% extra parameters and 5,000 steps to follow "add, remove or separate a stem," matching task-specific models on Slakh- and MoisesDB-derived benchmarks with open code [@zhang2025instructmusicgen]. SongEditor extended editing to full songs *with vocals*, supporting segment-wise editing (regenerate a section with modified lyrics) and track-wise editing (vocals versus accompaniment) [@yang2025songeditor]. MuseControlLite is the most practical open recipe: 85M-parameter decoupled cross-attention adapters with rotary positional embeddings on Stable Audio Open, giving time-varying melody, rhythm and dynamics control *plus* inpainting and outpainting at 6.75× fewer trainable parameters than a ControlNet baseline, melody accuracy rising from 56.6% to 61.1% with RoPE [@tsai2025musecontrollite]. Adjacent designs make the mask structural rather than temporal: MusicGen-Stem trains one codec per stem so one stem can be regenerated against fixed others [@rouard2025musicgenstem], MAGNeT's re-mask-and-redecode is inherently local [@ziv2024magnet], and melody-conditioned ControlNet branches on Stable Audio's diffusion Transformer reportedly support "keep my melody, re-render everything else" from top-k constant-Q peaks [@hou2024melodycontrolnet].

Industry converged on the same primitive. @adobe2024musicgenaicontrol framed the problem publicly as generate-then-edit-with-musical-parameters (tempo, structure, intensity, section remix, reference melody) — technically a productisation of Music ControlNet and DITTO, never shipped [@wu2024musiccontrolnet; @novack2024ditto]. Stable Audio 2.5 exposes inpainting as an API — upload audio, pick a region, generate the fill — with three-minute generation in under two seconds via adversarial relativistic-contrastive post-training, whose open sibling renders twelve seconds of 44.1 kHz stereo in roughly 75 ms on an H100 [@novack2025arc]. The full-song platforms accreted region editing under user pressure: Suno's Replace Section, Udio's region inpainting, ElevenLabs's verse/chorus regeneration and ACE-Step's repainting are one operation under four names (see S3.9) [@suno2026; @udio2025; @elevenlabs2025music; @gong2025acestep].

Two conclusions follow. Region editing is table stakes in audio: every serious system of 2025–2026, open or closed, supports selecting a span and regenerating it, and sub-second inference makes doing so interactive. And natively mask-trained models beat post-hoc tricks. Inversion editing is elegant and needs no paired data, but trades quality against fidelity to unedited material, works best on model-generated audio, and offers no control surface beyond the prompt; models trained with masks or instruction triplets (AUDIT, InstructME, Instruct-MusicGen, SongEditor, MuseControlLite) expose an explicit editing interface, preserve context by construction, and — in InstructME's case — hold the composer's harmony fixed while doing it. The studio's audio stage should therefore rest on a mask-trained, control-adapted open renderer, not on inversion of a text-to-music model.

### 8.4 S4.4 AI→human feedback, explanation and plan preview

@rezwana2023cofi coded 92 existing co-creative systems against the COFI framework and found that the field concentrates on AI generative ability and *almost entirely lacks AI→human communication* — explanations, statements of intent, expressions of confidence — which they argue is precisely what distinguishes a partner from a tool. Their companion study compared two versions of a co-creative sketching system, one contributing only sketches and one also communicating its intentions and reactions; the communicating version was perceived as more collaborative, engaging, partner-like and reliable, though some found it intrusive [@rezwana2022perceptions]. This taxonomy's tally corroborates the diagnosis: two primary entries here against eleven for audio editing.

What little exists points in consistent directions. RefinPaint's feedback model answers *where* to modify before the inpainter answers *how* — an AI-produced annotation on the composer's own score, and the only music system surveyed that marks locations rather than replacing content [@ramoneda2024refinpaint]. Cococo's users, given voice lanes, semantic sliders, an example-similarity slider and multiple alternatives, spontaneously developed "debugging" strategies to work out what the model was doing; against a generate-and-accept baseline in a within-subjects study with 21 novices, comprehensibility rose to 5.3 from 3.2 and controllability to 5.9 from 3.5 [@louie2020cococo]. That users invent debugging behaviour in the absence of explanation is an argument for supplying it. The XAIxArts line makes controls legible from the model side by regularising MeasureVAE's first four latent dimensions onto rhythmic complexity, note range, note density and average interval jump, with visualisations of each [@bryankinns2021xai]; @scurto2021coexplorer inverted the channel, letting like/dislike feedback steer an agent's parameter-space exploration.

The plan-preview pattern is the most directly transferable. ReaLJam displayed the accompaniment agent's *upcoming* chords in a waterfall view split into a committed window that cannot change and an adaptive window that may; five of six experienced pianists wanted the visible preview, and tunable protocol parameters absorbed the wide variation in their other preferences [@scarlatos2025realjam]. ExpressEdit's Examine panel is the non-real-time equivalent, showing how each temporal, spatial, operational and parameter reference was resolved, and was rated 6.1 of 7 [@tilekbay2024expressedit]. Side Views generalises both as persistent, on-demand previews of what an operation *would* do before committing [@terry2002sideviews]. Constraint validators occupy the same slot from the checking side, as in MultiVerse's rule-based enforcement of creator-authored lyric constraints [@wang2026multiverse], as does test-based verification of edits [@zhang2025groove].

The compiler framing has an unusually natural answer here, which is reason to keep the metaphor. A compiler does not silently emit a binary: it emits diagnostics naming a location, a rule and a reason, and a build log recording what it did with which inputs. The musical analogue is a per-compile log stating which annotation governed which bars, which model or solver handled each span, which alternatives were considered, where the system was least confident, and which constraints it could not satisfy — every line anchored to a score element identifier and therefore clickable. This subsumes RefinPaint's where-to-modify as a diagnostic, ReaLJam's committed/tentative split as a preview, ExpressEdit's Examine as reference resolution, and Cococo's debugging as an intended workflow rather than a coping strategy. Nothing in the surveyed literature implements it for music.

### 8.5 S4.5 History, versioning, alternatives and branching

The HCI foundation is the alternatives-and-subjunctive lineage collected by @terry2002sideviews Side Views' persistent on-demand previews of possible outcomes, Juxtapose's parallel authoring of interface variants with linked editing and runtime tuning, and Lunzer and Hornbæk's subjunctive interfaces for viewing and controlling alternative scenarios side by side — all resting on the claim that near-term experimentation, variation and comparison are core creative needs that premature commitment suppresses, with lab studies reporting more alternatives explored under parallelism. @grossman2010chronicle attacked provenance instead: Chronicle records a graphical document's full video and event history so a user can click any region of the *final* artefact, see the workflow, tools and settings that produced it, and play the sequence back; its study found this useful and easy to use. Luminate updated alternatives for LLMs by having the model first generate a design space's *dimensions* and then populate a structured grid users browse, filter and combine; fourteen professional writers found it useful for exploration [@suh2024luminate].

Music-specific machinery is sparse but real. @foscarin2019diff defined a diff procedure for *scores* at the graphical-notation level, converting each bar and voice to a tree, combining sequence edit distance over bars with tree edit distance over beaming and tuplet hierarchies, and rendering two Verovio scores side by side with differences highlighted; validated against OMR output, it was motivated by collaborative editing and version control. It is the only research artefact for musical diff found in this review, and without one there is no way to show a composer what a compile changed. On the product side, Flat.io auto-syncs every edit, creates a major version at most every fifty modifications, lets free users restore the ten most recent major versions and paid users scrub *every individual change* with a video-like slider, marks change locations on a score scrollbar with per-contributor colour coding, and exports any historical state to MusicXML or MIDI [@flat2026history]. Fine-grained, attributable linear notation history is therefore a solved delivery problem; the DAW-project analogues, Splice Studio's git-like commit history and Blend's project forking, are both reportedly defunct, suggesting limited pull for file-level versioning of opaque projects. mei-friend takes a third route, pairing an MEI code view with live Verovio rendering, committing encodings to GitHub, and attaching standards-based Web Annotations to score elements by identifier — at once a projectional-editing pattern, a git history for symbolic music and an annotation anchor, on thin published documentation [@goebl2023meifriend].

What does not exist anywhere is branch and merge with *musical* semantics. Score diffs exist, per-edit linear histories exist in products, alternatives interfaces exist in generic creative tools, and generation systems produce candidate sets — Cococo's alternatives, Calliope's batch generation of up to a thousand candidates for selected bars [@tchemeube2022calliope], Magenta Studio's Interpolate [@roberts2019magentastudio] — but no system lets a composer fork a passage into two arrangements, develop both, and merge the horn writing of one into the string writing of the other with conflicts reported musically. Luminate's dimension-first grid and Chronicle's click-to-provenance likewise have no music analogue.

Provenance logging is also where the editing loop meets the authorship argument, which raises the stakes above convenience. @micchi2021ikeepcounting documented more than twenty-five distinct human interventions in one AI-assisted song — structure from a Markov model, chords from an LSTM, lyrics from GPT-2, human filtering of candidates, human-composed verse melodies, human arrangement and production — and argued that deliberate selection and modification of machine material *constitutes* authorship, releasing the result under CC BY-SA. The U.S. Copyright Office's 2025 copyrightability report reached a compatible legal position: prompts alone do not confer authorship, but human-authored expressive inputs, creative selection and arrangement, and modifications of AI output are protectable to the extent of the human contribution, case by case [@usco2025part2]. A studio that logs every annotation, compile, accepted or rejected suggestion and manual edit against score element identifiers is therefore not merely building an undo stack but generating the evidentiary record on which the composer's claim rests. Chronicle's click-to-history, Flat's contributor colouring, mei-friend's commits and Foscarin et al.'s diff are its four components, and no system combines them.

### 8.6 Implications for the studio

The clearest design conclusion is that the edit stage should be a *selection-scoped suggestion pipeline*, not a chat window and not a regenerate button. The contract recurring across DirectGPT, ExpressEdit, Teddy, programming by demonstration and every successful music system in S4.2 is identical: the composer selects a region of the score, expresses intent in whatever modality is nearest to hand — typed instruction, sketch on the staff, hummed line, reference clip, slider — and receives inspectable, editable proposals with their interpretation exposed, which the composer modifies before accepting. Proposals must therefore land in the editable notation surface, carry the reference resolution that produced them, and be revisable without a second round-trip through the model. FlowComposer's decade-old observation that composers rarely regenerate more than eight bars, and Rhapsody Refiner's participants valuing "moments, not whole ideas," together fix the default edit unit at a phrase — not a section and never a song.

The technical gap the studio is best placed to close is symbolic instruction editing. Audio has mask-trained editors, instruction-tuned editors, inversion editors, region-editing APIs and shipped product features; symbolic music has attribute-conditioned rewriting on piano, infilling repurposed as editing, and one careful study of zero-shot natural-language editing confined to drum grooves. The route through is visible even though no one has taken it: build a (score, annotation, edited score) corpus by InstructPix2Pix's synthetic-triple recipe, inverting known musical transformations and having a language model describe them; adopt ExpressEdit's four-part reference grammar as the annotation schema, with temporal references resolving to bars, beats and formal sections, spatial references to staves, voices and registers, operations to a closed vocabulary of musical edits and parameters to sliders and reference examples alike; adopt "Not that Groove"'s unit-test methodology as the verification layer, so an edit request becomes a checkable specification; and keep the compile step deterministic where a program-based intermediate representation allows. Because the studio is symbolic-first, its edits are semantically inspectable in a way audio edits never are, which is what makes testing and diffing possible at all.

The half of the loop no existing system builds is the return channel, and it is the half a compiler makes obvious. Every component of the build log sketched in S4.4 exists somewhere — RefinPaint's where-to-modify, ReaLJam's committed-versus-tentative preview, Side Views' on-demand previews, Chronicle's click-to-provenance, Foscarin et al.'s score diff so that reviewing an AI edit resembles reviewing a patch — and none has been assembled for music; above them sits the branching layer nobody has built, forked arrangements of one annotated passage compared along explicit musical dimensions as in Luminate and merged with conflicts reported musically. That log is also the authorship record. Between @micchi2021ikeepcounting's argument from documented intervention and the Copyright Office's position that human selection, arrangement and modification are protectable to the extent of the human contribution [@usco2025part2], a studio keeping a complete, inspectable trail of who changed what, when and under which annotation produces not merely a better editing experience but the proof that the composer wrote the piece.

## 9. Play: real-time co-performance (S5)

The compose–annotate–compile–edit loop is turn-taking: the composer acts, the compiler responds, the composer edits. Play is the branch of the same loop in which the response must arrive within a beat. It matters to this studio for two reasons. The founder is a keyboardist, and an arrangement that can be *played with* on a Nord Stage over MIDI is a different test of a compiled score than one that can only be auditioned. And the real-time literature is the oldest continuous line of "machines that listen and respond" in music; it has already worked out vocabulary (Rowe's score-driven versus performance-driven input, instrument versus player paradigm), interaction patterns (anticipation displays, kill-switches, per-user protocol settings) and training recipes (RL against coherence rewards with an offline teacher) that the turn-taking studio can reuse [@rowe1993interactive; @scarlatos2025realjam; @wu2024realchords]. In the taxonomy's terms Play is D5 *simultaneous* rather than human-initiated, and it forces latency and future-visibility trade-offs that offline compilation can ignore. This section moves from score following (S5.1) through improvisation partners (S5.2) and RL-tuned or streaming neural models (S5.3) to the design spaces, pedagogy and games that frame the practice (S5.4).

### 9.1 S5.1 Score following and accompaniment

The score-driven pole of Rowe's taxonomy was established in a single year by two systems. Dannenberg's on-line accompaniment algorithm (ICMC 1984) matched a live monophonic performance against a stored score by dynamic programming and drove accompaniment playback from the resulting tempo estimate, demonstrated live with trumpet [@dannenberg1984online]. Vercoe's Synthetic Performer, demonstrated at IRCAM the same year, reportedly tracked a flautist through pitch and fingering sensors and learned the player's tempo tendencies across rehearsals; it heads an MIT line that runs through Csound's orchestra-and-score compile model to the present [@vercoe1984syntheticperformer]. Dannenberg's Human-Computer Music Performance programme (2011–2014) generalised from soloist-plus-accompanist to the computer as a *performing partner* in popular-music ensembles: beat- and section-level synchronisation, human cueing roles and "active scores," machine-readable, structure-aware scores that can be re-sequenced live with repeats and cues, with the Accomplice keyboard-accompaniment project among its offshoots [@dannenberg2014hcmp]. Active scores are an early representation designed for both humans and machines to edit and follow, and thus a direct ancestor of an annotated, compilable score. At MIT, Egozy's group has reported real-time in-browser score following by online time warping (Caren and Egozy, JAES 2025), and ConcertCue streams time-synchronised programme notes to audience phones cued to musical events, deployed with the Boston Symphony Orchestra and other ensembles [@egozy2016harmonix; @egozy2018concertcue]. ConcertCue addresses listeners rather than generators, but its data structure, typed content anchored to score positions and delivered at performance time, is the one the studio's annotation layer needs (S2.1). The evidence level throughout S5.1 is engineering demonstration and deployment rather than controlled study, and by the taxonomy's own count it is the thinnest node in Play.

### 9.2 S5.2 Improvisation partners

The performance-driven, player-paradigm pole has an equally long history. George Lewis's Voyager, begun in 1987, listens through pitch-to-MIDI and generates multiple independent voices that may respond to, ignore or initiate against the human improviser; Lewis frames it explicitly as *not* an instrument the human controls but a partner rooted in Afrological improvisation aesthetics, the far pole of the control axis [@lewis2000toomanynotes]. Biles's GenJam (ICMC 1994) evolved populations of jazz licks over a chord progression with a human "mentor" supplying real-time good/bad judgments as the fitness function, later trading fours with the player; it is remembered for exposing the *fitness bottleneck* of human-in-the-loop evaluation and is the earliest formalisation of annotation as fitness, the primitive form of the studio's annotate-then-regenerate loop [@biles1994genjam]. Pachet's Continuator learned a variable-order Markov model of a player's MIDI phrases in real time and continued each phrase in the same style when the player stopped, with reported studies by Addessi and Pachet in which this reflexive interaction increased children's engagement; Flow Machines generalised the idea to *Markov constraints*, style plus hard constraints on meter, harmony and position, and to lead-sheet generation in FlowComposer (S3.2) [@pachet2003continuator; @papadopoulos2016flowcomposer]. IRCAM's OMax and Somax2 practise "stylistic reinjection," learning a Factor Oracle over the live stream and improvising by recombining the performer's *own* material, with Somax2 adding reactive listening to the current harmonic context; the approach needs no external corpus, an ethically clean route to style [@assayag2006omax].

Two recent systems show what neural models add. Notochord (AIMC 2022) is an autoregressive model over MIDI *sub-events* (instrument, pitch, time, velocity) trained on Lakh MIDI with under 10 ms latency; because each sub-event is sampled separately, a performer can fix or constrain any of them, yielding harmonisers, improvisers and likelihood-based interfaces from one MIT-licensed model, the clearest real-time analogue of editing a note in the score and letting the model re-condition [@shepardson2022notochord]. The jam_bot (ISMIR 2025) is a stage system built with Dream Theater keyboardist Jordan Rudess: a Music Transformer fine-tuned on Rudess's own recorded basslines, chords and melodies, prompted into distinct roles (lead, accompaniment, call-and-response), driven by a low-latency scheduler, with a preview screen of upcoming decisions, restart and kill-switch controls to preserve performer agency, and a kinetic sculpture externalising the model's state; it premiered in September 2024 after a year of weekly co-design and was evaluated by performance rather than controlled study [@blanchard2025jambot; @huang2018musictransformer]. It demonstrates personalised symbolic models fine-tuned on one artist's material with explicit role assignment, and repeats the pattern of previewing AI intent before it commits [@huang2025creativityinteraction].

### 9.3 S5.3 RL-tuned and live neural models

Two earlier neural systems set the terms. Piano Genie (IUI 2019) decoded eight buttons in real time into plausible 88-key piano music through an RNN autoencoder with a discrete bottleneck constrained so that button contour maps to pitch contour; the human supplies rhythm, phrasing and rough contour and the model compiles exact pitches, the real-time cousin of humming or scribbling a line [@donahue2019pianogenie]. Co-Explorer (TOCHI 2021) used deep reinforcement learning to explore a synthesizer's parameter space while adapting to the sound designer's like/dislike feedback, evaluated through observational studies and a workshop with professional sound designers, a concrete design for feedback-as-steering [@scurto2021coexplorer].

The ReaLchords line is the most complete recent programme. ReaLchords (ICML 2024) is an *online* chord-accompaniment model for a live melody: frame-based at sixteenth-note resolution with a 5,041-chord vocabulary, pretrained by maximum likelihood on about 38k Hooktheory melody–chord pairs and RL-fine-tuned with self-supervised reward models scoring harmonic and temporal coherence plus a KL term distilling from an offline teacher that sees the future melody, "forcing anticipation" [@wu2024realchords]. Note-in-chord ratio rose from 37.0% for the online MLE model to 54.3%; the RL model recovered from a tritone transposition at beat 17 within two to three beats where MLE never did; and ten musicians in 192 pairwise comparisons significantly preferred it [@wu2024realchords]. ReaLJam (CHI EA 2025) wrapped it in a web interface in which the agent continually predicts how the performance will unfold and shows upcoming chords in a *waterfall* descending toward the keyboard, committed chords opaque and revisable predictions translucent, with round-trip latency under 100 ms at 150 BPM [@scarlatos2025realjam]. In a study with six experienced musicians over 60-minute sessions, enjoyment averaged 4.3/5 but perceived adaptation only 2.7/5; RL-trained models beat the pretrained one on every measure; five of six wanted visible chord previews; participants valued surprise that still matched expectation and wanted genre control and structure awareness (agents ignored four-bar phrase structure); and preferences for protocol settings varied so strongly by individual that per-user customisation was concluded to be essential [@scarlatos2025realjam]. GAPT (ICLR 2026) diagnosed *reward hacking* in this recipe, coherence rewards driving collapse to repetitive, low-coverage chords, and added a co-evolving discriminator separating policy trajectories from real data; it traced a Pareto frontier of roughly 85% note-in-chord against Vendi-score diversity, and 12 expert musicians rated it highest on adaptation quality, adaptation speed and perceived control and agency, significantly beating ReaLchords on speed and agency (p < 0.05); the family is now an open PyTorch stack [@wu2026gapt]. Streaming Generation for Music Accompaniment (ISMIR 2026) carried the idea into audio: a decoder-only Transformer over DAC tokens at 50 Hz listens to an incoming stem and emits an accompanying stem across 18 General MIDI instrument families from Slakh2100, formalising two design knobs, *future visibility* (a latency offset from −4 to +4 s) and output chunk size; on 1,024 clips with COCOLA coherence, Beat-F1 and FAD plus blind listening, larger future visibility improved coherence at the cost of required inference speed, and naive maximum-likelihood streaming training proved insufficient without future context [@wu2026streaming; @manilow2019slakh].

The "live music model" line addresses audio at the ensemble level. Magenta RealTime (June 2025) generates a continuous 48 kHz stereo stream in 2-s chunks with a 10-s context over SpectroStream tokens, steered by weighted blends of MusicCoCa text and audio embeddings, trained on roughly 190k hours of instrumental stock music and released as open weights; Lyria RealTime is the hosted sibling with tempo, density and brightness controls and control latency of at most 2 s, whose PromptDJ-MIDI demo maps physical controllers to those parameters [@lyria2025livemusic; @google2025lyriarealtime]. Magenta RealTime 2 (June 2026) cut control latency about 15-fold to roughly 200 ms with 40-ms frames and causal sliding-window attention, runs in real time on Apple Silicon (230M and 2.4B models) with a C++ engine, DAW integration and a suite of example applications built on that engine, and accepts MIDI note input, holding a chord makes the ensemble follow the harmony, with a drums toggle [@lyria2025livemusic]. Live Music Diffusion Models (May 2026) reached similar latency by a different route, repurposing the 340M Stable Audio Open Small into a block-wise streaming generator with KV-caching and "ARC-Forcing" adversarial post-training that curbs error accumulation without RL; it offers text-conditioned streaming, *sketch-based synthesis* from time-aligned top-k CQT loudness controls extracted from the musician's live audio, and stem-conditioned jamming with adjustable future visibility (−2 to +2 s), deployed through ONNX and JUCE as a "generative delay" effect on a gaming laptop at about 30 ms latency; three musicians (saxophone, guitar, cello) described it as a musical partner rather than an effect, with acknowledged EDM bias from MTG-Jamendo training [@novack2026lmdm; @novack2025arc; @evans2024stableaudioopen]. On the symbolic side, MIDInfinite ported the Anticipatory Music Transformer to the browser through WebGPU, generating 51 notes per second on an M3 MacBook Pro and staying ahead of playback 72.9% of the time (86.3% with 2 s of buffering) [@zhou2024midinfinite; @thickstun2024anticipatory]. Aria-Duet placed the 650M Aria piano model in a turn-taking duet on a Disklavier, motivated explicitly by rejecting text prompting as an asynchronous workflow [@bradshaw2025aria].

### 9.4 S5.4 Design spaces, practice and play

The field acquired a systematic map in 2026. A Design Space for Live Music Agents (CHI 2026) reviewed 184 systems (153 papers and 31 videos from 731 screened, 89.8% inter-annotator agreement) into 31 dimensions and 165 codes across four aspects: Usage Context (user and agent roles, topology), Interaction (I/O modality, planning, temporal structure, control mode and scope, system initiative, agency framing as tool, partner or hybrid), Technology (model class, learning, adaptation, latency emphasis, integration) and Ecosystem (authorship, economics, culture); it found a trend from reactive accompanists toward proactive partners, recurring control-versus-novelty and coherence-versus-diversity trade-offs, and that only about 5% of systems discuss ecosystem or policy, and it is published as a living annotated database [@kim2026designspace]. Agents in Concert (IUI 2026) is a reflective case study of deploying multiple agents in a real concert with Rudess and the MIT Chamber Chorus, documenting engineering, rehearsal and performer-agency lessons; its full text was not retrieved and its content is inferred from the related jam_bot coverage [@brade2026agentsinconcert; @blanchard2025jambot]. Both sit within MIT's Music Technology and Computation programme and Huang's research group, whose stated method is weekly co-design with musicians [@mit2026mtcshowcase; @huang2025creativityinteraction]. Behind them lies Rowe's 1993 framework, still the standard vocabulary: score-driven versus performance-driven input, transformative versus generative versus sequenced response, and the instrument paradigm (extending a performer's gesture) versus the player paradigm (an artificial musician with its own personality) [@rowe1993interactive]. The systems in S5.1 are score-driven and in the instrument paradigm; Voyager, OMax and jam_bot are performance-driven players; ReaLchords and Magenta RealTime 2 are performance-driven but transformative, following the human's line.

The practice side is anchored in games and pedagogy. Harmonix, founded in 1995 by Egozy and Rigopulos out of Machover's Media Lab group, whose director's stated goal was "making every human being into a musician," let non-musicians *perform* music through simplified controllers and a scrolling note highway in Guitar Hero (2005) and Rock Band (2007), reportedly selling more than 35 million units [@egozy2005harmonix; @machover1992hyperinstruments]. Egozy's own account describes how these games designed for musical expression within game constraints through scoring, freestyle sections and the note-highway abstraction [@egozy2016harmonix]. Kiri Miller's ethnography argued that such games constitute a form of musical performance and informal learning of rhythm, structure and listening while analysing their limits, and remains the canonical reference for game-based music learning [@miller2009schizophonic]. Harmonix's DropMix (2017) and Fuser (2020) reportedly turned *arrangement by stem selection* into a game mechanic, time-stretching and pitch-shifting stems from different songs to a common key and tempo and scoring drops on downbeats, evidence that casual users assemble coherent arrangements when the system handles alignment [@harmonix2020fuser]. The note highway is the direct visual ancestor of ReaLJam's waterfall and Piano Genie's contour mapping [@scarlatos2025realjam; @donahue2019pianogenie]. Egozy's MIT course 21M.385 Interactive Music Systems has students build real-time Python/Kivy systems, synthesis, sequencing, MIDI and gesture input, note-highway and rhythm-game interfaces, culminating in a public demo concert, a tested curriculum for teaching composers to build musical software [@egozy21m385]. Two CMU systems extend "generate to fit what is playing" beyond composition: Music-aware Virtual Assistants (UIST 2024) re-sang spoken notifications in harmony with the current music and were rated a better fit, less intrusive and more delightful than standard alerts, and RISE (ISMIR 2025) re-sequenced existing songs in real time to match workout intensity through structure analysis and recurrence-matrix cutpoints, with transitions rated 4.3/5 against 4.5/5 for unmodified music by six raters on 514 clips and preferred by 11 of 12 users [@wang2024musicaware; @wang2025rise]. RISE in particular is non-destructive, structure-aware rearrangement of audio, a Play-time analogue of editing form.

### 9.5 Implications for the studio

Play is not a separate product but the same compiled material under a latency budget, and the literature specifies that budget. ReaLJam achieved under 100 ms round trip for symbolic chords, Magenta RealTime 2 about 200 ms control latency for audio on a laptop, Live Music Diffusion Models about 30 ms as a plugin, and Streaming Accompaniment made explicit that coherence rises with future visibility while inference must run faster to pay for it [@scarlatos2025realjam; @lyria2025livemusic; @novack2026lmdm; @wu2026streaming]. The arrangement the studio compiles should therefore be *playable* in the two modes Rowe's taxonomy names: a score-driven instrument mode in which the composer plays or sings against the compiled score and the system follows, the Dannenberg–Vercoe line now available in a browser through online time warping, and a performance-driven player mode in which a ReaLchords-class agent or a Magenta RealTime 2 ensemble responds to what the composer plays on the Nord Stage [@rowe1993interactive; @dannenberg1984online; @egozy2016harmonix; @wu2024realchords]. The ReaLchords recipe, MLE pretraining on lead-sheet data, RL against coherence rewards, distillation from an offline teacher that sees the future, and GAPT's discriminator against collapse, is open in PyTorch and its chord-symbol output plugs directly into a lead-sheet workflow; jam_bot shows that the same class of model can be fine-tuned on one composer's own material and assigned roles [@wu2026gapt; @blanchard2025jambot].

The interaction lessons transfer beyond real time. Every successful system in this section externalises the agent's intent before it commits, ReaLJam's committed-versus-tentative waterfall, jam_bot's preview screen and kill-switch, Notochord's per-sub-event constraints, and the studies show why: adaptation was rated only 2.7/5 even when enjoyment was 4.3/5, five of six musicians wanted to see upcoming chords, and perceived control and agency were the measures on which GAPT most clearly won [@scarlatos2025realjam; @blanchard2025jambot; @shepardson2022notochord; @wu2026gapt]. This is the AI→human communication direction that COFI found almost absent across co-creative systems (S4.4), and the studio's compile step should adopt the same visual grammar for previewing what it is about to change [@rezwana2023cofi]. ReaLJam's finding that protocol preferences varied strongly by individual and did not track objective ratings argues for per-composer settings rather than a single tuned agent, and the Design Space's trade-offs, control versus novelty and coherence versus diversity, are the same axes the offline compiler must expose as annotations [@kim2026designspace].

The gaps are visible in the node counts. Score following is the thinnest node in Play, with no recent system that follows a *compiled, annotated* score and re-synchronises an AI ensemble to a composer's rubato, even though active scores anticipated exactly that representation [@dannenberg2014hcmp]. The RL-tuned agents produce chord symbols rather than arrangements, ignore four-bar phrase structure and lack genre control; the live audio models have no long-form structure and, in the open case, no vocals; and the evidence base is small (N = 6, 12 and 3 interactive participants across the three interactive studies, plus a ten-musician listening panel for ReaLchords) and drawn almost entirely from expert improvisers [@scarlatos2025realjam; @wu2026gapt; @novack2026lmdm; @wu2024realchords]. Only about 5% of live-agent systems discuss authorship or economics, which matters for a studio whose Play mode will generate material a composer may later keep [@kim2026designspace]. The games lineage suggests the remedy on the practice side: Harmonix, Miller and Fuser show that system-handled alignment and clear anticipatory visuals let non-experts perform and arrange with agency, which is the population the studio's Play mode should be evaluated with next [@egozy2005harmonix; @miller2009schizophonic; @harmonix2020fuser].

## 10. Evidence and evaluation (E)

Every design claim made so far in this review — that infilling preserves ownership, that annotations beat prompts, that composers want to stay in notation — is either an empirical claim or an aesthetic preference dressed as one. This section collects the evidence that lets the distinction be drawn: controlled studies of co-creative music systems (E1), field studies of practitioners (E2), deployed systems with published usage data (E3), metrics for generated music (E4), creativity-evaluation frameworks (E5), and benchmarks probing whether models know any music theory (E6); it closes with a concrete evaluation protocol. The organising finding is a mismatch. The generative-model literature is measured almost entirely with distributional audio metrics that correlate weakly with human judgement, while the interaction literature — which measures what the studio actually claims to improve — consists of a few dozen studies with median N in the low teens, one four-week field study, and one deployed system with telemetry.

### 10.1 E1 Controlled studies of co-creative music systems

The most load-bearing study here is Cococo [@louie2020cococo], because it isolates the interface from the model. Louie, Coenen, Huang, Terry and Cai built a four-voice editor over Coconet [@huang2017coconet] with four AI-steering tools — voice lanes restricting generation to chosen voices and time spans, semantic sliders (conventional↔surprising, happy↔sad), an example-based similarity slider, and multiple alternatives for audition — all implemented as soft priors on the sampling distribution rather than by retraining. A within-subjects study with twenty-one novice composers (two 15-minute pieces, one per interface), preceded by a formative study with eleven, compared Cococo against a conventional generate-and-accept infilling interface. On seven-point scales Cococo scored higher on controllability (5.9 vs. 3.5), comprehensibility (5.3 vs. 3.2), self-efficacy (5.9 vs. 3.7), sense of collaboration (5.9 vs. 4.0), creative expression (5.5 vs. 3.8) and ownership (5.2 vs. 3.8), all at p<0.01. Participants built pieces bit-by-bit, voice by voice, in generate–audition–edit loops rather than accepting whole outputs, and developed explicit strategies for *debugging* the model. The authors' conclusion — that generative capability "may need to be partitioned into smaller, semantically meaningful tools" — is the closest thing in this literature to a specification for an annotation layer.

Expressive Communication [@louie2022expressive] asked whether interface work survives model improvement. Its 2×2 design crossed model (PerformanceRNN vs. Music Transformer [@huang2018musictransformer]) with interface (a "Radio" condition curating ten randomly generated whole phrases vs. a "Steering" condition permitting chunk-by-chunk composition with semantic filters), in a task requiring composers to communicate a given card's feeling. Twenty-six composers aged 24–60 produced over 100 pieces, and twenty listeners made 1,020 head-to-head comparisons. Both factors mattered and neither dominated: the better model produced pieces that better evoked the target feeling (p<0.001) and sounded more musical (p<1e-5), while the steering interface also improved evocation and decisively raised ownership (4.8 vs. 2.42) and control (5.0 vs. 2.58) at p<1e-6. Steering mattered most when the target emotion fought the model's prior — that is, it compensated for model bias. The authors' formulation, that "more expressive models and more steerable interfaces are important and complementary," is the warrant for building interaction machinery during rapid model progress; the study's structure (composer self-report plus blind listener judgement of communicative intent) is a reusable protocol.

Amuse [@kim2025amuse] supplies the equivalent evidence for multimodal annotation. Its within-subjects study compared Amuse-plus-Aria against Aria alone [@donahue2024hookpadaria] with ten songwriters (eight hobbyist, two professional) writing an eight-bar chorus in 25 minutes, analysed by Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Inspiration support rose from 4.60 to 6.20 (p<0.01) and task alignment from 4.30 to 6.10 (p=0.016); the CSI facets of controllability (4.40→5.80), collaboration (3.90→5.80), exploration (4.40→6.10) and expressiveness (4.20→6.00) all improved at p<0.05. Two nulls matter as much: completion time was unchanged (17.4 vs. 17.6 minutes) and externally judged quality did not differ, though *self-rated* quality rose (5.30→6.40, p=0.023). A listening study with forty-five raters and 900 pairwise judgements found Amuse's progressions on a par with an LSTM harmonic prior for coherence, ahead of raw GPT-4o (p = .009) and most preferred for keyword relevance in 58% of judgements. The honest reading is that multimodal inspiration changes the *experience* of composing measurably and the *artefact* not at all.

The MMM-C study [@tchemeube2023mmmc] quantifies the cost of the opposite design choice. Integrating the Multi-Track Music Machine [@ens2020mmm; @tchemeube2022calliope] into Cubase as a plugin whose only exposed parameter was temperature, Tchemeube and colleagues ran a three-part mixed-methods study with eighteen expert composers (eight hobbyist, ten professional). Usability was acceptable (SUS 71.4–75.7 across three tasks) and acceptance moderate (TAM ease 3.1–3.3/5, usefulness 3.4–3.7/5), CSI enjoyment strongest at 3.85/5. But ease of control scored 5.2/10 against a *desire for more control* of 9.5/10 — the largest measured control deficit in this literature, and a direct measurement of what an under-parameterised compile step feels like. Since the model was strong and the DAW integration real, the complaint is squarely about the control channel.

Smaller studies triangulate individual primitives. ChordRipple's chord-substitution study found adventurous recommendations significantly increased chord novelty (p=0.0039) [@huang2016chordripple]. Drawlody [@liang2024drawlody] evaluated sketch-to-melody with eighteen participants (six musically trained), reporting user-friendliness of 4.38/5 against 2.94 for a chord-based interface and 2.38 for a DAW, and interpretability of 4.50/5 — notable because that score attaches to a visualisation of *how the sketch was mapped to the melody*, i.e. to feedback about the compile step rather than output quality. RefinPaint [@ramoneda2024refinpaint] ran a listening study with fifteen annotators across fragment sizes of 50%, 30% and 10%, finding its critic-plus-inpainter loop preferred at all sizes, with four amateur composers reporting improved drafts. Composer's Assistant 2 [@malandro2024composersassistant2] found no quality difference between real music and pieces co-composed with its infilling controls. Loop Copilot [@zhang2023loopcopilot] evaluated conversational orchestration of audio backends with eight users (SUS 75.3±15.3, TAM 4.09±1.09), drawing the complaints that recur throughout: limited attribute control, unpredictable prompt responsiveness, no DAW integration. MultiVerse [@wang2026multiverse] compared creator-authored constraints against prompting with ten songwriters, who preferred explicit constraint authoring despite its cost in iteration speed. ComposerX had 32.2% of its outputs judged human-composed against 55.4% for genuine human pieces, without reporting rater counts [@deng2024composerx], and MIDI-LLM's in-the-wild deployment covered fifty-eight musicians and roughly 4,000 generations, with lead-sheet generation most accepted [@wu2025midillm].

Music Creation by Example [@frid2020example] found, across 104 video creators, that example-based input is more natural than tag or text search for people who think in references rather than theory terms. Two studies go beyond turn-taking. ReaLJam [@scarlatos2025realjam], built over the RL-tuned ReaLchords accompanist [@wu2024realchords], ran six experienced pianists through hour-long sessions across a baseline plus eight modified settings. RL-tuned agents beat the pre-trained baseline on all measures; enjoyment averaged 4.3/5 but perceived adaptation only 2.7/5; five of six wanted the agent's upcoming chords displayed before commitment; and — the finding most relevant to shipping defaults — preferences across the other settings varied so widely between individuals that no configuration won. AI as Social Glue [@suh2021socialglue] studied thirty participants in fifteen stratified pairs co-composing with and without Cococo's model, and found it acted as a *role transformer*: pairs drifted from co-composing to co-*producing*, evaluating output rather than authoring material.

The only longitudinal study in this cluster is Rhapsody Refiner [@krol2025ownership], a tool deliberately unable to compose from scratch: it masks notes in the musician's own MIDI and re-predicts them with MusicBERT [@zeng2021musicbert] under user-set parameters. Eight practising musicians used it for four weeks with journals, interaction logs and interviews, analysed thematically. Participants valued it for "moments, not whole ideas," rejected automatic completion, and reported that its dependence on their own input produced ownership of both process and artefact; imperfection prompted problem-solving rather than frustration. This is the strongest available evidence for the studio's central bet, and a demonstration of the study design the field lacks.

Against all of this sits the CMU experiment by Oros, Telang and Randall [@oros2026cmu], the field's sharpest cautionary result and its weakest-documented one. In a randomised design, 140 musically trained participants each composed a 15-second melody on a small keyboard; a randomly assigned group could use Udio [@udio2025] to generate audio "for inspiration," and separate listeners rated all melodies on creativity, enjoyment and musicality. As reported, AI-assisted melodies were slower, used fewer notes and were judged less creative, and human-only melodies outperformed the AI inspirations themselves. These claims need care: as of September 2026 the work exists only as a conference poster (November 2025) and a university news story (January 2026), with no public paper, no effect sizes, no rater counts, and no disambiguation of "slower" (tempo or composition time); the related SSRN working paper "Help That Hurts" [@oros2026helpthathurts] was verified only through its Crossref record. Taken as indicative, the study says that bolting a prompt-to-audio generator beside a composition task can degrade trained composers' output. It says nothing about an integrated symbolic co-creator, which is why the studio should be evaluated with the same randomised, blind-rater design rather than cite the result rhetorically.

### 10.2 E2 Field studies and practitioner perspectives

The requirements literature here is qualitative and remarkably consistent. Its founding document is the AI Song Contest study [@huang2020aisongcontest], a thematic analysis of the thirteen teams (61 people, 2–15 per team, median 4) that entered the 2020 contest. Teams used a median of four model types and "rarely attempted end-to-end generation"; instead they *decomposed* songwriting into melody, harmony, bass, drums, lyrics and structure, generated massively (one team 450 melodies, another 10,000 lyric lines) and curated post hoc because the models were not steerable, authoring structure by hand because small models lacked holistic context. The call for interfaces that are "decomposable, steerable, interpretable, and adaptive" is a requirements list written by practitioners before the tools existed. HAISP [@morris2024haisp] turned that into a resource, coding the *process* descriptions contest teams submitted — which tools for which task, what control creators retained — from 34 submissions in 2023 and, in 2025, the 2024 cohort, whose comparison surfaced differences in creative agency between general-purpose and fine-tuned custom tools.

I Keep Counting [@micchi2021ikeepcounting] documents the first-person counterpart, logging more than twenty-five human interventions in one contest entry and arguing that deliberate selection and modification of AI material constitutes authorship. The deepest professional study remains Sony CSL's [@deruty2022sonycsl], a reflection on 6–18-month collaborations with six professional acts using in-house prototypes. Its findings read as design constraints: practitioners want "pull" (explicit queries) not "push" (unsolicited suggestions); they want the system *primed on their own audio*; they generate and curate, navigate latent spaces, and exploit artefacts as aesthetic material. The frictions named are leaving the DAW, the absence of recognisable controls, and the lack of visualisation. The paper also mounts the strongest challenge to a symbolic-first studio, arguing that in-studio popular-music practice is audio-first and that audio tools fit it better than MIDI tools — a claim to answer with rendering and stems rather than dismiss (see S3.8). Newman, Morris and Lee [@newman2023perceptions] interviewed six professional creators for 60–90 minutes each with two-coder analysis; their design implications nearly restate the studio's stance: confine AI to ideation rather than intentional decisions; provide interoperability through MIDI, WAV and *MusicXML* export or in-software integration; separate "influences" (ideas) from "mechanisms" (housekeeping); and prefer open-source, explainable models to black boxes.

Complaints about text-to-audio tools are now quantified. Ronchini and colleagues [@ronchini2025ttmuserstudy] gave seventeen producers from seven countries an hour with a custom interface pairing MusicGen [@copet2023musicgen] with HT-Demucs stem separation. Text-to-music was seen as an ideation aid, not production-ready: 94% would use it in ideation, while the barriers were intent–output misalignment, failure to align to a project's tempo, key and beat grid, and limited editability, plus ethical concerns about copyright, compensation and homogenisation from Western-centric training data. Fu and colleagues [@fu2025novice] followed three teams of novice producers through a ten-week course (nine of twenty interviewed) and identified an emergent stage they call "collaging and refinement" — the labour of integrating heterogeneous AI outputs into a coherent track, precisely the assembly step this review calls *compile*, and one no tool supports. Ford and colleagues [@ford2024reflection] had eight composer-researchers each compose with a different AI tool while pausing hourly to reflect on screenshots, and found reflection clustered around *curation*: decisions concentrate where alternatives are compared, which argues for surfacing history and alternatives, not only generation (see S4.5). Cavez and colleagues' interviews with nine professional composers [@cavez2024challenges] analysed notation software through the Cognitive Dimensions of Notations, documenting premature commitment, viscosity and continued reliance on paper — justification for a loosely structured annotation layer over a strict symbolic model, and the motivation for their EuterPen prototype [@cavez2025euterpen] (cross-ref S1.3).

Sturm, Ben-Tal and colleagues' folk-rnn case study [@sturm2019machinefolk] tracked an ABC-notation model through years of use by traditional and contemporary musicians, in concerts, an album and a musical, concluding that generated tunes are *raw material* requiring editing, arrangement and performance, and that the relevant evaluation is usefulness to practice rather than likelihood; its companion paper [@sturm2019openquestions] adds the engineer's duty to document limitations and evaluate for fairness and transparency, not accuracy alone. An early wave of studies on generative AI in composition *teaching* is also appearing [@oros2026helpthathurts], whose "help that hurts" finding — AI ideation reducing the quality and originality of student composition output — is plausibly the same experiment written up, since it shares the authors, the finding and the period; it should not be read as independent corroboration of the CMU poster. But these papers were verified only through Crossref records, appear in venues of mixed quality, and their designs could not be checked; treat them as signals worth replicating, not results.

### 10.3 E3 Deployed systems with usage data

Only four systems in this review report deployment usage data. Hookpad Aria [@donahue2024hookpadaria] is the most directly relevant: a copilot inside a web lead-sheet editor supporting continuation, infilling of spans in the middle of existing material, and harmony-from-melody or melody-from-harmony, built on anticipatory conditioning [@thickstun2024anticipatory]. From its March 2024 release it had generated 318,000 suggestions for 3,000 users, of which 74,000 were accepted into songs — roughly 23% acceptance over the reporting period to early 2025. This is the best public evidence that span-level suggestions inside a notation editor are wanted and used, and it makes acceptance rate a cheap, ecologically valid signal — the authors' "data flywheel for music co-creation." The Bach Doodle [@huang2019bachdoodle] answers the scale question: users entered two-bar melodies on a simplified staff and Coconet harmonised them in four voices in about two seconds in-browser, drawing over 55 million queries in three days. Its by-product is the more durable contribution — a CC BY 4.0 dataset of 21.6 million harmonisations from 8.5 million sessions, with melody, harmonisation, user rating and playback count: a unique record of how people rate AI harmonisations of their own material. FlowComposer [@papadopoulos2016flowcomposer] contributes the oldest and most actionable professional usage observation: composers using its constrained lead-sheet regeneration rarely generated more than eight bars at a time, using it for fragments inside longer human compositions. Casini and colleagues [@casini2025sunoudio] provide the negative image, clustering text embeddings of user prompts, metatags and generated lyrics from Suno and Udio over May–October 2024; the resulting map of how people try to steer a text-to-song system — including the peculiar metatag strategies users invent to obtain control the interface withholds — catalogues the pain a symbolic annotation layer is meant to remove.

### 10.4 E4 Metrics for generated music

Automatic metrics for generated music are, on current evidence, adequate for regression testing and inadequate for judging quality. The default has been Fréchet Audio Distance [@kilgour2019fad], a Fréchet distance between Gaussians fitted to VGGish embeddings of reference and generated audio — proposed for music *enhancement* and validated only against ratings of enhancement artefacts, then adopted wholesale for generative evaluation. Gui and colleagues [@gui2024fad] documented its failure modes — sample-size bias, VGGish as a poor embedding, weak reference sets — and prescribed a better recipe: extrapolate to infinite sample size, use CLAP-LAION-music embeddings and music-specific references, and compute per-song FAD for outlier detection. KAD [@chung2025kad] abandons the Gaussian assumption entirely for an MMD kernel distance that is distribution-free, unbiased, convergent at small sample sizes and GPU-scalable, reporting stronger alignment with human perception. Fréchet Music Distance [@retkowski2024fmd] is the first widely reported distributional metric over *symbolic* music in this corpus. More useful for a compiler are Yang and Lerch's interpretable symbolic features [@yang2020evaluation] — pitch-class histograms and transition matrices, note-length histograms, inter-onset intervals — compared as distributions against a reference set via KL divergence and overlap area, and released as mgeval. These are metrics a composer can read, which suits them to compile-time diagnostics rather than scoring.

For text-conditioned output the standard adherence measure is the CLAP score [@wu2023clap], a cosine similarity between prompt and audio embeddings, evaluated against caption corpora: MusicCaps' 5,500 expert-captioned ten-second clips [@agostinelli2023musiclm] and the Song Describer Dataset's 1.1k crowd captions over 706 CC-licensed tracks [@manco2023songdescriber], the latter created partly because MusicCaps is YouTube-derived and licence-constrained. MusicEval [@liu2025musiceval] supplies expert reference ratings — 2,748 clips from 31 systems over 384 prompts, rated by fourteen professionals for 13,740 ratings on overall impression and text alignment — plus a CLAP-based MOS-predictor baseline, and became the basis of AudioMOS Challenge 2025 Track 1 [@huang2025audiomos]. That challenge (24 teams) and the ICASSP 2026 Automatic Song Aesthetics Evaluation challenge established MOS prediction as a research line, including Audiobox Aesthetics' four axes (production quality, complexity, enjoyment, usefulness). Such predictors are pre-filters that rank candidate renders before the composer hears them — useful inside a compile loop, worthless as a claim about the work.

The strongest results here measure the metrics themselves. Music Arena [@kim2025musicarena] runs live pairwise human-preference evaluation of text-to-music models with LLM-based routing across heterogeneous model signatures, collects listening behaviour and free-text feedback, and releases rolling open data — infrastructure the studio could reuse. MAD [@huang2025aligning] showed FAD to be inconsistent and weakly correlated with human judgement, proposed MAUVE Audio Divergence over self-supervised embeddings, and released the MusicPrefs dataset; MAD reached an average rank correlation of 0.84 against FAD's 0.49 on synthetic tests, and 0.62 against FAD's 0.14 with MusicPrefs. Grötschla and colleagues [@grotschla2025benchmarking] gathered 15,000 pairwise comparisons from 2,500 raters over 6,000 songs from twelve state-of-the-art generators, producing the first large human-preference ranking and documenting how weakly FAD, KAD, CLAP and related metrics track it; Zhang and colleagues [@zhang2025aesthetics] independently found aesthetic and distributional metrics inconsistent with human preference across five leading systems. The survey by Kader and Karmaker [@kader2025survey] names poor metric–perception correlation, cross-cultural bias and absent standardisation as the open problems. The conclusion the studio should adopt explicitly: distributional audio metrics are sanity checks — did this build regress? — and never evidence that a co-creative tool works.

Three further instruments serve purposes a co-creative studio needs. MiRA [@batlleroca2024mira] combines audio-similarity metrics (CLAP, Discogs-EffNet, cover-identification embeddings) to detect replication of training data in generated audio above roughly 10% — the open baseline for auditing any renderer the studio ships, alongside work on whether watermarks in training data survive into outputs [@epple2024watermarking]. Echoes of Humanity [@figueiredo2025echoes] contributes a template for listener discrimination: a blind, Turing-like pairwise test with randomised crossover over real-world Suno outputs and human songs, controlling pairwise similarity. Its causal finding — that discrimination *rises* when the paired items are similar, and that the cues cited are mostly vocal and technical — means any "can listeners tell compiled from hand-arranged" test must control pair matching or it measures nothing. At population scale, the Deezer/Ipsos survey [@deezer2025ipsos] of 9,000 adults across eight countries found 97% failed to distinguish fully AI-generated tracks blind, while 80% wanted labelling — undercutting "listeners cannot tell" as a design goal and supporting provenance metadata as a feature (see C3).

### 10.5 E5 Creativity and co-creativity evaluation frameworks

The instruments for measuring creative *support* predate music AI and are better developed than the metrics above. The Creativity Support Index [@cherry2014csi] is the workhorse: a validated survey scoring a tool on six factors — Collaboration, Enjoyment, Exploration, Expressiveness, Immersion, Results Worth Effort — from paired agreement items, with paired-factor comparisons weighting each by task importance to yield a 0–100 score. It is used, whole or by facet, across this literature [@louie2020cococo; @tchemeube2023mmmc; @kim2025amuse], making per-facet reporting the pragmatic choice. Karimi and colleagues [@karimi2018evaluating] supply what CSI alone misses: four questions — *who* evaluates, *what* is evaluated (product, process, interaction, user experience), *when* and *how* — organising a survey whose central finding is a warning that co-creative systems overwhelmingly evaluate user experience and under-measure the creativity of the collaboration or of the artefact. For the studio that means measuring how annotations change compiled outputs, not only how composers feel about annotating.

The computational-creativity tradition contributes complementary standards. Jordanous's SPECS [@jordanous2012standardised] prescribes defining creativity for the domain from fourteen empirically derived components — originality, value, intention and emotional involvement, domain competence, variety — before testing against it. Ritchie's eighteen criteria [@ritchie2007criteria] formalise the relation between an "inspiring set" and an output set using ratings of typicality and quality — the structure of a studio compile, where the composer's material is the inspiring set and the question is whether outputs are typical of it and good without being copies. Colton's creative tripod [@colton2008tripod] holds that a system is perceived as creative when it exhibits skill, appreciation and imagination; *appreciation* — evaluating one's own output against the composer's stated constraints — is the missing leg in nearly every generator surveyed here and a design goal for the compiler's critic. Boden's distinctions [@boden2004creativemind] fix the vocabulary: psychological versus historical creativity, and combinational, exploratory and transformational creativity. In those terms the studio's claim is modest and testable — the compiler performs *exploratory* generation inside a space the composer defines and transforms. Kantosalo and Toivonen's modes [@kantosalo2016modes] give the interaction lens: the loop is *alternating* at the artefact level but *task-divided* at the role level, and the two plausibly require different measures. Finally, COFI's survey of ninety-two co-creative systems [@rezwana2023cofi] found almost no AI→human communication — both a gap and a measurement problem, since no instrument evaluates the quality of such an explanation.

### 10.6 E6 Music-theory competence benchmarks

If an LLM is to sit at the front of the compiler, interpreting textual annotations and dispatching to symbolic operators, its musical competence is a load-bearing engineering parameter. The benchmarks are discouraging. MusicTheoryBench, released with ChatMusician [@yuan2024chatmusician] — a LLaMA-2-7B continually pretrained on MusicPile's roughly 4B tokens of ABC notation and music text — tests college-level theory knowledge and reasoning by multiple choice; ChatMusician beats LLaMA-2 and GPT-3.5 zero-shot, while GPT-4's accuracy on the reasoning split is reported near random (a figure recalled rather than re-verified). ZIQI-Eval [@li2024ziqieval] scales the knowledge question to over 14,000 questions in ten categories and fifty-six sub-categories, reporting all sixteen LLMs tested as poor. ABC-Eval [@zhao2025abceval] localises the failure: across 1,086 items in ten sub-tasks over ABC scores, seven models including GPT-5, Gemini and DeepSeek exceed 90% on syntax while scoring near random on sequence-level tasks such as emotion — they parse the notation and cannot hear it. WildScore [@mundada2025wildscore] extends the question to multimodal reasoning over real score images, finding clear gaps.

The audio side is no better grounded. MuChoMusic [@weck2024muchomusic] built 1,187 human-validated multiple-choice questions over 644 tracks from MusicCaps and the Song Describer Dataset, covering theory and instrumentation knowledge as well as cultural and functional reasoning, and found five open audio-language models over-relying on the language modality — answering plausibly without really listening. That matters for any pipeline where an audio-LLM interprets a hummed phrase or a pasted reference recording as an annotation. SynTheory [@wei2024musictheory] asks whether generative backbones encode theory at all, using a synthetic MIDI-and-audio dataset isolating tempo, time signature, notes, intervals, scales, chords and progressions and probing Jukebox and MusicGen layer by layer: the concepts are linearly detectable, with detectability varying by model size and layer — a usable diagnostic before trusting a backbone with an annotation vocabulary. Controlled comparisons of adaptation strategies show that such choices materially change outcomes and that standard metrics behave idiosyncratically for music [@kumar2026howfar], and surveys of music foundation models [@ma2024foundation] frame the conclusion architecturally: LLMs are best used as orchestrators around specialised symbolic models and deterministic analysers, with their theory claims verified rather than trusted.

### 10.7 An evaluation protocol for the studio

The literature above supports a specific, affordable instrument set; what matters is stating what each part measures and what it cannot see. The core comparative instrument should be the Creativity Support Index reported per facet rather than as a single number [@cherry2014csi], since facet-level reporting is what makes results commensurable with Cococo, MMM-C and Amuse [@louie2020cococo; @tchemeube2023mmmc; @kim2025amuse]. Alongside it belong the four constructs this literature repeatedly shows moving when control granularity changes — ownership, controllability, comprehensibility and self-efficacy — using the seven-point item wording from Cococo and Expressive Communication so effects can be compared against published baselines of 5.9 versus 3.5 for controllability and 5.2 versus 3.8 for ownership [@louie2020cococo; @louie2022expressive]. This battery measures whether an annotation layer changes experienced agency. It cannot see whether the music got better: Amuse raised self-rated quality while externally judged quality did not move [@kim2025amuse]. Self-report alone will flatter any well-designed interface.

The artefact question needs blind paired listener comparisons on a *communicative* task, following Expressive Communication: the composer is given a target to convey, listeners judge in forced-choice pairs whether the intent came across and how musical the result is, and the analysis crosses interface with model conditions so the two are not confounded [@louie2022expressive]. Two refinements are mandatory: pairs must be similarity-matched, since Echoes of Humanity showed causally that discrimination rises when items resemble each other [@figueiredo2025echoes]; and rater pools must be large, with Grötschla and colleagues' 15,000 comparisons from 2,500 raters as the scale to aspire to and Music Arena as reusable infrastructure [@grotschla2025benchmarking; @kim2025musicarena]. This measures whether compiled output communicates and pleases. It does not measure whether the composer would put their name on it, and it under-weights structure and long-range form, since paired tests use short excerpts — the weakness structural analyses identify in deep generators generally [@dai2022missing].

Deployment telemetry is the cheapest and most ecologically valid instrument, and Hookpad Aria establishes the metric: suggestions offered, suggestions accepted, the ratio [@donahue2024hookpadaria]. For an annotation-driven compiler the analogue is richer — acceptance rate per annotation type, edit distance between compiled output and what the composer kept, recompile cycles before acceptance, and the span length of accepted material, read against FlowComposer's observation that composers rarely want more than eight bars at once [@papadopoulos2016flowcomposer]. Such telemetry captures revealed preference continuously and at scale, and can seed a public dataset as the Bach Doodle's did [@huang2019bachdoodle]. Its blind spot is severe: acceptance measures local attractiveness, not whether the finished piece is good or whether the composer's voice survived, and a system that maximises acceptance can drift into the curation trap Social Glue documented [@suh2021socialglue].

That blind spot is what a longitudinal in-practice study is for, and Rhapsody Refiner's four-week ecological design — a small cohort of practising musicians, journals, interaction logs, interviews and thematic analysis [@krol2025ownership] — is the model, with Sony CSL's 6–18-month embedded collaborations as the more ambitious form [@deruty2022sonycsl]. Only this design can observe whether composers use the compiler for "moments, not whole ideas," whether ownership persists past novelty, and whether the tool changes what they write; it is also the only way to test the failure the CMU experiment gestures at under conditions where the assistance is integrated rather than bolted on [@oros2026cmu]. Four supporting instruments complete the set cheaply: interpretable symbolic diagnostics as compiler regression tests [@yang2020evaluation], with Fréchet Music Distance for distributional drift [@retkowski2024fmd]; KAD or MAD rather than raw FAD for any audio renderer, strictly as sanity checks [@chung2025kad; @huang2025aligning; @gui2024fad]; MiRA-style replication audits plus provenance logging of every human intervention as authorship evidence [@batlleroca2024mira; @micchi2021ikeepcounting]; and SynTheory-style probing with MusicTheoryBench, ABC-Eval and MuChoMusic as acceptance tests on any model considered for the compiler's front end [@wei2024musictheory; @yuan2024chatmusician; @zhao2025abceval; @weck2024muchomusic]. The plan should be written against Karimi's four questions, so the *interaction* and the *process* are evaluated and not only the experience [@karimi2018evaluating], and against SPECS' requirement that "creative support" be defined for this domain before it is measured [@jordanous2012standardised].

### 10.8 Implications for the studio

Read together, this evidence licenses three of the studio's core commitments and refuses a fourth. It licenses region-scoped, constraint-driven regeneration over whole-output generation: Cococo's ownership and control gains, Expressive Communication's finding that steering compensates for model bias, MMM-C's measured control deficit under a one-slider design, and Rhapsody Refiner's finding that transforming authored material sustains ownership all point the same way [@louie2020cococo; @louie2022expressive; @tchemeube2023mmmc; @krol2025ownership]. It licenses multimodal annotation as a steering channel, through Amuse's within-subjects gains, Drawlody's usability margin over chord and DAW baselines, and example-based input's advantage over text search [@kim2025amuse; @liang2024drawlody; @frid2020example]. And it licenses notation-surface integration, through Aria's 74,000 accepted suggestions, FlowComposer's eight-bar usage pattern, and professional composers asking for MusicXML interoperability and control preservation [@donahue2024hookpadaria; @papadopoulos2016flowcomposer; @huang2019bachdoodle; @newman2023perceptions]. What it does not license is any claim that the music will be better: Amuse found no difference in externally judged quality, Composer's Assistant 2 found parity with real music rather than superiority, and the CMU experiment leaves live the hypothesis that assistance can subtract [@kim2025amuse; @malandro2024composersassistant2; @oros2026cmu]. The studio's defensible claim is about agency, control and the shape of the work, and should be evaluated as such.

The gaps are as instructive as the findings. Sample sizes are small and skew novice: the median controlled study here has under twenty participants, and the professional studies are mostly interview-based [@newman2023perceptions; @deruty2022sonycsl; @cavez2024challenges]. There is one four-week field study of a music co-creation tool [@krol2025ownership] and one deployed system with public acceptance telemetry [@donahue2024hookpadaria]; nothing in between, and nothing longitudinal on a notation-native compiler. No study evaluates an *annotation* as the unit of analysis — no published work reports whether a composer's typed, drawn or hummed instruction was honoured, the studio's central measurable claim, and the instrument for it does not exist. Nor is there any established way to evaluate the quality of an AI's explanation of what it did, which COFI shows is unmeasured because it is unbuilt [@rezwana2023cofi].

Practically, the studio should instrument itself from the first release rather than plan a study later. Acceptance telemetry per annotation type, recompile counts and kept-span lengths cost little to log and answer questions no questionnaire reaches; the CSI-plus-agency battery and a similarity-matched paired listening protocol belong in standing infrastructure, run at every major interaction change; and one longitudinal cohort, on the Rhapsody Refiner model, should run continuously. Two of the gaps above are themselves candidate contributions: an *annotation-fidelity* measure — did the compiler honour this mark, and can the composer tell — and an evaluation of AI→human explanation quality on notation, for which RefinPaint's "where to modify" critic and ReaLJam's commitment display are the only prior art [@ramoneda2024refinpaint; @scarlatos2025realjam]. Either would give the studio empirical claims no existing system can make.

## 11. Substrate: what an open-source studio would be built on (B)

The preceding parts of the stage view describe what a composer-centred studio should *do*; this section surveys what it would be *made of*: notation engines and editors (B1), symbolic toolkits (B2), the runtime carrying MIDI, audio and plugin integration (B3), the corpora any learned component must be trained on — whose licences, not sizes, govern what an open project may ship (B4) — and the pedagogy of music-as-computation (B5). Versions and dates are reported where the underlying notes verified them and hedged where they did not.

### 11.1 B1 Notation engines and editors

**Browser engraving.** Verovio is the strongest open route to an engraving-quality, *annotatable* score in a browser: a C++20 library (dual LGPL-3.0/GPL-3.0, v6.2.1 May 2026) compiled to WebAssembly, reading MEI natively plus MusicXML, Humdrum and ABC and emitting SVG, MIDI and a *timemap* for score–audio synchronisation [@pugin2014verovio]. Two properties matter for annotation: every rendered glyph carries an element id mapping back to the source encoding, making click-to-annotate and scribble overlays anchored to *symbolic* elements feasible rather than merely graphical; and MEI encodes editorial layers, so the difference between what the human wrote and what a machine proposed can live in the score file [@pugin2014verovio]. mei-friend demonstrates the pattern: MEI code beside a live Verovio rendering, GitHub-backed commits, and W3C Web Annotations on element ids [@goebl2023meifriend]. Interchange must nonetheless be MusicXML 4.0, implemented by over 250 applications; its JSON successor MNX remained unfinished through August 2026, and neither has an annotation model beyond directive text (S2.1) [@w3c2021musicxml40].

**The alternatives trade annotation depth for simplicity.** OpenSheetMusicDisplay (BSD-3, v2.0.0 June 2026) lays out MusicXML automatically but is explicitly a renderer, not an editor [@osmd2026], sitting on VexFlow (MIT), a low-level engraver still the right building block for chord grids and sketch overlays [@vexflow2024]. alphaTab (MPL-2.0) is one of the few open engines shipping notation and synchronised SoundFont playback together [@alphatab2026], and abcjs re-renders ABC to SVG on every keystroke with playback — the cheapest text-to-notation round trip, and important because ABC is what specialised music LLMs emit most fluently (S3.6) [@abcjs2026; @walshaw2011abc]. LilyPond is the batch, text-compiled engraving benchmark and literal ancestor of the compile metaphor [@lilypond2026; @nienhuys2003lilypond], while Humdrum contributes an encoding idea rather than an engine: parallel spines put a `**harm` column beside the notes in one plain-text file [@huron1995humdrum].

**MuseScore is the editor to integrate with, not to compete against.** MuseScore Studio 4.x is a GPL-3 C++/Qt 6.9 editor with SMuFL fonts, VST3 hosting, MusicXML and MIDI interchange, MuseSounds libraries behind the proprietary Muse Hub and a QML plugin API extended in 4.6 [@musescore2026studio; @musegroup2025musescore46]. Two facts shape integration: the "extensions framework" meant to replace that read-mostly API was deferred, leaving external scripted manipulation a pain point; and 4.7 (2026) shipped a beta MP3-to-MSCZ converter, Muse Group's only shipping AI feature, its announced Cantai choir integration delayed [@musescore2026studio; @musegroup2025musescore46]. MuseScore Studio 5, begun December 2025, targets DAW-like automation, a piano-roll, audio staves and MIDI mapping: the notation editor is converging on the DAW from the opposite direction to Suno Studio [@musescore2026studio; @suno2026]. The most-used free notation editor is thus open source, speaks the interchange formats, feeds licence-clean corpora and is entering the studio's territory with no generative capability — an installed base for a studio that treats MSCZ and MusicXML as first-class.

**The commercial landscape and the web platforms.** MakeMusic ended development and sales of Finale on 26 August 2024 and steered users to Dorico Pro at a $149 crossgrade [@makemusic2024finale]; Dorico 6 and 6.2 added proofreading and engraving refinements but no machine learning [@steinberg2025dorico6], and Sibelius through February 2026 added themes, engraving rules and scripting, again with none [@avid2026sibelius]. On the web, Flat offers a collaborative editor with REST and embed APIs and per-edit version history, Noteflight a comparable but less open one [@flat2026; @flat2026history]; Soundslice aligns notation to reference recordings through explicit *syncpoints* behind a Data API, the machinery an "insert example audio" annotation needs [@soundslice2026]; and Hookpad's lead-sheet editor, with colour-coded Roman numerals and theory hints, hosts the Aria co-writer (C1, E3) [@donahue2024hookpadaria].

### 11.2 B2 Symbolic toolkits

**music21 is the reference analysis layer.** Cuthbert and Ariza's toolkit parses MusicXML, MIDI, ABC, Humdrum `**kern` and MEI into a hierarchical `Stream` model, writes MusicXML, MIDI and LilyPond, supplies key finding, `RomanNumeral` analysis, chord identification and voice-leading, and bundles a corpus including all 371 Riemenschneider Bach chorales, Palestrina and the Viennese quartets; v10.5.0 (June 2026) is BSD-3-Clause on Python 3.11 or later [@cuthbert2010music21]. Two limits bound its role: pure-Python parsing is too slow for corpus-scale preprocessing, and MusicXML round-tripping preserves pitch, rhythm and spanners but not layout, with MEI export absent [@cuthbert2010music21; @liao2024symusic]. It is an analysis backend and vocabulary source, not a real-time engine or a lossless notation store.

**The processing stack has consolidated.** symusic is an MIT C++20 core with nanobind bindings that loads and saves MIDI and ABC and applies vectorised transforms, reporting parsing hundreds of times faster than mido, pretty_midi and miditoolkit, and now serving as MidiTok's I/O backend [@liao2024symusic]. MidiTok (MIT) implements ten tokenisations — REMI, REMI+, MIDI-Like, TSD, Structured, CPWord, Octuple, MuMIDI, MMM and PerTok — with BPE and Unigram training on the Hugging Face backend; its MMM-style per-track-bar scheme maps onto region-scoped editing, making tokeniser choice an interface decision as much as a modelling one (S3.1, S3.2) [@fradet2021miditok; @ens2020mmm]. partitura (Apache-2.0, v1.9.0 May 2026) covers the axis the others do not, parsing MusicXML, MEI, `**kern` and MIDI into a `Score` with note arrays and computing score-to-performance alignment — the primitive connecting a passage played on a keyboard to the notation it annotates [@cancinochacon2022partitura; @karystinaios2023chordgnn]. MusPy, unmaintained since 2022, is now a design reference for dataset management, representations and objective metrics [@dong2020muspy].

**Legacy interoperability and adjacent toolkits.** The older stack — mido, pretty_midi, miditoolkit and note-seq with its `NoteSequence` protobuf, a proven design for an internal representation — cannot be discarded because public checkpoints assume it; these details are recorded as partially verified [@raffel2014prettymidi; @huang2020popmusictransformer; @hsiao2021compoundword]. Magenta.js showed small symbolic models run client-side, shipping TensorFlow.js ports of MusicVAE, Coconet and Piano Genie that powered the Bach Doodle and Cococo, but it is effectively frozen, so new browser deployments should plan on ONNX Runtime Web or WebGPU [@roberts2018magentajs; @huang2019bachdoodle; @louie2020cococo; @lyria2025livemusic]. Muskits-ESPnet is the open path from a symbolic line plus lyrics to sung audio [@wu2024muskitsespnet].

### 11.3 B3 Runtime and integration

**Web MIDI is the binding platform constraint.** In 2026 the Web MIDI API works in Chrome 43+, Edge 79+, Opera and Firefox 108+ behind a site-permission add-on, with global coverage around 81 per cent, but Safari on desktop and iOS supports it not at all and has published no roadmap, Apple citing fingerprinting [@w3c2026webmidi]. For a studio whose compose path includes playing material in from a keyboard this is decisive: a purely web deployment excludes Apple users from live input, making a desktop shell an architectural requirement. Web Audio, by contrast, is universal, AudioWorklet permits sample-accurate WebAssembly DSP off the main thread, and Tone.js (MIT) supplies the transport layer.

**Synthesis is ready in the browser and headless.** spessasynth is a pure TypeScript SF2/SF3/DLS synthesizer running in an AudioWorklet that also plays MIDI and renders offline to WAV, under Apache-2.0; FluidSynth (LGPL-2.1) remains the reference SoundFont synthesizer for offline rendering in research pipelines and SFZ supplies a redistributable sample body, these last two partial [@spessasynth2026; @liao2024symusic; @dong2020muspy]. A compiled score can thus be auditioned in the client and rendered headlessly for evaluation (E4) [@alphatab2026].

**Plugin protocols and integration precedents.** Composers work in DAWs, so a compiler that cannot appear inside one is an island. Only one protocol was verified directly: CLAP, announced June 2022 by u-he and Bitwig, an MIT-licensed C ABI with per-note modulation, supported by Bitwig, REAPER and FL Studio; VST3, Audio Units, ReaScript, Ableton Link's tempo synchronisation over a LAN and Max for Live are recorded as recalled rather than re-verified [@clap2022]. The deployment precedents matter more. Magenta Studio's five Max for Live devices operate on the selected clip under Apache-2.0, establishing "the AI operates on the clip you selected, inside your DAW" as the reference architecture [@roberts2019magentastudio]; MMM4Live carried the Metacreation lineage to the same surface but stayed a closed beta without a published study [@metacreation2023mmm4live; @ens2020mmm]; and Composer's Assistant in REAPER and MIDI-GPT in Cubase and Ableton are the fullest expressions of the pattern (S3.2) [@malandro2024composersassistant2; @pasquier2025midigpt]. AbletonMCP, an MIT-licensed Model Context Protocol server plus Remote Script, adds conversational control over tracks, clips, notes and arrangements [@ahuja2025abletonmcp]. The founder's own instrument sits at the easy end: the Nord Stage family reportedly transmits on a global MIDI channel with program change and class-compliant USB-MIDI, making it an ordinary Web MIDI device in a Chromium host — though this entry is unverified recall [@clavia2023nordstage4].

### 11.4 B4 Datasets and corpora, and what their licences permit

Generation papers routinely report dataset sizes and omit licences; for an open project the licence is the more consequential number, because it decides whether a trained artefact can be released at all.

| Licence class | Corpora (size; licence) | What a released artefact may do |
|---|---|---|
| Permissive (CC0 / CC-BY) | PDMX, >250k public-domain MusicXML (CC-BY 4.0) [@long2024pdmx]; OpenScore Lieder, 1,200+ proofread songs (CC0), and String Quartets [@gotham2022openscore]; GiantMIDI-Piano, 10,855 pieces, ~1,237 h [@kong2020giantmidi]; ATEPP, 11,674 performances, ~1,000 h [@zhang2022atepp]; Bach Doodle, 21.6M melodies [@huang2019bachdoodle]; Lakh MIDI, roughly 176,600 files (reportedly CC-BY *on the collection*) [@raffel2016lakh] | Release weights; subject to the Lakh caveat |
| Non-commercial (CC-BY-NC-SA) | MAESTRO, ~200 h / 1,276 performances (partial) [@hawthorne2019maestro]; Aria-MIDI, 1.19M files / ~100,629 h transcribed piano [@bradshaw2025ariamidi]; Hooktheory/TheoryTab via Sheet Sage, ~50 h of functional-harmony analyses aligned to audio [@donahue2022sheetsage]; DCML corpora (partial) [@hentschel2021mozart]; MidiCaps (partial) [@melechovsky2024midicaps] | Published experiments and prototypes only |
| Restricted or murky | MetaMIDI, 436,631 files (registration, no redistribution) [@ens2021metamidi]; Los Angeles MIDI, ~405k files (provenance unstated) [@lev2024lamidi]; MetaScore, 963k MuseScore-sourced scores [@xu2025metascore]; Wikifonia (site closed 2013) [@wang2020pop909]; musescore.com scrapes [@xmader2021musescore] | Nothing shippable; research-only at best |

The Lakh caveat is the most widely ignored fact in the symbolic literature: the CC-BY licence covers the *collection*, while the files are user transcriptions of copyrighted songs, so commercial use is a grey zone and quality is uneven [@raffel2016lakh]. PDMX is the clean alternative — public-domain-only MusicXML from musescore.com with ratings enabling quality filtering, released CC-BY and shown to improve multitrack generation when rating-filtered, which its authors call the largest available copyright-free symbolic dataset [@long2024pdmx]; for a notation-first studio it is doubly attractive because it is score-level, carrying dynamics, articulations and lyrics.

**Analysis corpora.** For machine-proposed harmonic annotations (S2.2), two corpora supply both training data and a format. When in Rome holds roughly 2,000 Roman-numeral analyses of about 1,500 works in RomanText (`.rntxt`, parsed by music21), the largest open training set for the task and the basis of AugmentedNet and RNBert [@gotham2019wheninrome; @cuthbert2010music21; @napoleslopez2021augmentednet; @sailor2024rnbert]. The DCML corpora instead store expert chord, phrase and cadence labels *inside* MuseScore files as staff text [@hentschel2021mozart; @neuwirth2018abc] — annotations living in the score file being exactly the pattern the studio needs.

**Captions, chords and audio.** MidiCaps pairs over 168,000 Lakh-derived files with LLM-generated captions from extracted tempo, chord, instrument and mood features [@melechovsky2024midicaps]; MusicCaps and Song Describer are the audio-side caption sets used by nearly every text-to-music model [@agostinelli2023musiclm; @manco2023songdescriber]; and MetaScore's 963,000 MuseScore-sourced scores were converted to REMI+/MMT tokens rather than generated as notation [@xu2025metascore]. Chordonomicon adds over 666,000 chord progressions with section, genre and harmonic-function labels, its licence partial [@kantarelis2024chordonomicon]. Slakh2100's 2,100 Lakh files rendered into 145 hours of stems give exact symbolic-to-stem pairs for rendering evaluation, alongside MusicNet, MedleyDB and MUSDB18, all partially verified [@manilow2019slakh]. Among the small classics POP909's melody-versus-accompaniment split is a direct analogue of compiling a piano part from a lead sheet [@wang2020pop909; @huang2017coconet], and HumTrans's 56.22 hours of humming, whose baselines reach roughly 6.8 per cent note F1, quantify why an interactive correction step is mandatory (S1.2) [@liu2023humtrans; @gupta2024dynhumtrans; @cartwright2015vocalsketch].

The shipping rule follows. Anything released with weights should be trained on the permissive tier — PDMX and OpenScore for notation, GiantMIDI-Piano and ATEPP for expressive piano, the composer's own material for personal style — with per-file licence metadata retained; the non-commercial tier is legitimate for published experiments provided the boundary is documented; the restricted tier is for reading about, not training on. Composer's Assistant, trained only on permissively licensed MIDI, shows a competitive infilling model can be built inside these constraints [@malandro2024composersassistant2].

### 11.5 B5 Education and the pedagogy of music-as-computation

**Two MIT courses bracket the design space.** Cuthbert's 21M.383 Computational Music Theory and Analysis teaches symbolic computational musicology entirely through Python and music21 notebooks, from score representation and `Stream` hierarchies through MusicXML to corpus statistics on the Bach chorales, Roman numerals and algorithmic composition; one session treats the 371 chorales as a queryable dataset — the corpus that trains Coconet — and the materials are CC-BY-NC-SA on OpenCourseWare [@cuthbert2023ocw21m383; @cuthbert2010music21; @huang2017coconet]. It is a tested pedagogy for treating notation as data and analysis as a program. Egozy's 21M.385 Interactive Music Systems is its interactional complement, with students building real-time Python and Kivy applications using FluidSynth-backed synthesis, MIDI sequencing, generative composition, gesture sensors and game-style graphics [@egozy21m385]; its notation-free architecture is the deliberate inverse of the studio's stance. Egozy's writing on the Harmonix titles analyses how they designed for *musical expression* within game constraints, and his in-browser score following is a component for aligning live playing to notation [@egozy2016harmonix; @egozy2018concertcue].

**Music-as-code platforms show the metaphor scales to novices.** TunePad has learners write Python in notebook-like cells producing beats, basslines, chords and melodies with real-time playback, with Playbooks as combined tutorial-and-code documents; its cell interface is a direct precedent for a composition-as-program editor [@horn2022tunepad]. EarSketch has students place licensed loops on tracks and impose algorithmic structure through `fitMedia` and `makeBeat`, with reported gains in intent to persist in computing especially among under-represented students, its claimed million-plus reach recorded as partial [@freeman2019earsketch]. Sonic Pi puts `live_loop` at the centre — code edited while it sounds — an *edit-while-playing* loop worth borrowing for annotate-compile cycles [@aaron2016sonicpi], and Hookpad's colour-coded Roman numerals and chord hints are a pedagogy embedded in a composition tool [@donahue2024hookpadaria].

**Practice apps and games.** The commercial tutor ecosystem — Yousician, Simply Piano, Flowkey, Melodics, Piano Marvel, SmartMusic, Chordify and Moises — rests on real-time pitch and onset detection, gamified progression and, increasingly, source separation and chord recognition that turn any recording into practice material; efficacy studies are few and the entry is partial, but these products set expectations for immediate feedback on playing and singing, and Moises shows stem separation as a route for ingesting example audio [@yousician2026; @moises2025aistudio]. Miller's ethnography of Guitar Hero and Rock Band argues that such games constitute musical performance and informal learning [@miller2009schizophonic], and Harmonix's DropMix and Fuser made *arrangement by stem selection* a game mechanic with the engine handling key and tempo matching [@harmonix2020fuser]. One caution qualifies the picture: the first empirical studies of generative AI in composition, verified only through Crossref metadata and of mixed venue quality, include an experiment on AI ideation in composition tasks — not in a classroom setting — reporting reduced originality of output [@oros2026helpthathurts], very possibly the same study as the CMU poster discussed under E1 rather than a second result [@oros2026cmu] — indicative rather than conclusive, but supporting the view that assistance at ideation is the risky configuration and assistance at realisation the safer one.

### 11.6 Implications for the studio

The build-versus-adopt line can be drawn sharply. The studio should adopt Verovio and MEI as its score surface and annotation store, MusicXML 4.0 as its interchange boundary, music21 as its analysis backend, symusic and MidiTok as its corpus and tokenisation layer, partitura for score–performance alignment, and spessasynth or FluidSynth for audition and headless rendering [@pugin2014verovio; @w3c2021musicxml40; @cuthbert2010music21; @liao2024symusic; @fradet2021miditok; @cancinochacon2022partitura; @spessasynth2026]. It should not build an engraver, a parser, a tokeniser or a synthesizer. It should build the three things the substrate lacks: a typed, time- and element-anchored annotation layer that survives round trips through MusicXML and MEI; a lossless bridge between an LLM-friendly textual representation — ABC being the practical candidate, with Humdrum's spines as the model for carrying marks alongside notes — and engraving-grade notation; and the compile orchestration routing each annotation to the right specialised model [@walshaw2011abc; @abcjs2026; @huron1995humdrum]. The integration target is MuseScore rather than a fork, with a CLAP or VST3 bridge and a desktop shell to escape the Web MIDI gap [@musescore2026studio; @clap2022; @w3c2026webmidi].

The data decision is a licensing decision and should be made once, at the start: training only on the permissive tier costs scale relative to Aria-MIDI's hundred thousand hours but buys the right to release weights [@long2024pdmx; @gotham2022openscore; @kong2020giantmidi; @zhang2022atepp; @bradshaw2025ariamidi; @malandro2024composersassistant2]. Per-file licence metadata carried through preprocessing lets any released artefact state its provenance, which C2 and C3 show is becoming a regulatory as well as an ethical requirement.

Three substrate gaps are research opportunities rather than engineering chores. No notation format has an adequate annotation model: MEI's editorial layers come closest and Humdrum spines and RomanText cover parallel data and harmony, while MusicXML and MNX cover neither [@pugin2014verovio; @gotham2019wheninrome; @huron1995humdrum; @w3c2021musicxml40]. Score corpora are large but *annotated* corpora are small — When in Rome and the DCML sub-corpora are effectively the whole supply of expert harmonic annotation — which caps what an annotate step can learn and argues for treating human corrections as training data from day one [@gotham2019wheninrome; @hentschel2021mozart]. And the education literature validates the compile metaphor while compiling loops and samples rather than *notation*; a notation-native compiler is the missing member of that family [@horn2022tunepad; @freeman2019earsketch; @aaron2016sonicpi].

## 12. Context: products, law, ethics and economics (C)

The environment around a composer-centred studio changed more between 2024 and 2026 than the research did. This section covers the product landscape (C1), bifurcated into audio text-to-song platforms pulled into label-licensed walled gardens and symbolic assistants embedded in existing tools while the notation incumbents ship nothing generative; law and policy as of September 2026 (C2), where German courts have held memorisation in model weights to be an infringing reproduction while United States doctrine still requires human authorship; the provenance and disclosure infrastructure that emerged in response (C3); and the economics and creator attitudes that define the studio's audience (C4).

### 12.1 C1 The product landscape 2024–2026

**The audio family and its drift toward editing.** Suno exemplifies the paradigm and the pressure toward editability within it: around the generator it built Suno Studio, announced 24 September 2025 as a browser "generative audio workstation" with a multitrack timeline, stem separation and per-section regeneration, with Studio 2.0 on 13 August 2026 adding MIDI import, recording and editing plus a chat-bar assistant — the MIDI living inside the workstation, not in the generator's conditioning path [@suno2026]. Effective 3 September 2026 as part of the Warner settlement, downloads were capped at seven lifetime on the free tier, twenty per month on Pro and sixty on Premier, with prior models to be retired when licensed ones launch [@suno2026; @wmg2025suno]. Udio traced the same arc earlier, then disabled downloads in October 2025 and announced a 2026 platform in which creations stay inside the walled garden, fingerprinted and filtered [@udio2025; @umg2025udio]. Google's Lyria family offers the most complete audio editing loop among large-vendor products, with the Music AI Sandbox's Create, Extend and Edit verbs, Lyria RealTime's prompt-weighted streaming, SynthID watermarking, Lyria 3 in the Gemini app from February 2026 and the acquisition of Producer.ai — formerly Riffusion — announced on 24 February 2026 and rebranded by the trade press as Google Flow Music that April [@google2025lyria2; @lyria2025livemusic; @forsgren2022riffusion]. Eleven Music launched in August 2025 on Merlin and Kobalt licences with claimed commercial clearance [@elevenlabs2025music], Stable Audio's Open model was trained only on Creative Commons audio [@evans2024stableaudioopen], and Mureka reportedly plans structure before generating, on vendor benchmarks only [@kunlun2025mureka].

**DAW-native features and symbolic assistants.** Logic Pro 11 (May 2024) shipped Session Players — Drummer joined by a Bass Player and a Keyboard Player with complexity and intensity controls — which follow the project's Chord Track and, decisively, produce MIDI regions the user can edit [@apple2024logicpro11]. This is the arranger-keyboard idea of F3 reborn inside a DAW; its parameters are a reasonable floor for the studio's compile controls, its gaps the absence of notation, multimodal annotation and arrangement-level reasoning. Ableton Live 12 took a pointedly different position, using machine learning only for retrieval while its MIDI Generators and Transformations are rule-based and fully editable — a reminder, recorded as partial, that transparency is itself a feature many producers trust [@ableton2024live12]. Moises AI Studio generates context-aware stems fitted to the user's audio while declining full songs or vocals [@moises2025aistudio], Splice Create assembles licensed human-made samples rather than generating anything [@splice2024create; @splice2025landr], and WavTool, whose GPT-4 "Conductor" edited projects from natural language, was acquired by Suno in June 2025 and became Suno Studio's basis — the text-to-song leader buying editability [@suno2025wavtool]. On the symbolic side Hookpad Aria is the closest shipping analogue of the studio's loop, serving region-scoped chord, melody or joint infills from the Anticipatory Music Transformer fine-tuned on over 50,000 public TheoryTab analyses, as ordinary editable notation [@donahue2024hookpadaria; @thickstun2024anticipatory]; Composer's Assistant 2 is the open equivalent in REAPER, infilling arbitrary track-measure selections with roughly ten controls [@malandro2024composersassistant2]; MIDI-GPT brings bar-level multi-track infilling with attribute control into Cubase and Ableton under a non-commercial licence [@pasquier2025midigpt]; and Scaler 3 with the Orb and Captain Chords plugins shows the demand for lead-sheet-to-arrangement inside DAWs, on rule-based engines with per-block re-rolls and no notation, both partial [@scaler2025v3; @hexachords2022orb]. AI singers form a separate, symbolic-first category: Synthesizer V Studio 2, ACE Studio, Vocaloid 6 and Kits.ai take note-level input plus lyrics and produce sung vocals with per-note pitch and timbre editing, Kits.ai selling licensed revenue-shared artist voices, so a compiled score's vocal line can be rendered without any text-to-song model [@dreamtonics2025synthv2]. Neutone Morpho runs neural audio models live as plugins with an SDK for training on one's own recordings, making "insert example audio" realisable as a timbre annotation on consented material [@neutone2024morpho], and AbletonMCP heads a wave of LLM agents driving DAWs from natural language [@ahuja2025abletonmcp].

**The empty quadrant.** Against all of this the notation incumbents ship nothing generative: Dorico, Sibelius and MuseScore Studio added proofreading, engraving, scripting and audio work through 2026 with no generative composition, and Finale is discontinued [@steinberg2025dorico6; @avid2026sibelius; @musegroup2025musescore46; @makemusic2024finale]. As of September 2026 a notation-native AI studio has no direct competitor; the nearest neighbours are a lead-sheet web app, two DAW plugins and one DAW feature set, none operating on engraved notation with multimodal annotations. That prompt-only control frustrates users is independently evidenced by the analysis of Suno and Udio prompts, metatags and lyrics from 2024, which documents the elaborate metatag strategies users invent to steer systems offering no structural handles [@casini2025sunoudio].

### 12.2 C2 Law and policy as of September 2026

**The label litigation and its settlements.** On 24 June 2024 Universal, Sony and Warner, coordinated by the RIAA, sued Suno in the District of Massachusetts and Uncharted Labs, Udio's operator, in the Southern District of New York for unlicensed copying of recordings for training, the complaints including side-by-side outputs replicating recordings [@umg2024vsuno]. Plaintiffs added DMCA §1201(a) stream-ripping claims in September 2025, and on 26 May 2026 Universal and Sony moved to assert 61,026 recordings after discovery indicated training on millions of theirs [@umg2024vsuno]. Universal settled with Udio on 29 October 2025, combining compensation with licences and a 2026 walled-garden platform with fingerprinting and artist opt-in [@umg2025udio]; Warner settled with Udio and announced a Stability AI partnership on 19 November 2025, then settled with Suno on 25 November 2025, committing Suno to licensed models in 2026, deprecation of existing ones, artist opt-in and download caps [@wmg2025suno]. Sony and Universal versus Suno remains live and is the bellwether for fair use in music-AI training; the template is licensing in exchange for containment [@suno2026].

**The German rulings changed the analysis.** On 11 November 2025 the Munich Regional Court I held in GEMA v. OpenAI, over nine German song lyrics, that memorisation of lyrics in model weights is a reproduction under §16 UrhG not covered by the text-and-data-mining exception in §44b UrhG and Article 4 of the DSM Directive, that outputting them infringes again, and that liability attaches to OpenAI rather than the prompting user [@lgmunich2025gemaopenai]. On 31 July 2026 the same chamber decided GEMA v. Suno — the judgment is July 2026, the oral hearing originally set for 26 January 2026 was postponed over a recusal motion and heard on 9 March 2026, which is why January dates are sometimes reported for this case — over outputs resembling well-known catalogue songs, finding the works effectively stored in Suno's models so that memorisation amounts to reproduction, that stream-ripping from YouTube circumvented technical protection measures, that §44b does not apply where works are retained in reproducible form, and that Suno rather than prompting users is liable for infringing outputs generated in Germany [@lgmunich2026gemasuno]. This first European judgment on the merits against a music generator extends the lyrics logic to music itself, with two consequences for any model distributed in the EU: memorisation audits become mandatory, for which MiRA is the open baseline, and clean training data becomes the only reliable defence [@batlleroca2024mira].

**United States copyrightability points the other way, in the studio's favour.** The Copyright Office's *Copyright and Artificial Intelligence* series — Part 1 on digital replicas (2024), Part 2 on copyrightability (29 January 2025) and Part 3 on training in pre-publication form (May 2025), the final Part 3 not confirmed published as of September 2026 and dates drawn from secondary sources — sets out the operative distinction. Part 2 holds that prompts alone confer no authorship, while human-authored expressive inputs, creative selection and arrangement, and modifications of AI output are protectable to the extent of the human contribution; Part 3 treats training as prima facie reproduction, unlikely to be fair use where outputs compete with the training works or the data was obtained unlawfully [@usco2025part2]. Thaler v. Perlmutter fixed the floor: the D.C. Circuit affirmed in March 2025 that the Copyright Act requires a human author, and certiorari was denied on 2 March 2026, leaving undecided how much human contribution suffices [@thaler2025perlmutter]. Together these lines yield the conclusion that matters most for design: human-authored, human-edited notation is protectable, prompt-only output generally is not, and a provenance log may generate evidence of authorship as a by-product of ordinary operation [@usco2025part2; @thaler2025perlmutter; @micchi2021ikeepcounting].

**Transparency and likeness regimes.** Under Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models have applied since 2 August 2025, with enforcement powers from August 2026: technical documentation, a copyright policy respecting machine-readable opt-outs under Article 4(3) of the DSM Directive, and a published "sufficiently detailed summary" of training content on the Commission's July 2025 template, from neither of the latter two of which open-source models are exempt [@eu2024aiact]. For a studio trained only on public-domain, Creative Commons and consented data that summary is trivial to produce. Tennessee's ELVIS Act, which took effect in 2024, was the first United States state law extending the right of publicity to voice including simulations, with liability both for unauthorised use and for distributing tools whose primary purpose is producing such replicas [@tennessee2024elvis; @dreamtonics2025synthv2]. Concord Music Group v. Anthropic, filed October 2023 over lyrics and recorded as partial for 2026, tests output-side infringement for language models [@concord2023anthropic], and Sturm and colleagues' 2019 framing of the open questions — authorship, training-data legitimacy, the status of style — anticipated most of this [@sturm2019openquestions].

### 12.3 C3 Ethics, provenance and disclosure

**Consent standards emerged from inside the industry.** Ed Newton-Rex resigned as VP Audio at Stability AI in November 2023 over its fair-use position on training and launched the non-profit Fairly Trained in January 2024, certifying models trained only on licensed, public-domain or consented data, early certifications including Beatoven, Soundful, Kits.ai and LANDR [@newtonrex2024fairlytrained]. The "Statement on AI training" of 22 October 2024 — one sentence declaring unlicensed use of creative works for training an unjust threat to creators' livelihoods — launched with roughly 10,500 signatories and later exceeded 50,000; both entries are partial [@newtonrex2024statement]. Spawning built the opt-out infrastructure — Have I Been Trained? over LAION-5B, the Do Not Train registry and ai.txt — while its founders produced Holly+, a model of Holly Herndon's own voice offered for others to use, as consent-based training in artistic practice [@spawning2022haveibeentrained; @herndon2021hollyplus].

**Disclosure has become platform infrastructure.** On 25 September 2025 Spotify announced an impersonation policy permitting removal of unauthorised AI voice clones, a spam filter against mass uploads and sub-30-second bait tracks — 75 million "spammy" tracks removed in the preceding year — and support for the DDEX AI-disclosure standard so distributors can credit AI's role in vocals, instrumentation or production [@spotify2025aipolicy]. Deezer's telemetry charts the scale: fully-AI tracks rose from roughly 10 per cent of daily deliveries in January 2025 to over half at peak in June 2026, around 90,000 per day; they have been labelled to listeners since June 2026 and excluded from recommendation, and while they draw only 1 to 3 per cent of streams, up to 85 per cent of those streams were identified as fraudulent in 2025 and demonetised [@deezer2026aistats]. Watermarking supplies the technical complement — Google applies SynthID to all Lyria outputs [@google2025lyria2], and research on watermarking *training* data tests whether audio watermarks survive into a generator's outputs [@epple2024watermarking] — while MiRA audits in the other direction, using audio similarity metrics to detect exact replication above roughly 10 per cent [@batlleroca2024mira]. Why this matters commercially is illustrated by United States v. Michael Smith, who pleaded guilty on 19 March 2026 to wire-fraud conspiracy of $8.09 million after streaming hundreds of thousands of AI-generated tracks through roughly 1,040 bot accounts, detected by the Mechanical Licensing Collective rather than by the platforms [@usdoj2024smith].

**What not to do, and the position that follows.** The musescore.com scrape is the negative exemplar: an unofficial dump of scores, metadata and user data harvested through the site's public API and distributed over IPFS so it could not be removed, unlicensed, largely user arrangements of copyrighted works and in breach of the platform's terms — used, without disclosure, by several symbolic-model papers [@xmader2021musescore]; PDMX demonstrates the clean alternative within the same source material [@long2024pdmx]. The studio's ethical position is therefore implementable rather than aspirational: outputs are symbolic and human-authored, so authorship rests with the composer and is protectable [@usco2025part2]; computation runs locally where possible, so the composer's material is not a training contribution by default; training data is restricted to public-domain, Creative Commons and consented sources with per-file licence metadata retained, making a Fairly Trained-style certification and an EU AI Act training summary achievable [@newtonrex2024fairlytrained; @eu2024aiact; @long2024pdmx]; vocal rendering, if offered, uses consented singing-synthesis voices [@dreamtonics2025synthv2; @tennessee2024elvis]; and provenance is emitted by design, as DDEX AI credits and as a compile history recording which material was human-authored and which annotations produced which generated regions [@spotify2025aipolicy; @micchi2021ikeepcounting]. Residual risks should be stated rather than concealed: stylistic imitation of living composers remains possible, transcribed-from-audio corpora carry greyer provenance than PDMX, and any bundled audio renderer inherits its own training-data questions [@bradshaw2025ariamidi; @evans2024stableaudioopen].

### 12.4 C4 Economics and creator attitudes

The economic studies commissioned by collecting societies frame the stakes in revenue terms. Goldmedia's *AI and Music* study for GEMA and SACEM, published in January 2024 on a survey of roughly 15,000 members, projected the generative music market at about $3 billion by 2028 and put 27 per cent of creators' revenues at risk by that date, with 71 per cent judging risks to outweigh opportunities and 95 per cent wanting disclosure of training data [@goldmedia2024gemasacem]. CISAC's study with PMP Strategy (December 2024) modelled generative output at roughly €64 billion by 2028 with creators risking a modelled 24 per cent of revenues through substitution and unlicensed training [@cisac2024pmp]. Both are partially verified. The attitudinal picture is measured more precisely: APRA AMCOS's report of 19 August 2024, surveying over 4,200 members, found 54 per cent agreeing that AI can *assist* the creative process against 82 per cent fearing for their livelihood, with 97 per cent wanting policy action and training-data disclosure, 95 per cent demanding permission before use, 23 per cent of revenue at risk by 2028 and 89 per cent of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander creators fearing cultural appropriation [@apraamcos2024ai]. Listeners cannot police the boundary themselves: the Deezer and Ipsos survey of 9,000 adults across eight countries found 97 per cent unable to distinguish fully-AI tracks in blind listening, 80 per cent wanting labelling and 65 per cent opposing training on copyrighted material [@deezer2025ipsos] — though detectability is not fixed, since in a blind crossover design over real Suno outputs listeners' accuracy rose causally when pairs were made more similar [@figueiredo2025echoes].

What professional composers ask for, when asked directly, reads as a specification of the studio. Newman, Morris and Lee's ISMIR 2023 study of six professionals across classical, jazz, film, game, interactive-media and electroacoustic practice — 60- to 90-minute semi-structured interviews, two-coder analysis — produced four design implications: preserve creative control by confining AI to ideation rather than intentional decisions; provide interoperability through MIDI, WAV and MusicXML export or integration into software already in use; separate "influences" from "mechanisms"; and prefer open-source, explainable models over black boxes [@newman2023perceptions]. The same preferences recur in the folk-rnn practitioner work and Sony CSL's eighteen-month embedded study, which add that tool-switching kills adoption and that documented human selection and modification is what practitioners regard as constituting ownership [@sturm2019machinefolk; @deruty2022sonycsl; @micchi2021ikeepcounting]. The studio's positioning is thus not a moral posture adopted at cost but the configuration these studies describe as acceptable.

### 12.5 Implications for the studio

The competitive conclusion is that the empty quadrant is real and defensible. Every 2026 product either generates opaque audio from a prompt and is now enclosed by licensing settlements, or generates editable symbolic material inside a DAW at clip or bar granularity, or engraves notation with no generative capability at all [@suno2026; @udio2025; @apple2024logicpro11; @malandro2024composersassistant2; @steinberg2025dorico6; @avid2026sibelius; @musegroup2025musescore46]; nothing compiles engraved notation from human-authored material plus multimodal annotations. Hookpad Aria validates the interaction primitive — select a region, ask, accept or edit — on a small, consented, functional-harmony dataset, showing it can be productised without a foundation model or a legally encumbered corpus [@donahue2024hookpadaria; @thickstun2024anticipatory], and Suno Studio and WavTool supply the reverse confirmation: the leaders of the rejected paradigm spent two years acquiring editability, arriving at MIDI editing inside a workstation whose generator still cannot read it [@suno2026; @suno2025wavtool].

The legal conclusion is that the studio's architecture is also its legal strategy. Because outputs are notation the composer wrote, selected, arranged and edited, they fall on the protectable side of the Copyright Office's Part 2 analysis and the Thaler rule, whereas prompt-only outputs generally do not [@usco2025part2; @thaler2025perlmutter]; and because training is confined to public-domain, Creative Commons and consented corpora, the memorisation-as-reproduction holding of the Munich rulings is not merely survivable but inapplicable, while the EU AI Act's training-content summary becomes a differentiator rather than a burden [@lgmunich2025gemaopenai; @lgmunich2026gemasuno; @eu2024aiact; @long2024pdmx]. Three features follow and should be first-class: a provenance log recording authorship and edit history at region granularity, serving at once as authorship record, DDEX AI-credit source and debugging aid; a memorisation audit in the evaluation harness for any released model; and per-file licence metadata carried through the data pipeline [@micchi2021ikeepcounting; @spotify2025aipolicy; @batlleroca2024mira].

Two gaps remain. No product in this landscape emits provenance a composer could use to demonstrate authorship, and no legal or platform regime specifies what such a record should contain, so a well-designed compile history is a contribution to practice as much as to software. And the economic literature measures what creators fear and want in aggregate while offering no longitudinal evidence on whether an assistive, symbolic, provenance-emitting tool changes professional output or income [@apraamcos2024ai; @goldmedia2024gemasacem; @cisac2024pmp] — evidence the studio is positioned to generate, which is the argument in E2 for building telemetry and longitudinal study design in from the first release [@newman2023perceptions; @deruty2022sonycsl].

## 13. Gap analysis and a research agenda

The survey's most useful output is not the inventory but the shape of what is missing. The gaps below were derived by intersecting two things: the density of the taxonomy's nodes — where the corpus is thin — with the studio's own design requirements, which are stated as positions on the ten coding dimensions. A node that is crowded but irrelevant (text-to-song, 23 primary entries) is not a gap; a node that is thin *and* load-bearing is. Each gap names the nearest prior art to build on, so that none of them starts from nothing.

### 13.1 Gaps that are candidate contributions

**G1. Annotations as a compilable instruction set on notation.** *(Node S2.4: four primary entries. Dimensions D4, D6, D7.)* This is the central gap and the one the studio is best positioned to close. The constituent parts are all present in the corpus and never assembled: typed, musical-time-anchored annotation records in MEI, Dezrann and RomanText [@pugin2014verovio; @giraud2018dezrann; @gotham2019wheninrome]; free-form ink, images and audio placed on and between staves without an engraver enforcing syntax, as in EuterPen [@cavez2025euterpen]; a reference grammar that decomposes a mixed natural-language-and-sketch request into temporal, spatial, operation and parameter components with per-modality resolvers and inspectable suggestions, as in ExpressEdit [@tilekbay2024expressedit]; selection-then-instruction as an interaction, as in DirectGPT [@masson2024directgpt]; a converged control vocabulary that supplies the annotation *types* [@vonrutte2023figaro; @guo2022musiac; @chen2024sympac]; and a demonstration that a music agent can act on musician-readable structural feedback in a text grammar, as in Libretto [@xu2026libretto]. The research questions are which annotation types composers actually reach for, how ambiguous marks should be resolved and disambiguated, and how conflicts between annotations are reported rather than silently resolved.

**G2. A lossless bridge between an LLM-friendly textual IR and engraving-grade notation.** *(Nodes S3.1, B1. Dimension D7.)* No verified system in the corpus generates MusicXML or MEI natively. Generation lives in MIDI event tokens, ABC text or piano-roll tensors, and MetaScore converts scores *out* of MuseScore format into REMI+ rather than in [@xu2025metascore]. ABC is the pragmatic candidate because the strongest symbolic music LLMs already speak it, including MuPT's synchronized multi-track SMT-ABC and NotaGen [@qu2024mupt; @wang2025notagen], but ABC cannot express what an engraver needs. Verovio's MEI-native pipeline is the natural target on the notation side [@pugin2014verovio]. Building and evaluating a round-trip that loses nothing a composer cares about is both a prerequisite for the studio and a publishable contribution.

**G3. Notation-editor-native generative assistance.** *(Node S3.2 crossed with dimension D8.)* The DAW side is well served — Composer's Assistant in REAPER, MIDI-GPT in Cubase and Ableton, Magenta Studio, MMM4Live [@malandro2024composersassistant2; @pasquier2025midigpt; @roberts2019magentastudio; @metacreation2023mmm4live]. The notation side has DeepBach's 2017 MuseScore plugin and Hookpad Aria's lead-sheet copilot and essentially nothing else [@hadjeres2017deepbach; @donahue2024hookpadaria]. Dorico, Sibelius and MuseScore Studio ship no generative features as of 2026 [@steinberg2025dorico6; @avid2026sibelius; @musegroup2025musescore46]. Practitioners consistently say tool-switching kills adoption [@deruty2022sonycsl; @newman2023perceptions], and MuseScore is GPL-3 with a DAW-converging roadmap. A notation-native studio has no direct competitor.

**G4. Multimodal editing on a score.** *(Nodes S4.1, S1.3. Dimension D4.)* Sketch-conditioned music generation exists in isolation — contour and density curves in Draw and Listen!, independent pitch and rhythm sketches in Music SketchNet, drawn curves with visual alignment explanations in Drawlody [@benetatos2022drawlisten; @chen2020sketchnet; @liang2024drawlody] — and natural-language plus sketch editing exists for video [@tilekbay2024expressedit]. Nothing combines language, sketch, hum and attached example on notation. Drawlody's usability scores against chord-grid and DAW baselines suggest the drawn contour is worth treating as a first-class annotation type.

**G5. A return channel: the compiler's build log.** *(Node S4.4: two primary entries. Dimension D6.)* COFI's coding of 92 co-creative systems found the AI-to-human direction almost entirely absent [@rezwana2023cofi]. The exceptions are instructive and few: RefinPaint's critic marks *where* a score should be modified before proposing how [@ramoneda2024refinpaint], Cococo offered explanation and debugging affordances [@louie2020cococo], ReaLJam renders committed chords opaque and tentative ones translucent so the human can see the agent's plan before it commits [@scarlatos2025realjam], and ExpressEdit's examine panel shows how each reference was interpreted [@tilekbay2024expressedit]. The compiler metaphor supplies the missing design directly: a build log that states which annotation produced which material, which constraints bound, which were relaxed and why, and where the model was least confident. Work on explaining latent spaces for the arts is the nearest theoretical anchor [@bryankinns2021xai].

**G6. Versioning with musical semantics.** *(Node S4.5: five primary entries.)* Score diffing exists as tree edit distance rendered side by side in Verovio [@foscarin2019diff], Flat.io keeps per-edit history with contributor colouring [@flat2026history], and mei-friend commits MEI to GitHub [@goebl2023meifriend]. Branch and merge with musical semantics does not exist, and variation trees over compiled outputs — the pattern Luminate demonstrates for LLM design-space exploration [@suh2024luminate], with antecedents in Side Views and Chronicle [@terry2002sideviews; @grossman2010chronicle] — are unexplored for music. There is a second motive beyond convenience: a provenance log of human authorship and modification is exactly the record that the authorship analysis in §12.2 makes valuable, a point Micchi and colleagues raised from practice [@micchi2021ikeepcounting].

**G7. Symbolically-conditioned, composer-controllable audio rendering.** *(Node S3.8. Dimension D1.)* Rendering a compiled score to audio under human control is thin and research-grade. JASCO accepts chords, melody salience and drums but ships non-commercial weights; Coco-Mulla, Music ControlNet and MuseControlLite accept chords, MIDI or time-varying curves; MIDI-DDSP renders full MIDI expressively but monophonically; Magenta RealTime 2 accepts live MIDI notes at roughly 200 ms control latency on a laptop [@tal2024jasco; @lin2023cocomulla; @wu2024musiccontrolnet; @tsai2025musecontrollite; @wu2022mididdsp; @lyria2025livemusic]. No commercial full-song system accepts MIDI or chords as a *generation* condition. Inference-time optimization is the most promising general mechanism, since it turns any differentiable musical feature into a constraint on a frozen model without retraining [@novack2024ditto].

**G8. Interactive correction for loose input.** *(Node S1.2.)* On a clean humming corpus with corrected onset labels, off-the-shelf transcribers reach 0.43–0.60 note-plus-onset F1 and a purpose-built CNN-plus-dynamic-programming model roughly 0.67 [@liu2023humtrans; @gupta2024dynhumtrans]. Humming will therefore always need disambiguation — key and scale locking, quantisation grids, smoothing, click-to-fix — rather than a claim of correctness [@doremir2014scorecloud; @vochlea2021dubler]. The transferable principle comes from an unexpected place: one targeted human annotation, clicking repeat signs, lifted audio-to-score alignment from 33% to 82% [@bukey2024justlabel]. Cheap, well-placed human correction beats full automation, and designing where to spend the composer's attention is a research question in its own right.

**G9. Editable structural skeletons.** *(Node S3.4.)* Dai and Dannenberg's analyses established that deep generative outputs differ strikingly from human popular music in repetition and hierarchical structure [@dai2022missing; @dai2024interconnections]. The answers already point at the annotation layer: MusicFrameworks makes section and phrase structure, chords and a basic melody explicit user-editable inputs, and the whole-song cascaded diffusion model exposes four editable levels from form down to accompaniment [@dai2021controllable; @wang2024wholesong]. A studio whose compile step reads a human-authored form annotation inherits both the structural fix and the control surface.

**G10. Evaluating the loop rather than the artifact.** *(Nodes E1, E4.)* Distributional audio metrics correlate weakly with human preference — Fréchet Audio Distance has been argued to be superseded, and benchmarks against 15,000 human pairwise preferences show weak metric-human agreement [@chung2025kad; @grotschla2025benchmarking; @huang2025aligning]. The instruments that matter for a co-creative tool exist — the Creativity Support Index, ownership and control items, paired listener comparisons, and Karimi and colleagues' warning that co-creative systems over-measure user experience and under-measure the creativity of the collaboration [@cherry2014csi; @karimi2018evaluating] — but longitudinal, in-practice studies remain rare [@krol2025ownership; @deruty2022sonycsl]. Suggestion-level acceptance telemetry of the kind Hookpad Aria reports is the most under-used measure available [@donahue2024hookpadaria].

**G11. Orchestration without over-trusting the orchestrator.** *(Node E6.)* Specialised symbolic music LLMs outperform general models on composition tasks [@wang2025notagen; @qu2024mupt], but every benchmark that probes musical reasoning reports weakness: ZIQI-Eval found all sixteen models tested poor, MuChoMusic showed audio-language models over-relying on text, and probing work questions whether generation models encode theory concepts at all [@li2024ziqieval; @weck2024muchomusic; @wei2024musictheory]. The architecture this implies is an LLM as orchestrator around specialised symbolic models and deterministic analysers — with the analysers, not the language model, holding authority over theory.

**G12. Licence-clean symbolic training data at scale.** *(Node B4. Dimension D10.)* The clean sets are real but small relative to what the field trains on: PDMX at more than 250,000 public-domain MusicXML scores, OpenScore's CC0 editions, GiantMIDI-Piano, ATEPP and the Bach Doodle corpus [@long2024pdmx; @gotham2022openscore; @kong2020giantmidi; @zhang2022atepp; @huang2019bachdoodle]. The large and convenient sets are not: Aria-MIDI's 1.19 million files are CC-BY-NC-SA, Hooktheory's data is non-commercial, and scraped MuseScore dumps are legally untenable [@bradshaw2025ariamidi; @xmader2021musescore]. Composer's Assistant's decision to train only on permissively licensed MIDI shows the constraint is workable [@malandro2024composersassistant2]. Assembling and documenting a shippable symbolic training corpus, with an EU AI Act-style training-content summary, is unglamorous infrastructure work with outsized value to an open project.

### 13.2 A sequenced agenda

The gaps sort naturally into three horizons.

The **near horizon** is engineering that unblocks everything else and is publishable as systems work: the notation round-trip of G2, an annotation data model that reuses MEI or Dezrann's record shape rather than inventing one (G1's substrate), integration with an existing notation engine rather than a new renderer (G3), and the licence-clean corpus of G12. None of this requires new modelling. Existing infilling models can be wrapped immediately — the Anticipatory Music Transformer's anticipation tokens map directly onto "everything the composer wrote is a hard constraint," and it runs in a browser [@thickstun2024anticipatory; @zhou2024midinfinite].

The **middle horizon** is the studio's actual research contribution: the annotation compiler of G1, evaluated against the instruments of G10, with the build log of G5 as its distinguishing feature. This is where a defensible claim lies, because it is simultaneously an interaction contribution, a systems contribution and an evaluation contribution, and because the corpus contains no competitor. The multimodal editing surface of G4 and the structural skeleton of G9 are natural components of the same system rather than separate projects.

The **far horizon** is rendering and performance: symbolically-conditioned audio under composer control (G7), and the real-time branch, where the RL-tuned accompaniment line from ReaLchords through ReaLJam to post-training fixes for reward hacking has produced open code and demonstrated interaction patterns worth building on [@wu2024realchords; @scarlatos2025realjam; @wu2026gapt].

### 13.3 Concluding argument

Three claims stand up across the whole corpus, and together they make the case for the studio.

The first is that the composer-centered loop is not a contrarian preference but the direction every serious line of evidence points. Coconet described nonlinear scribbling and revision as the thing to model in 2017 [@huang2017coconet]. The controlled studies found that steering interfaces move composer control, ownership and self-efficacy even when the model is held fixed, and that interface and model gains are complementary [@louie2020cococo; @louie2022expressive]. The field studies found practitioners decomposing songs into parts, generating into those parts and curating, and asking for systems that are decomposable, steerable and interpretable [@huang2020aisongcontest; @morris2024haisp]. The one deployment with public telemetry shows musicians accepting span-level suggestions inside a notation editor at scale [@donahue2024hookpadaria]. And the commercial systems built on the opposite premise keep adding editing affordances after the fact [@suno2026; @google2025lyria2].

The second is that the technical substrate is unusually ready. Infilling under hard constraints is mature and, in several cases, permissively licensed and small enough to run locally [@thickstun2024anticipatory; @pasquier2025midigpt; @malandro2024composersassistant2]. Notation rendering, symbolic parsing and analysis are solved well enough to adopt rather than rebuild [@pugin2014verovio; @cuthbert2010music21; @liao2024symusic; @fradet2021miditok]. Hierarchical pipelines with editable intermediate levels already exist as architectural templates [@wang2024wholesong; @dai2021controllable]. Decompiling symbolic music into an editable program has been demonstrated with execution-based rewards, which makes "music as code" an empirical result rather than a metaphor [@kim2026decomposer].

The third is that the gap between what exists and what a composer needs is concentrated almost entirely in the *annotation and feedback* layer — the two thinnest load-bearing nodes in the taxonomy, S2.4 and S4.4. That is a fortunate place for a gap to be. It is an interaction-design and systems problem more than a frontier-modelling problem, which means a small open-source project with a clear thesis can make real progress on it without competing for compute. It is also the layer that determines whether the human stays at the center, since annotations are how intent enters and the build log is how the machine accounts for what it did.

The studio's premise, restated in the vocabulary this review has assembled: a score-driven, transformative system in the instrument paradigm [@rowe1993interactive], with alternating turn-taking [@kantosalo2016modes], bidirectional communication [@rezwana2023cofi], multi-level editable intermediate representations, and the AI in the role of colleague at compile time and coach when it annotates back [@lubart2005partners] — never author.
